# Proposed lesson — Acute seizures and status epilepticus in adult critical care
**DRAFT — AI-generated; not clinically reviewed. Not approved for publication, competency assessment or bedside protocol.** 24 November 2026. Proposed expansion of the existing acute-seizure scenario SC-08; no new canonical topic ID assigned.

## Learning objectives
- Recognize a suspected seizure and a time-critical convulsive emergency.
- Explain why cessation of visible convulsions does not prove seizure control.
- Describe the nursing assessment, treatment surveillance, escalation and documentation within an authorized emergency plan.

## Recognition and first response
Convulsive status epilepticus is a medical emergency. NICE NG217 uses a duration of at least five minutes for its emergency-treatment pathway; repeated seizures without recovery are also concerning for status epilepticus. In ICU, call for help promptly when a seizure is suspected; do not wait for the five-minute threshold to summon assistance or assess airway, breathing and circulation.

Note the onset or last known normal time, pattern of movement, responsiveness and recurrence. Protect from injury, avoid restraint and objects in the mouth, and coordinate safe positioning with the team when feasible, considering airway equipment and other devices. Do not give food, drink or oral medication to a patient who is convulsing or cannot swallow safely. Assess breathing and oxygenation, use the unit's emergency airway and resuscitation pathway if indicated, and obtain glucose as soon as feasible without delaying time-critical care.

## Collaborative treatment and monitoring
Verify the emergency plan, available vascular access, allergies, and any emergency medication already given, including the time and response. Administer only authorized treatment. NICE describes initial benzodiazepine treatment followed by urgent escalation for persistent seizures; subsequent antiseizure medicines and refractory therapy are team decisions. Observe ventilation, circulation, consciousness and recurrence during and after treatment. Promptly report respiratory depression or circulatory instability. Coordinate ordered testing for possible metabolic, toxic, infectious or structural contributors with the team; do not attribute the event to epilepsy alone.

## When movements stop but consciousness does not recover
Persistent unexplained impaired consciousness needs urgent reassessment. The 2025 UK multidisciplinary intensive-care consensus supports continuous EEG in persistently depressed consciousness and refractory or super-refractory status epilepticus. Neuromuscular blockade can conceal motor signs. Processed sedation indices cannot replace diagnostic EEG. If continuous EEG is unavailable, request specialist guidance about alternative monitoring or transfer. Sedative effects and a postictal state may coexist with ongoing electrographic seizures; the clinical team determines the diagnosis. Verify the ongoing prescribed antiseizure plan, and do not independently interrupt anesthetic infusions intended for seizure control.

## Fictional bedside application
An adult ICU patient has generalized convulsions. Emergency help is summoned, the onset is documented, and treatment is given under the authorized plan. The movements cease, but the patient remains unexpectedly unresponsive. The nurse reassesses airway, ventilation, circulation, glucose and recent medication exposure, urgently reports the change and asks the team to evaluate ongoing seizure activity and arrange EEG. The patient is not assumed to be seizure-free just because movement has ceased.

## Knowledge checks
1. A convulsive seizure is still occurring and its onset was four minutes ago. Should the nurse wait one minute before calling for help? **No.** Activate assistance and immediate assessment now; the diagnostic time threshold does not justify delaying the response.
2. After emergency treatment, convulsions stop but consciousness remains unexpectedly depressed. Is a reassuring processed sedation index enough? **No.** Urgently escalate and arrange specialist EEG assessment; the index does not exclude ongoing seizures.
3. A sedated patient receives a rescue antiseizure medicine. What must be handed over? **Answer:** The exact administration time, observed response, airway and circulatory status, any recurrence, and the authorized monitoring and treatment plan.
