# Trauma shock, transfusion physiology and possible TBI — ICU nursing
> **UNPUBLISHED PROPOSAL — AI-generated, not clinically reviewed.** Does NOT supply a resuscitation target, component ratio, blood product dose or independent intervention. Excluded from learner-facing curriculum pending multidisciplinary review.

## Why this is a distinct nursing lesson
In major trauma, shock, ongoing haemorrhage and coagulopathy can evolve *while* blood products and source control are arranged. Permissive-hypotension approaches described in some haemorrhagic trauma pathways are **not universally appropriate with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI)**. An ICU nurse must recognise secondary brain injury risk while escalating bleeding and monitoring transfusion-related metabolic complications.

## Objectives
1. Respond to suspected ongoing major haemorrhage with patient-first ABC/perfusion assessment and local trauma/major-haemorrhage activation.
2. Recognise that new confusion, pupil/focal changes, seizure or reducing consciousness warrant neurocritical/trauma escalation, including when low pressure appears 'acceptable' for other injuries.
3. Track temperature, ordered **ionised calcium** and other electrolytes, acid–base/lactate, coagulation/platelet/fibrinogen results and the *patient's perfusion* during major transfusion, recognising trends rather than assigning an independent component prescription.
4. Distinguish a possible transfusion reaction, wrong patient/component, ongoing source bleeding and metabolic complication. Follow identification and reaction-response procedures.
5. Give an urgent, structured, cross-disciplinary handover and follow serial reassessment after apparent haemostasis.

## Bedside approach (not a device or drug protocol)
**Stage A — assess and mobilise help:** Monitor airway, breathing, circulation, perfusion, mental state, injury/bleeding sites and temperature. Stay with the unstable patient; activate trauma/critical-care and local major-haemorrhage support; delegate authorised line/sampling/blood bank tasks. A transient normal blood pressure, early haemoglobin, absent external bleeding or a single coagulation result **cannot eliminate concealed or ongoing bleeding**.

**Stage B — neurotrauma exception:** The European 2023 major-trauma guideline specifies a *distinct* haemodynamic approach for **severe TBI**, unlike its restricted-volume approach for bleeding **without clinical evidence of brain injury**. **This draft intentionally provides no blood pressure or fluid target.** If severe head injury is suspected, communicate the concern immediately: do **not** apply a universal permissive-hypotension rule, delay neurological reassessment or independently alter vasoactive/fluid orders.

**Stage C — transfusion and associated physiology:** Under the local emergency blood policy, verify identity, product, documentation and monitoring even when urgency is high. Observe for unexpected fever, rash, breathing changes, pain, new hypotension or other signs compatible with a reaction and activate the transfusion reaction pathway. Monitor ordered temperature, ionised calcium, acid–base status, electrolytes, perfusion and labs; hypothermia and citrate-associated hypocalcaemia may aggravate coagulopathy and circulatory dysfunction. Report abnormalities promptly; **no independent calcium administration, warming-device settings, TXA, product ratios, lab-directed reversal or plasma selection**.

**Stage D — haemostasis, ongoing shock or neuro change:** If bleeding continues, measurements worsen or consciousness changes, re-escalate now; maintain surveillance after apparent control, including respiratory function, renal output and transfusion complications. Do not assume a warm patient, normalised blood test or completed transfusion definitively establishes haemostasis.

**Stage E — communication:** In one high-clarity handover: mechanism/injuries; suspected unseen source; trend of mental state and pupil/focal signs; airway and ventilatory support; haemodynamics and perfusion; actual blood products/time and reactions; temperature and ordered ionised calcium, platelet/coagulation/fibrinogen and acid–base trends; medicines/anticoagulant exposures; imaging/procedure pending; and actions/people already contacted.

## Fictional simulation (unvalidated and unscored)
1. **Initial response.** Adult after severe blunt trauma is cool, tachycardic and confused with a concerning haemodynamic trend. **Preferred action:** ABC/perfusion and neurological reassessment, call trauma/major haemorrhage help, coordinate approved monitoring and blood-bank tasks. **Unsafe:** defer because no external haemorrhage or single haemoglobin seems reassuring.
2. **Possible TBI during resuscitation.** Pupils/mental state change as blood pressure falls. **Preferred action:** urgently inform trauma, anaesthesia and neurocritical team; highlight the possible TBI-specific haemodynamic exception and support approved reassessment/resuscitation. **Unsafe:** accept deliberately low pressure as a universal target without recognising brain injury.
3. **Multiple products administered.** Ionised calcium and temperature trend down, while tachycardia and oozing persist. **Preferred action:** report evolving shock/metabolic/coagulation concerns, use verified product records, assess for transfusion reaction and support *ordered* monitoring/correction. **Unsafe:** independently dose calcium, prescribe plasma or infer haemostasis from one improving test.

## Three reviewer questions (not a scored quiz)
1. Why is a blanket low blood pressure strategy unsafe when severe TBI is suspected? **Expected:** cerebral perfusion concerns; team-specific goals differ.
2. Why monitor ionised calcium and temperature during major transfusion? **Expected:** electrolyte/coagulation and haemodynamic complications can occur; alert the team and follow authorised orders.
3. Does a single abnormal INR/platelet count automatically identify the correct blood component? **Expected:** no; assess patient and evolving injury/coagulopathy in the treating team’s protocol.
