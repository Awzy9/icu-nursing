# Acute gastrointestinal haemorrhage — critical care nursing
> **UNPUBLISHED PROPOSAL. AI-authored; not clinically reviewed.** Not a protocol, prescribing guide, or permission to undertake advanced interventions. Keep outside the original 40 topics, case bank, practice mode and the website until a clinical and licence gate is passed.

## Scope and why this is new
An adult ICU teaching module on **active bleeding**, not merely stress-ulcer prophylaxis. Distinguish suspected non-variceal upper GI haemorrhage, possible portal-hypertensive/variceal haemorrhage, and lower GI bleeding without assigning a source on the basis of stool colour alone. The 2026 ESGE **peptic-ulcer** update must not be used as a variceal-bleeding protocol.

## Learning objectives
1. Recognise haematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, melena, haematochezia and less visible haemorrhage as reasons for clinical assessment; colour or appearance alone does not confirm origin or current severity.
2. Prioritise the patient over a laboratory result: trends in consciousness, pulse, blood pressure, perfusion, breathing, oxygenation and visible loss; absence of early hypotension does not rule out major bleeding.
3. Activate urgent clinical/major-haemorrhage support for instability, protect from aspiration while the qualified airway team assesses need, and coordinate access, monitoring and labs per local protocol.
4. Support **prescriber-authorised** blood product preparation/identity checks, medication reconciliation (anticoagulants/antiplatelets) and coordinated GI/endoscopy/anaesthesia handover.
5. Distinguish active bleeding care from **risk-stratified stress-ulcer prophylaxis (SUP)** and participate in pharmacist/prescriber review once risk factors resolve.

## Nursing recognition and immediate sequence
- **Red flags:** new haematemesis or bleeding with syncope, deteriorating consciousness, cool/poor perfusion, shock signs, hypoxaemia, ongoing blood loss, or concern for aspiration. Blood pressure, haemoglobin and early appearance may be falsely reassuring; review trajectory and concurrent observations.
- **First:** remain with an unstable patient, reassess airway/breathing/circulation and perfusion, obtain urgent medical/GI/critical-care response, communicate source uncertainty and the observed bleeding pattern. Summon help rather than wait for a repeat haemoglobin or an endoscopy slot.
- **In parallel with the team:** record blood loss where practical, continuous/serial physiological observations as indicated; arrange authorised IV access, blood samples/crossmatch and blood bank liaison. Verify identity, product and monitoring under the institutional emergency transfusion procedure. Escalate any suspected transfusion reaction.
- **Handover:** onset and amount/appearance of bleeding; mental state and perfusion trend; anticoagulant/antiplatelet exposures and last known doses; liver/cirrhosis history; line access; available laboratory *trends*; blood products and actions already taken.
- **Endoscopy nuance:** the **2026 ESGE peptic-ulcer** recommendations caution against blanket emergent/urgent endoscopy before adequate resuscitation; persistent instability after resuscitation requires urgent clinician-directed assessment. Nurses do not decide timing or delay escalation based on a rigid clock rule.
- **After haemostasis:** watch for recurrent bleeding, deterioration, and procedure/transfusion complications; nutrition and anticoagulant resumption are individualized prescriber decisions, **not automatic bedside nursing actions**.
- **Variceal pathway is different:** portal-hypertensive bleeding requires specific hepatology/GI-led therapy and prevention decisions. Do not assume an acid-suppression-only or peptic-ulcer pathway covers suspected varices.

## SUP is prevention, not haemorrhage treatment
The **2026 Saudi Critical Care Society / Scandinavian / Kuwaiti** contextualized guidance considers coagulopathy, shock or chronic liver disease as factors for *risk-based* SUP; it also addresses discontinuation when risk factors disappear. It does **not** justify prescribing acid suppression to every ICU patient, automatically continuing it at discharge, or replacing acute bleed resuscitation with prophylaxis.
