# Acute liver failure (ALF) and acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF)
> **UNPUBLISHED PROPOSAL — AI-generated; not clinically reviewed.** Outside the first-release 40-topic inventory and the learner-facing working edition.

## Scope distinction
**ALF:** rapidly developing severe liver injury with coagulopathy and hepatic encephalopathy in a person without pre-existing cirrhosis (diagnostic criteria and exclusions determined by treating specialist). **ACLF:** acute decompensation in an individual with chronic liver disease/cirrhosis, with liver and often extrahepatic organ dysfunction. These syndromes have different prognostic frameworks and clinical priorities. Do not relabel either condition on the basis of an isolated INR, ammonia, transaminase value or confusion.

## Learning objectives
1. Recognise evolving neurological deterioration, aspiration risk and threats to airway protection in liver failure, while investigating alternative causes of delirium/encephalopathy.
2. Track glucose, haemodynamics, renal function/urine output, electrolytes, acid–base balance, infection concerns and bleeding, escalating new trends.
3. Distinguish monitoring and preparation for *team-directed* transplant-centre consultation from predicting transplant candidacy or operating a prognostic calculator independently.
4. Coordinate high-quality transfer handover and multidisciplinary review of prescriptions, sedation, nutrition and renal support.
5. Understand why ALF and ACLF require distinct neurological and prognostic interpretations.

## Bedside assessment and escalation
- **Immediate red flags:** decreasing responsiveness, rapidly changing mental status, seizure, new focal neurological findings, suspected aspiration, compromised airway, hypoglycaemia, rapidly evolving shock, infection or oliguria. Activate urgent ICU/medical assessment and local emergency pathway.
- **Neurological:** repeat structured examinations and hand over change over time; assess sedation exposure and potential confounding causes (stroke, sepsis, drug effect, glucose/electrolyte abnormalities). Do not call all agitation or sleepiness 'hepatic encephalopathy', and do not enter a negative delirium screen when not assessable.
- **Metabolic/renal:** obtain ordered glucose and serial electrolyte/renal/lactate assessments, trend fluid balance and urine output, report new hypoglycaemia or dysfunction. RRT and ammonia-removal strategies are intensivist/nephrology decisions, not routine nursing orders.
- **Bleeding/coagulation:** assess wounds, lines, mucosa and GI losses alongside platelet/coagulation trends. Abnormal INR in liver disease does not, by itself, indicate a nurse-directed blood product, reversal medicine or DIC diagnosis.
- **Airway/aspiration:** escalate impaired protective reflexes, hypoxaemia, respiratory fatigue or severe encephalopathy to airway-qualified staff; implement only authorised positioning/aspiration precautions. Never use a single score as an unsupervised intubation rule.
- **Escalation to liver/transplant specialists:** **2026 AASLD–AST guidance** calls for urgent transplant-centre referral for ALF, and specifically supports early contact rather than waiting until all criteria or severe deterioration. Referral does not mean every patient will be transplanted.
- **ACLF:** 2026 multisociety consensus emphasises *serial multiorgan assessment* over snapshot scores; liver, brain, kidney, respiration and circulation all matter. The team selects/uses a version-appropriate scoring system; no scoring table imported here.
- **Caution on ammonia:** values require context, method and syndrome; never use an ammonia result alone to establish/exclude all causes of altered mental state or independently titrate treatments.

## Fictional teaching case (not a published simulator)
**Phase 1:** A previously noncirrhotic adult with acute liver injury and rising INR is becoming drowsier. **Best priority:** assess ABC and neurological status, check urgent bedside glucose under protocol, alert ICU/hepatology team and report trends, prepare transfer coordination. **Unsafe distractor:** continue routine observations because only the INR has changed.
**Phase 2:** The patient has oliguria and worsening encephalopathy. **Best priority:** reassess airway risk, urine output, perfusion and metabolic trends; urgently escalate, support prescribed renal/neurological monitoring. **Unsafe distractor:** independently choose dialysis, sedatives or transfusion because an ammonia or INR number is high.
**Phase 3:** The receiving liver unit requests handover. **Best priority:** provide serial neurological/glucose/renal/haemodynamic findings, suspected aetiology and drug exposures, lines, treatments, pending tests and relevant contacts. **Unsafe distractor:** defer referral until transplant eligibility is certain.

## Three original knowledge checks (answers for clinical reviewers)
1. What differentiates ALF from ACLF at the syndrome level? **Expected:** pre-existing liver disease and differing organ failure context.
2. Does a raised ammonia value prove the mechanism of every acute mental-state change? **Expected:** no; reassess competing diagnoses and clinical condition.
3. Must referral await definite transplant eligibility? **Expected:** no; early discussion with specialist centre is recommended in ALF.
