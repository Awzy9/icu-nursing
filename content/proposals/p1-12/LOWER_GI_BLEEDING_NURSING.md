## Why it differs from the upper GI pathway
Haematochezia can arise from lower GI disease, **but a brisk upper GI haemorrhage can also present with red rectal blood**. The combination of bleeding and physiological deterioration should prompt investigation for either source, not a bedside nurse's assumption that all red blood must originate in the colon.

## Learning objectives
1. Identify high-risk rectal bleeding using repeated *clinical* assessments rather than stool colour, the latest haemoglobin or a single vital sign.
2. Activate urgent haemorrhage care for deteriorating perfusion, mental state, breathing or ongoing loss.
3. Recognise why upper GI bleeding remains in the differential with severe haematochezia and haemodynamic instability.
4. Support authorised access, specimens, blood bank and transfusion safety; collect a focused medication history, including antiplatelets, anticoagulants and last known doses.
5. Explain at a high level why ongoing instability may favour **team-directed CTA**, while colonoscopy is generally considered once stable and able to tolerate bowel preparation.
6. Recognise that bowel preparation in an unstable, vomiting or aspiration-prone ICU patient requires specific clinician assessment, not routine nursing initiation.

## Initial nursing assessment and response
**Recognition:** Record nature, frequency and estimated volume of bleeding where feasible; check airway/breathing/mental status, perfusion, pulse/BP trend, oxygenation, skin, urine output and relevant concurrent symptoms. A normal early haemoglobin cannot reliably eliminate active major blood loss.

**Immediate response:** Stay with the unstable patient, call local emergency/major-haemorrhage/GI response, reassess ABC/perfusion and communicate the changes. Arrange or support authorised monitoring, line access, crossmatch and laboratory work **in parallel**, without delaying the call to repeat blood tests.

**Focused context:** Recent procedures (including polypectomy), previous bleeding, liver disease/varices, abdominal symptoms and medication exposure are important in the handover. Do not diagnose diverticular haemorrhage or exclude an upper GI source on appearance alone.

**Blood product and anticoagulant safety:** Follow emergency release, identity, compatibility, reaction surveillance and ordered monitoring pathways. Escalate a due anticoagulant/antiplatelet for prescriber review when active bleeding is suspected. Stopping/reversing treatment, choosing components or using an unverified transfusion trigger are not independent nursing acts.

**Imaging / colonoscopy:** Escalate ongoing instability promptly: the clinician may pursue CTA, urgent procedural intervention, and/or upper endoscopy in patients with unstable haematochezia and suspected upper source. For patients who stabilise, full bowel preparation and endoscopy may be considered on a non-fixed clock by the GI team. Nurses should check preparedness, aspiration risk and contraindications to prescribed preparations; **never start rapid bowel prep autonomously**.

**After intervention:** Continue ordered serial observations, rebleeding and perfusion surveillance, bowel prep/procedure complications if applicable, and coordinate discharge/transfer guidance on antithrombotic decisions with prescribers. No universal timing of anticoagulant restart is specified.
