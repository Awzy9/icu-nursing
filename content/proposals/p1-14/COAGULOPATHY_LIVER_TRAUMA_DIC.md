# Coagulation changes in cirrhosis, trauma and suspected DIC — nursing interpretation limits
> **UNPUBLISHED AI-authored teaching proposal, NOT clinically reviewed.** No disease score, transfusion dose, blood component recommendation or reversal algorithm. Excluded from the original working edition.

## Learning goals
1. Recognise active bleeding and shock *before* interpreting a laboratory panel or deciding a diagnostic label.
2. Explain why the same prolonged PT/INR, platelet fall or fibrinogen value can have different significance in **cirrhosis/portal hypertension**, **major trauma** and **sepsis/suspected DIC**.
3. Know that **PT/INR in cirrhosis is a poor stand-alone predictor of bleeding**, so prophylactic plasma merely to “normalise” a laboratory number can be harmful and is not routine recommended care.
4. Recognise that **trauma-associated coagulopathy (TIC)** has injury, shock, fibrinolysis, dilutional, hypothermia and metabolic contexts; testing/treatment are clinician-directed, potentially using local viscoelastic methods.
5. Recognise that the **ISTH SSC updated its DIC definitions/scoring in 2025**. A 2001-era DIC score or a single elevated D-dimer should not be presented as the current universal or definitive rule.
6. Communicate serial clinical/laboratory trajectories in the context of *current bleeding*, renal/hepatic/organ dysfunction, anticoagulant exposure and specimen quality.

## Clinical-first triage
If the patient is hypotensive, poorly perfused, actively bleeding, newly encephalopathic or showing respiratory compromise, **remain with the patient, reassess ABC/perfusion and activate urgent bleeding/ICU help**. Avoid delaying escalation to redraw a possibly contaminated citrate sample, await a DIC calculator, or decide a plasma product. Repeat testing should proceed alongside care as ordered.

## Three distinct mechanisms and cautionary points
### A. Cirrhosis / portal-hypertensive bleed
- Standard PT/INR tests reflect only part of the altered coagulation system in cirrhosis. **An isolated elevated INR is not a reliable bleeding-risk predictor** and should not automatically trigger prophylactic plasma or be used alone to label DIC.
- **Variceal haemorrhage is strongly related to portal pressure**, not merely the clotting-test abnormality. AASLD and EASL caution against routine pre-procedural or spontaneous-bleed prevention by plasma solely to correct numbers. This **does not mean clinicians never administer blood products for active severe bleeding**: emergency resuscitation remains patient- and protocol-dependent.
- Cirrhosis also carries **thrombosis risk**, so “high INR means safely anticoagulated” is incorrect. Anticoagulation/antiplatelet decisions remain with prescribers.
- Abnormal ammonia, platelet and INR values do not automatically prove HE, DIC or transfusion eligibility; escalate the clinical syndrome and relevant trends.

### B. Major trauma with haemorrhagic shock / TIC
- Blood loss, tissue injury and hypoperfusion can produce rapidly evolving coagulopathy, compounded by resuscitation, hypothermia, acid–base and ionised-calcium disturbances. Transfusion/haemostatic management requires specialist-directed local trauma and blood-bank protocols.
- A single abnormal INR or platelet result **does not specify** a component, TXA decision, viscoelastic diagnosis or timing of intervention. Inform the treating team of bleeding sites, repeat clotting panels, fibrinogen, temperature, ordered ionised-calcium results, lactate/perfusion and blood product chronology.
- Severe suspected **TBI** creates a separate risk from applying a uniform low-pressure resuscitation rule. Escalate any neurological deterioration and avoid setting BP targets independently.

### C. Sepsis with suspected DIC (and other mimics)
- DIC is a clinical/laboratory process in the context of an underlying disorder, not simply a low platelet count or positive D-dimer. Fibrinogen can be misleadingly normal as an acute-phase reactant, and prolonged PT/aPTT may be due to liver disease, medicines or other causes.
- The **2025 ISTH SSC** proposed an updated definition and revised overt-DIC criteria. Full local version, scoring/validation and management must be checked by haematology. **Do not calculate a historical score and present it as the newest rule**, do not initiate reversal or product selection independently, and never delay patient rescue for score completion.
- Heparin exposure and thrombosis with platelet fall warrant team review of **HIT** differential as well as sepsis/DIC. Do not label HIT from the percentage fall alone.

## Original short case contrasts (unscored)
**Case 1 — cirrhosis:** A patient with portal hypertension has active haematemesis and a prolonged INR. **Best nursing priority:** emergency ABC/perfusion escalation, blood-bank/ordered GI team coordination, clear clinical/lab trend handover. **Unsafe:** automatically order/administer plasma solely to normalise INR; assume the INR determines bleeding severity.

**Case 2 — trauma:** A patient with multiple injuries remains hypoperfused after transfusion, becomes colder and develops new neurological signs. **Best nursing priority:** re-activate trauma/neurocritical response, assess patient, hand over temperature, ordered ionised calcium and evolving coagulation/blood product results. **Unsafe:** apply a blanket permissive BP target or independently choose calcium, TXA or blood component ratio.

**Case 3 — sepsis:** A septic adult develops line-site oozing and falling platelets, with a normal-range fibrinogen on one result. **Best nursing priority:** escalate evolving bleeding and organ dysfunction, notify laboratory of sample issues, repeat tests as ordered and report serial trends for possible DIC and alternatives. **Unsafe:** dismiss DIC because one fibrinogen is normal, or diagnose it from D-dimer alone.
