# Respiratory deterioration during or after transfusion — ICU nursing
> **UNPUBLISHED, AI-authored teaching proposal; NOT clinically reviewed.** This is a recognition and escalation exercise, not a diagnostic classification, resuscitation or diuretic/respiratory prescription. Separate from the original 40-topic and 20-scenario working edition.

## Learning goals
1. Detect respiratory deterioration with or without fever in a sedated, mechanically ventilated or noncommunicative patient receiving blood products.
2. Recognise overlapping possible causes including **transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO)**, **transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI)**, severe allergy/anaphylaxis, haemolysis, bacterial contamination and unrelated ICU respiratory causes.
3. Carry out the immediate **local suspected-transfusion-reaction pathway** and communicate timing, blood product and patient observations accurately.
4. Distinguish risk factors and diagnostic *clues* from a diagnosis or authority to prescribe diuretics, fluids, further blood products or ventilator changes.
5. Retain implicated product and administration set, safeguard line access, and coordinate blood-bank investigation without inadvertently infusing residual blood.

## Patient-first response
**High-risk change:** New hypoxaemia or increased oxygen/ventilator support requirement, tachypnoea (when spontaneously breathing), rising airway pressures or new bilateral pulmonary findings **during or after transfusion** is a potentially life-threatening event even without a rash or fever. In sedated patients the first cue may be the monitor or ventilator, not a patient complaint.

**Immediate nursing priority when a reaction is suspected:** Stop the **implicated blood transfusion** promptly; urgently assess airway, breathing, circulation, blood pressure/perfusion and other reaction signs; summon emergency medical/critical-care and transfusion-service help per hospital policy; maintain safe vascular access **without flushing the implicated transfusion set into the patient**. Follow local instruction on a **new/separate administration set** and compatible IV fluid. Preserve the unit and set, recheck recipient/component identity and document symptom onset and amount already infused. Do not restart the implicated unit or independently substitute a new component; the treating and transfusion teams direct any exception in life-threatening ongoing bleeding.

**Potential TACO considerations:** Susceptibility is increased in people with cardiac or renal dysfunction, positive fluid balance, older age and substantial/rapid transfusion exposure. New or worsening pulmonary oedema and circulatory overload signs may be present. A pressure rise, positive fluid balance, fluid overload findings and clinical response to clinician-directed diuresis may *support* the distinction, but none alone proves TACO.

**Potential TRALI considerations:** Acute hypoxaemia/pulmonary oedema related temporally to transfusion may represent TRALI, including in a critically ill patient with other lung-injury risks. Distinguishing TRALI from TACO often requires clinical, blood-bank and respiratory evaluation; they can overlap or remain indeterminate. **Do not diagnose TRALI solely because there is fever or a normal blood pressure.**

**Time-window safety:** The commonly used surveillance definitions consider TACO during or **up to 12 hours after** transfusion and TRALI with onset during or **within 6 hours** of transfusion; the named condition is determined using full criteria and competing causes, not only the clock. A later deteriorating patient still needs urgent evaluation even if outside a surveillance definition. Avoid incorrectly teaching “all post-transfusion dyspnoea is TRALI” or “only reactions while the unit runs matter.”

**What to share immediately:** Baseline and new respiratory/ventilator findings, onset relative to component start/stop, type/amount/number of components, observations of blood pressure/perfusion, fluid balance and renal/cardiac history, rash/fever/pain, alternative causes (sepsis, aspiration, cardiac event), relevant ordered chest imaging/labs and actions already taken. Clinicians determine tests (which may include imaging, natriuretic peptides, echocardiography), diagnosis, diuresis/fluids and respiratory therapy.

## Fictional cases — proposal only; not scored or validated
**Case A:** A mechanically ventilated patient with AKI receives a red-cell unit. New oxygen desaturation, increasing ventilator support need and BP change occur during infusion. **Priority:** stop implicated unit for suspected reaction, assess ABC/perfusion, call urgent help and blood bank, do not flush residual blood and preserve component/set. **Unsafe option:** continue the unit because the patient cannot report chest discomfort.

**Case B:** A patient with chronic cardiac disease develops breathing difficulty and a positive fluid balance after transfusion. **Priority:** recognise possible TACO while preserving **TRALI and other urgent causes** in the differential; summon clinician review and support ordered tests/management. **Unsafe option:** independently diagnose TACO, give diuretics and resume transfusion without team direction.

**Case C:** Within hours of transfusion a patient has severe hypoxaemia and fever but no clear pressure elevation. **Priority:** treat as potential severe transfusion reaction and assess causes with the emergency/blood-bank team. **Unsafe option:** declare TRALI from fever alone and skip product/identity investigation.

## Review questions (not graded)
1. What is the first priority when respiratory compromise creates concern for a transfusion reaction? **Expected:** stop implicated unit, assess ABC/perfusion, summon clinical and transfusion support, preserve access without flushing residual unit blood.
2. Can a single elevated BP or a single BNP result establish TACO, or fever establish TRALI? **Expected:** no. These are contextual clues requiring a full assessment.
3. Why are “within 6 hours” and “up to 12 hours” not universal rules for dismissing deterioration? **Expected:** surveillance criteria aid classification, but patient safety and differential assessment take precedence.
