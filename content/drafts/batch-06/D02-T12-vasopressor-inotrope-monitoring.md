# Vasopressor and inotrope monitoring
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Prescriber decisions (drug, dose, targets) are described only as what the sources say the team may order; infusion-site and extravasation care are flagged as unsupported by any source read.

## 1. Overview
Vasopressors raise blood pressure by constricting blood vessels; inotropes increase the force of the heart's contraction. ISMP classifies intravenous adrenergic agonists and inotropic medications among its high-alert categories, and the FDA page lists vasopressors among high-risk infusions for which an independent double check may be required. The nurse monitors the patient's response (blood pressure against the ordered target, perfusion, rhythm, urine output), the access and the infusion, and escalates when the target is not met or the patient worsens. The prescriber chooses the drug, target and titration limits; some units delegate titration to the nurse within a written protocol.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. State what SSC 2026 says about the initial mean arterial pressure (MAP) target in septic shock, and why that is a starting point set by the team.
2. Explain when the sources say invasive blood pressure monitoring is advised.
3. List perfusion measures the SSC 2026 page names and how they are used together.
4. Describe pump and infusion safety measures for vasoactive infusions.
5. Recognise warning signs that need urgent escalation.
6. Separate nursing, prescriber and pharmacist responsibilities, and identify what the sources do not cover.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Patients on vasoactive infusions are the most unstable on the unit. Small changes in rate, a pump problem or a line failure can cause large changes in blood pressure within minutes. The nurse's continuous observation is the main safeguard.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Blood pressure and MAP against the ordered target.** SSC 2026 (septic shock only) recommends an initial MAP target of 65 mm Hg over higher targets (strong; moderate certainty). Its remark says it is not feasible to hold MAP at exactly 65, so a reasonable range (for example within 5 mm Hg) should be used and vasopressors titrated to keep MAP in that range. For adults 65 years or older it suggests an initial MAP range of 60–65 mm Hg over higher ranges (conditional; low certainty). The actual target is the prescriber's order for the individual patient.
- **Perfusion, not pressure alone.** SSC 2026 suggests serial lactate measurements to guide resuscitation in sepsis with elevated lactate or septic shock (conditional; low certainty) and using capillary refill time as an adjunct to other perfusion measures (conditional; low certainty). Its remark warns that fluids should not be continued simply until lactate normalises. Also look at skin (temperature, mottling), mental state and urine output.
- **How blood pressure is measured.** SSC 2026 suggests either invasive or non-invasive blood pressure monitoring (conditional; very low certainty) and advises invasive monitoring in patients with shock who need intermediate-to-high doses, escalating doses or multiple vasopressors, who are having frequent arterial blood sampling, or whose non-invasive readings are inconsistent on repeated assessment. The 2014 ESICM consensus (ungraded best practice) recommended arterial and central venous catheter insertion in shock not responding to initial therapy or needing a vasopressor infusion. See the arterial-line draft (D02-T04).
- **Rhythm and heart rate.** The SSC 2026 remarks note that norepinephrine may be preferred with tachyarrhythmia or significant sinus tachycardia and epinephrine with bradyarrhythmia; this is a prescriber choice, but it tells the nurse that rhythm change is clinically relevant to drug selection.
- **Cardiac function.** SSC 2026 suggests inotropes for septic shock with cardiac dysfunction and persistent hypoperfusion despite adequate fluid and arterial pressure (conditional; very low certainty), and states that inotropes should be used in addition to, not instead of, vasopressors in patients who need vasopressors to maintain MAP.
- **Access and site.** SSC 2026 suggests starting vasopressors peripherally rather than delaying until central access is secured (conditional; very low certainty), with the remark that data are insufficient to recommend a duration of use, dose or access route (size or site of peripheral line); midline catheters were not considered. The older ESICM 2014 text says central access may be required and preferred when vasopressor infusions are needed. **These differ**, and the platform must not present either as a universal rule; follow local policy.
- **The infusion:** pump programming, labelling, drug library, double-check status and alerts (see D05-T16).

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Confirm the order (drug, concentration, target and titration limits) before starting; verify pump programming at the moments the FDA page names (change of shift, setting change, new bag, new tubing) and obtain an independent double check where policy requires it.
- Monitor and record blood pressure and MAP, heart rate and rhythm, perfusion and urine output at the intervals set by the unit; trend them rather than reacting to a single value.
- Titrate only within a written protocol or order set; otherwise call the prescriber for any change to the plan.
- Use the monitoring the sources describe: invasive pressure monitoring when advised, other patient monitors (cardiac, oximetry, end-tidal CO₂, glucose as relevant) to detect over- or under-infusion, as the FDA page says.
- Keep a back-up plan for pump failure: stay with the patient while a working pump is obtained, as the FDA page describes for high-acuity patients.
- Plan replacement of bags or syringes so that the infusion is not interrupted; the FDA page notes that pump problems can cause under-infusion or delay in therapy. The method of changeover is local policy and is not sourced here.
- Report drug-related or pump-related incidents and near misses.
- **Better supported (Phase 3):** US FDA labels for norepinephrine, dopamine, epinephrine and phenylephrine each say to avoid extravasation, to check the infusion site for free flow (frequently), and to monitor for signs of it; the norepinephrine and dopamine labels say to infuse into a large vein and to monitor the extremities for skin changes in susceptible patients; the epinephrine label says blanching along the vein may precede obvious extravasation and suggests considering a change of infusion site. The labels also describe a clinician-directed emergency treatment by infiltration with an adrenergic blocking agent (not taught; no doses). The Open RN grids add limb colour and temperature monitoring with dopamine and ECG, blood pressure, cardiac output and urine output monitoring with dopamine and dobutamine (see D05-T03).
- **Peripheral administration evidence (Phase 3):** a 2026 JAMA Netw Open meta-analysis (CC BY) found a pooled minor adverse event incidence of about 2.3 % for peripheral vasopressors, with major events rare through short peripheral catheters and venous thromboembolism seen only with midline catheters in its data; most studies were retrospective with unclear or high risk of bias. A 2026 Critical Care Explorations study notes that peripheral vasopressor protocols vary widely in drugs, doses, duration and monitoring requirements. **No standard monitoring frequency, recognition sign list or nursing management procedure was found.**
- **Still unresolved:** the frequency of site checks, the nursing recognition signs and first-line management of extravasation, line compatibility and dedicated-lumen practice; follow local policy and flag for clinician review.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Blood pressure and MAP against target; heart rate and rhythm; skin perfusion and capillary refill; urine output; lactate trend where ordered; mental state; oxygen saturation; pump alarms and settings; infusion site and line patency; fluid balance; peripheral perfusion of limbs (fingers and toes); frequency set by unit policy.

## 9. Important findings – SSC 2026 statements for septic shock (recommendation-level)

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
MAP below the ordered target despite increasing doses; rising dose requirement or a second or third agent added; new arrhythmia or heart-rate change; cold, mottled or blue fingers or toes; rising lactate or falling urine output; new confusion; chest pain; sudden fall in pressure after a bag, syringe or pump change; pump alarm, occlusion or suspected disconnection; swelling, pain, blanching, discolouration or leakage at the infusion site (unresolved – see above); infusion approaching empty with no replacement ready.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately if the target is not reached, the dose requirement is rising, a new arrhythmia or chest pain develops, perfusion worsens, the infusion is interrupted, a pump fails, or the site looks abnormal. For the failing pump, stay with the patient while a working pump is obtained.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, septic shock, MAP 58 against an ordered target of 65 and falling over the last 20 minutes. **B:** on the first vasoactive infusion for 6 hours; at the last titration the pressure responded briefly; urine output has fallen. **A:** inadequate response and worsening perfusion. **R:** please review now; I need a decision on increasing support or adding a second agent, and I am checking the line, pump and lactate.

## 13. Potential complications
Persistent hypotension; loss of blood-pressure support if an infusion is interrupted or a pump fails (the FDA page names over-infusion, under-infusion and delay in therapy); drug-related errors. Drug-specific adverse effects (for example arrhythmia, limb or organ ischaemia) and infusion-site injury are general clinical knowledge that was NOT traced to any source read and are flagged for clinician review.

## 14. Documentation
Order, drug and rate as programmed, double-check record, MAP target and the values at each assessment, titration steps and who authorised them, perfusion findings, rhythm, urine output, lactate results, line and site checks, pump alarms and actions, escalations and responses.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At the start of the shift the nurse reads the MAP target range in the order, checks that the pump settings match the order and the label on the line, checks the arterial waveform and site, confirms that a replacement syringe is ready, and records a baseline set of perfusion observations. When MAP drifts below range, she checks the line and pump before changing anything, and calls the doctor if the protocol does not cover the next step.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 72-year-old man with septic shock is on a vasoactive infusion. His MAP has been at 62 for 30 minutes, his fingers are cool, and his urine output has fallen. The nurse checks the pump and line, confirms the arterial trace is accurate, reports to the doctor that the target is not met despite escalating support and that perfusion is worsening, and asks for a decision about the next step. The doctor also asks for a repeat lactate; the nurse records the result and the response.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (understanding).** What does SSC 2026 recommend as the initial MAP target in adults with septic shock?
A. An initial MAP target of 65 mm Hg over higher targets, with vasopressors titrated to keep MAP within a reasonable range of it.
B. An initial MAP target of 85 mm Hg, because higher targets have been shown to improve organ perfusion in these patients.
C. A systolic pressure above 120 mm Hg, since the systolic pressure is the better guide to perfusion than the mean pressure.
D. No specific target, because the page says that the target should always be chosen at the bedside by the nurse alone.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The page gives a strong recommendation (moderate certainty) for an initial MAP of 65 over higher targets, with a remark about using a range.
**Why not B:** The page recommends 65 over higher targets.
**Why not C:** The statement concerns mean arterial pressure, not systolic pressure.
**Why not D:** The page gives a target; the order for the individual patient comes from the prescriber.

**Q2 (judgment).** A patient on a vasoactive infusion has an MAP below the ordered range for 20 minutes. What is the best first nursing action?
A. Increase the infusion rate at once, without checking the trace, the line or the pump, to bring the pressure up quickly.
B. Document the value and recheck at the next scheduled observation, because a single low value is expected on vasopressors.
C. Check the patient, the arterial trace, the line and the pump, and report to the prescriber if the target is still not met.
D. Stop the infusion immediately, since the drug is clearly not working in this patient and a different agent will be needed.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The FDA page recommends monitoring and checking the pump; the nurse confirms the reading and the infusion before escalating, and titrates only within an order or protocol.
**Why not A:** Titration needs an order or protocol and a check of the reading and equipment first.
**Why not B:** A sustained low pressure in a patient on support needs action, not delay.
**Why not D:** Stopping a vasoactive infusion abruptly without an order is unsafe.

**Q3 (application).** Which patient with shock is the best example of one for whom SSC 2026 advises invasive blood pressure monitoring?
A. A patient on a low, stable dose of a single vasopressor and consistent cuff readings on repeated checks.
B. A patient on escalating doses of vasopressor whose cuff readings are inconsistent on repeated assessment.
C. A patient who is recovering and no longer needs vasopressors but has a normal blood pressure on every cuff reading.
D. A patient with mild hypotension who responds to a single small fluid bolus.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The remark advises invasive monitoring for escalating doses, intermediate-to-high doses, multiple vasopressors, frequent arterial sampling or inconsistent non-invasive readings.
**Why not A:** Stable, low-dose therapy with consistent cuff readings does not meet those criteria.
**Why not C:** A patient off vasopressors does not meet the criteria.
**Why not D:** Mild hypotension responding to a bolus does not meet them either.

**Q4 (understanding).** What do SSC 2026 and the older ESICM 2014 text say about the access used to start vasopressors?
A. Both say a central line must be in place before any vasopressor is started in a patient with septic shock, whatever the delay this causes.
B. Both say vasopressors may be given through an arterial line, because it gives continuous blood pressure readings.
C. Both say the site does not matter and may be chosen by the bedside nurse without any local policy.
D. SSC 2026 suggests starting peripherally rather than delaying, while ESICM 2014 said central access may be preferred; local policy decides.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The two sources differ, and SSC 2026's own remark says data are insufficient on duration, dose and site.
**Why not A:** SSC 2026 suggests not delaying for central access.
**Why not B:** Arterial lines are for monitoring, not for vasopressor administration.
**Why not C:** Local policy and the order define the access and site.

**Q5 (safety).** Which of the following is a measure the FDA page describes for a patient on a high-risk infusion whose pump fails?
A. Leaving the patient briefly to find a replacement pump, so that as little time as possible is lost on the unit.
B. Switching to a rapid bolus of the same drug by hand to cover the interruption in the infusion while the pump is replaced.
C. Staying with and closely monitoring the patient while a colleague obtains a working pump.
D. Waiting until the end of the shift to report the failure and keeping the pump in use until then, to avoid a gap.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The FDA page describes staying with and closely monitoring the patient while another staff member obtains a working pump.
**Why not A:** The page says the nurse should stay with the patient.
**Why not B:** A manual bolus of a vasoactive drug is not described and is unsafe without an order.
**Why not D:** Faulty pumps are to be removed from use and reported.

**Q6 (scope).** Which of these is a prescriber decision rather than a nursing task?
A. Checking the pump programming against the order each time a new syringe or bag is connected to the line.
B. Setting the MAP target and deciding whether to add a second agent.
C. Documenting the blood pressure trend, the perfusion findings and the urine output at each assessment.
D. Reporting a sudden fall in pressure after a bag change to the prescriber without delay.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Target-setting and choosing agents are prescriber decisions.
**Why not A:** Programming verification is a nursing safety check.
**Why not C:** Documentation is a nursing task.
**Why not D:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
