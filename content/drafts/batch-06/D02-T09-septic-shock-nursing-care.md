# Septic shock – the nursing role in the first hours and in persistent shock
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Statements are attributed at recommendation level to the SCCM page for SSC 2026; treatment decisions are the prescriber's.

## 1. Overview
Septic shock is sepsis with circulatory failure that needs vasopressors to maintain blood pressure and is accompanied by hypoperfusion. SSC 2026 states that sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies and that treatment and resuscitation should begin immediately (good practice statement). The nurse's role is to recognise deterioration early, make sure the time-critical elements happen (cultures, antimicrobials, fluids, lactate, access, escalation), monitor the response closely, recognise when shock is persisting, and communicate clearly. Choices of antimicrobials, fluid volume and vasoactive drugs are made by the prescriber.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe the time-critical elements the SSC 2026 page lists for septic shock and the nurse's part in each.
2. Describe how to monitor response to fluids and vasopressors, using several perfusion measures together.
3. Recognise features of persistent or worsening septic shock and escalate.
4. Explain why SSC 2026 advises reassessing fluid need rather than giving fluid by routine.
5. Separate nursing actions from prescriber, pharmacist and microbiology decisions.
6. Recognise what these sources do not tell the nurse.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
The SSC 2026 statements below set time expectations for antimicrobials (ideally within 1 hour of recognition for septic shock), ICU admission (within 6 hours, for patients who need it) and source control (ideally within 6 hours). Nurses are often the first to notice deterioration, and are responsible for several of the actions that meet those targets.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Recognition and screening.** For acutely ill adults in hospital, SSC 2026 recommends using NEWS, NEWS2, MEWS or SIRS rather than qSOFA as a single tool to screen for sepsis (strong; moderate certainty) and states that sepsis is a clinical diagnosis and must not be ruled in or out with a single biomarker or test (good practice statement).
- **Perfusion assessment.** SSC 2026 suggests serial lactate to guide resuscitation (conditional; low certainty) and capillary refill time as an adjunct to other measures of perfusion (conditional; low certainty). Its remark advises individualising fluid after the initial bolus and the lactate decrement rather than continuing fluid until lactate normalises. It describes clinical features suggestive of unstable shock – the page text read lists severely reduced blood pressure and mottled skin among others – and says that unstable shock is determined by physical examination.
- **Blood pressure.** MAP target and monitoring are described in D02-T12.
- **Fluid response.** SSC 2026 suggests using dynamic measures (for example response to a passive leg raise or a fluid bolus, using stroke volume, stroke volume variation, pulse pressure or pulse pressure variation) over physical examination or static measures alone (conditional; low certainty).
- **Source of infection:** look for a source that may need drainage or removal of a device; SSC 2026 suggests early source control, ideally within 6 hours, when a specific anatomical source requires it (conditional; very low certainty).
- **Patient factors the page names for fluid choices:** heart failure, cirrhosis, traumatic brain injury – the SSC remarks mention these when discussing fluid strategy and fluid type, which are prescriber decisions.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- **Escalate promptly** when screening scores or clinical signs suggest sepsis or septic shock, using the local sepsis pathway.
- **Blood cultures:** SSC 2026 recommends collecting blood cultures as soon as possible and ideally before antimicrobials (strong; low certainty). The nurse collects them (or ensures collection) without letting this delay antimicrobials unduly; see D06-T01 for the sepsis bundle.
- **Antimicrobials:** SSC 2026 recommends giving them immediately, ideally within 1 hour of recognition, for possible, probable or definite septic shock (strong; very low certainty). The nurse prepares, checks allergy status, gives as ordered and records the time. Which drug and dose is a prescriber and pharmacist decision.
- **Access and monitoring:** establish the access the team needs; SSC 2026 suggests vasopressors can be started peripherally rather than delayed (conditional; very low certainty), with the caveat in D02-T12.
- **Fluids:** SSC 2026 recommends crystalloids as first-line fluid (strong; moderate), suggests balanced crystalloids over 0.9% saline (conditional; moderate) except in patients with sepsis and traumatic brain injury, where it suggests 0.9% saline, and suggests an initial weight-based minimum crystalloid volume within the first 3 hours (conditional; low) with frequent reassessment to avoid under- or over-resuscitation. The nurse gives the ordered fluid, observes the response and reports signs of overload or non-response.
- **Vasopressors and inotropes:** see D02-T12.
- **Lactate:** collect and record serial lactate as ordered; trend the values.
- **Support:** SSC 2026 suggests against using antipyretic therapy, pharmacological or cooling, to improve clinical outcomes in sepsis, but notes that this does not apply to pain or symptom control or to patients with other temperature-control indications; the nurse follows the order and does not treat fever by reflex.
- **Communicate:** use a structured handover, keep the family informed according to policy, and document times.
- **Not covered here and flagged:** nursing details of line care, extravasation, haemodynamic troubleshooting, nursing titration protocols and local sepsis-bundle documentation are not supported by sources read.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Blood pressure and MAP; heart rate and rhythm; respiratory rate and oxygen saturation; temperature; capillary refill, skin colour, temperature and mottling; mental state; urine output and fluid balance; lactate trend; vasopressor requirements; oxygenation and ventilator needs; blood glucose; time stamps for cultures, antimicrobials, fluids, ICU admission and source-control steps.

## 9. Important findings – SSC 2026 septic-shock statements (recommendation-level)

|---|---|---|---|

| Cultures | As soon as possible, ideally before antimicrobials | Strong; low | Do not delay antimicrobials unduly |
| Antimicrobials | Immediately, ideally within 1 hour of recognition (possible/probable/definite septic shock) | Strong; very low | Record time |
| Fluid | Crystalloid first-line; balanced crystalloids over 0.9% saline (0.9% saline in sepsis with traumatic brain injury); initial weight-based minimum volume in the first 3 hours; frequent reassessment | Strong, moderate; conditional, moderate and low | Reassess after each bolus |

**Updated ESICM 2025 general-shock monitoring (only abstract and comparison table examined):** Monitor capillary refill serially, supplemented by skin temperature and mottling. In persistent shock, assess fluid responsiveness before further fluids and use dynamic rather than static indicators where applicable. Keep these general-shock monitoring statements distinct from the SSC 2026 septic-shock treatment recommendations. Full ESICM 2025 text was not read.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Rising vasopressor requirement; MAP persistently below target; rising or non-clearing lactate; cold, mottled skin or prolonged capillary refill; falling urine output; new confusion; worsening oxygenation; new arrhythmia; signs of fluid overload after boluses; a source that has not been addressed; a delay in antimicrobials or cultures; a patient with sepsis awaiting an ICU bed.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Recognition, escalation, monitoring, collecting cultures and lactate, timing and recording, communicating, family updates | Independent nursing |
| Giving ordered antimicrobials and fluids; titrating vasoactive infusions within protocol | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Choosing antimicrobials, fluids, vasoactive drugs, steroids, bicarbonate; deciding targets and source control; ICU admission | Prescriber decision (with pharmacist, microbiology and surgical teams) |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for suspected sepsis with organ dysfunction, hypotension, a rising lactate, a vasopressor requirement or deterioration. For persistent shock, assess perfusion and respiratory status and summon help rather than waiting for the next review. Reassess the response to prescribed resuscitation, including new signs of fluid intolerance; do not independently give repeated fluid boluses solely to normalize lactate. If vasoactive access fails or extravasation is suspected, stop delivery through the affected site and obtain urgent clinician-directed alternative support under the local pathway (see D02-T12 and MD-01).
SBAR — **S:** bed X, septic shock, MAP below target and rising vasopressor requirement 3 hours after the first fluid bolus. **B:** cultures taken, antimicrobials given at the recorded time, fluids as ordered; lactate not falling. **A:** worsening perfusion despite resuscitation; source not yet identified. **R:** please review now; I need a decision about further fluid versus more support, a source search and further monitoring.

## 13. Potential complications
Under- or over-resuscitation with fluids (named in the SSC 2026 remark). Persistent shock, organ failure, arrhythmia and delay-related harm are general clinical knowledge that was NOT traced to the sources read and are flagged for clinician review.

## 14. Documentation
Time of recognition; screening score; time and number of cultures; time of antimicrobials and agents; fluid type, volume, times and response; lactate values and times; MAP target and values; vasopressor changes; perfusion findings; urine output; escalation calls and responses; ICU admission and source-control timing; family communication.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At 14:05 a patient with a rising early-warning score and a hot, flushed appearance becomes hypotensive. The nurse calls the sepsis team, draws blood cultures and a lactate, checks allergies and prepares the antimicrobial that the doctor orders, starts the ordered fluid, records the times in the sepsis chart and notes the capillary refill and urine output before and after the bolus.


**What to compare after the bolus.** Record the blood pressure and capillary refill before and after the ordered fluid. Compare the findings together and report any new increase in oxygen requirement or breathing difficulty during reassessment. Use the urine-output trend over a stated interval. It provides context for perfusion, while a brief interval may be too short to show a response. If findings remain concerning, report them before further treatment decisions.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 66-year-old woman with a urinary source is on a vasoactive infusion after fluid resuscitation. Four hours later the dose requirement has risen, her lactate is not falling, and her skin is mottled. The nurse reports these changes, asks the doctor to review for a drainable source and cardiac dysfunction, and records the sequence and times of care.


**Why the rising support requirement matters.** Review the blood pressure alongside the vasoactive dose required to maintain it. In this case, worsening mottling and persistent lactate elevation make the overall trend concerning. Report the dose and pressure trend, the timing of the lactate measurements, and the latest skin and urine findings. Serial lactate and capillary refill can inform reassessment alongside the rest of the clinical picture. The team can use the pattern to reassess source control, cardiac function and other contributors.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A patient with suspected septic shock needs blood cultures and antimicrobials. What is the best sequence according to SSC 2026?
A. Wait until the culture results return before giving any antimicrobial, so that the choice of drug is exactly right for the organism.
B. Give antimicrobials first and take blood cultures only if the patient does not improve afterwards on the chosen treatment.
C. Take the cultures after the antimicrobials have run in, because the first dose is the priority and cultures can wait until later.
D. Obtain cultures promptly, ideally before antimicrobials; give emergency antimicrobials without avoidable delay.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** SSC 2026 recommends taking blood cultures as soon as possible and ideally before antimicrobials (strong, low certainty); in possible, probable or definite septic shock, antimicrobial therapy should be given immediately, ideally within 1 hour of recognition (strong, very low certainty). Cultures must not become a reason to delay emergency treatment. Coordinate investigation and urgent source-control assessment in parallel.
**Why not A:** Waiting for culture results would greatly delay treatment.
**Why not B:** Cultures should be obtained, not skipped, and not only if the patient fails to improve.
**Why not C:** Cultures are ideally taken before antimicrobials, without unnecessary delay.

**Q2 (application).** A patient with septic shock remains poorly perfused after initial fluid treatment. The team is considering another bolus. Which assessment approach best informs the next decision?
A. Use the current blood pressure and a single central venous pressure measurement to decide whether further fluid is needed.
B. Use dynamic measures (such as response to a passive leg raise or a bolus) in preference to examination or static measures alone.
C. Repeat the initial volume based on the patient's weight, then assess the cumulative response once all fluid has been administered.
D. Base the next bolus on the lactate trend without evaluating the response to the previous fluid.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** SSC 2026 conditionally favors dynamic assessment rather than static measures or examination alone (low certainty). Depending on the method and measurement conditions, assess the hemodynamic response to passive leg raising or an authorized fluid challenge using flow or suitable pressure-derived indices. Fluid responsiveness does not establish a need for fluid; assess congestion, perfusion and risk of harm.
**Why not A:** Static pressure measurements alone do not establish fluid responsiveness or the benefit of another bolus.
**Why not C:** The page stresses frequent reassessment to avoid under- or over-resuscitation.
**Why not D:** The remark advises against continuing fluid simply until lactate normalises.

**Q3 (prioritization).** Four patients with sepsis are on the unit. Which needs the most urgent medical review?
A. A patient with a stable MAP at target, a falling lactate, a warm peripheral circulation and a rising urine output.
B. A patient with a mild fever, normal vital signs, a normal lactate and no organ dysfunction on the early-warning score.
C. A patient whose vasopressor requirement is rising with mottled skin and a lactate that is not falling.
D. A patient who is well three days after starting antimicrobials, eating and drinking, and about to move to the ward.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Increasing vasoactive support, mottling and a persistent lactate abnormality suggest ongoing shock. Activate urgent review, assess perfusion and treatment delivery, and report trends. Lactate has non-hypoperfusion causes, so do not escalate fluids merely to normalize it.
**Why not A:** These are signs of response.
**Why not B:** This patient still needs clinical monitoring, but the information given does not suggest the same immediate instability as option C. A normal lactate alone cannot exclude shock.
**Why not D:** A recovering patient does not need urgent review.

**Q4 (safety).** A patient with septic shock has a temperature of 38.8 °C and is comfortable. What does SSC 2026 say about antipyretic therapy?
A. It suggests against using antipyretic therapy to improve outcomes, though this does not apply to symptom or pain control.
B. It recommends cooling patients with septic shock to a normal temperature straight away, to improve their chances of recovery.
C. It recommends routine antipyretics as a standard part of the first-hour sepsis bundle, whatever the temperature.
D. It says that antipyretic therapy is required before giving antimicrobials, in order to prevent reactions to the first dose.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The page suggests against antipyretic therapy, pharmacological or cooling, for improving clinical outcomes, with exceptions for symptom control or other indications.
**Why not B:** The page does not recommend routine cooling.
**Why not C:** Antipyretics are not part of the bundle.
**Why not D:** No such requirement is stated.

**Q5 (application).** Following the acute resuscitation phase, a patient with septic shock no longer needs escalating vasopressors or ongoing volume expansion but has increasing weight, peripheral oedema and positive fluid balance. What does SSC 2026 suggest the team may consider after assessing the patient's cardiorespiratory status and clinical course?
A. Active fluid removal after the acute resuscitation phase, as a suggestion with very low certainty.
B. Further large fluid boluses to treat the oedema, which indicates that the circulation is dry.
C. Stopping all monitoring now that the blood pressure is stable, to allow the patient to rest.
D. Switching to an albumin-only fluid policy for all patients, whatever their condition.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** SSC 2026 conditionally suggests considering active fluid removal after initial resuscitation (very low certainty). The team reviews cardiorespiratory function, vasoactive support, fluid balance and trajectory. Monitor for hypotension or poor perfusion during authorized removal; peripheral oedema does not prove adequate intravascular volume.
**Why not B:** Oedema and weight gain suggest overload, not a dry circulation.
**Why not C:** Monitoring continues after the pressure is stable because the patient's condition can change.
**Why not D:** The page suggests crystalloids alone over supplemental albumin in general, with exceptions.

**Q6 (scope).** Which of the following is a prescriber decision rather than a nursing action?
A. Taking the blood cultures and the lactate sample, labelling them correctly and recording the times in the sepsis chart.
B. Noting capillary refill, skin colour, mental state and urine output before and after each fluid bolus that is given.
C. Reporting a rising vasopressor requirement to the doctor using a structured handover such as SBAR without any delay.
D. Choosing the antimicrobial agent and deciding whether to add corticosteroids.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Selecting drugs and deciding on steroids are prescribing decisions.
**Why not A:** Specimen collection and timing are nursing tasks.
**Why not B:** Observation is a nursing task.
**Why not C:** Escalation is a nursing responsibility.
