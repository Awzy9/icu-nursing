# Blood product administration and recognising transfusion reactions
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Bedside steps are taken from the Circular where it gives them and otherwise from a US nursing textbook checklist (attributed); rates, monitoring intervals and first-line actions after a reaction are left to local transfusion policy.

## 1. Overview
Blood and blood components can save life but carry risk. Serious reactions can follow a small volume, can look alike at first (fever, breathlessness, low blood pressure), and need immediate action. The Circular of Information states that the intended recipient and the blood container must be properly identified before transfusion starts, that aseptic technique is required, that the patient must be observed and vital signs recorded before, during and after the transfusion, and that if a reaction occurs the transfusion must be stopped immediately. The nurse's role is verification, careful administration, observation, recognition, stopping, escalating and documenting. Decisions about whether to transfuse, what to transfuse and how to treat a reaction belong to the prescriber and the transfusion service.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe the administration safeguards in the Circular's general instructions for use.
2. Name immediate and delayed transfusion reactions and their key features as the Circular describes them.
3. Distinguish features that point to different reaction types, and explain why the nurse acts first and classifies later.
4. Describe what must happen when a reaction is suspected: stop, escalate, send items to the transfusion service, document.
5. Identify which patients are at particular risk of circulatory overload.
6. Separate nursing actions from prescriber and transfusion-service decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
ICU patients are often unconscious, sedated, ventilated, septic or bleeding, so classic symptoms (itching, chills, back pain) may be missing. The Circular notes that lack of symptoms does not exclude an acute haemolytic reaction, and that in anaesthetised patients haemoglobinuria, hypotension and bleeding from a coagulopathy may be the first signs. Patients with cardiac, kidney or lung disease, those who are already fluid-positive, and those receiving large or rapid transfusions face additional hazards.

## 6. Clinical assessment
**Before the unit is started (the Circular's general instructions):**
- The intended recipient and the blood container must be properly identified before the transfusion starts. How identification is performed is defined by the transfusion service and local policy; the Circular refers readers to the AABB Standards and Technical Manual, which were not read. The Open RN checklist (US textbook) describes a two-person verification in the presence of the patient that matches the order and the patient, comparing the name and identification number on the wristband with the blood bag label, the bag identification number, ABO group, Rh compatibility, the compatibility test interpretation and the patient's transfusion-service number; it also describes confirming written informed consent before a non-emergency transfusion, asking about allergies and earlier reactions, and starting the transfusion within a set time of collecting the unit from the blood bank (the interval is a local policy and is not quoted here).
- Inspect the container immediately before use. It must not be used if the container is not intact or the appearance is abnormal – the Circular names excessive haemolysis, a significant colour change in the bag compared with the tubing segments, floccular material and a cloudy appearance as examples – and the transfusion service must be contacted.
- Obtain baseline vital signs (the Circular calls for periodic observation and recording before, during and after; the Open RN checklist says to take vital signs shortly before starting, assess breath sounds, heart rate, blood pressure, skin colour and current laboratory results, and identify risks such as fever, heart failure, kidney disease or fluid overload risk).

**During and after:**
- Watch for the reactions listed in section 9. Life-threatening reactions can follow only a small volume, so the Circular states the rate should initially be slow unless the clinical condition indicates otherwise; the actual rates are for the order and local policy.
- Be aware that the Circular describes fever (a temperature rise of at least 1 °C) and/or chills/rigors during or within 4 hours after transfusion as possible features of a febrile non-haemolytic reaction (FNHTR) *when no other cause is established*. Do not diagnose FNHTR from fever alone. Stop and assess a suspected acute reaction, notify the clinical team and transfusion service as required, and exclude dangerous alternative causes according to severity and local policy. The precise post-transfusion observation interval remains local policy.
- Delayed reactions (haemolytic, 2 to 14 days; post-transfusion purpura, 7 to 10 days) occur after the patient has left the transfusion; fever, an unexplained fall in haemoglobin or a new positive antiglobulin test may be the signs.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Check the order, patient identity and container as local policy requires, and do not start if anything does not match.
- Inspect the product; if it looks abnormal, do not use it and contact the transfusion service.
- Use aseptic technique. The Circular states that entering the container in a way that breaks the system limits how long the component remains usable (expiry 4 hours after entry at room temperature, 24 hours if refrigerated), and that transfusion should be completed within 4 hours after the container is entered.
- Use a sterile filter designed to remove clots and aggregates (the Circular says generally a standard 150 to 260 micron filter), per local policy.
- **Do not add medications or solutions to the tubing used for blood or run them through it at the same time**, with the one exception the Circular names – 0.9% sodium chloride injection – unless FDA-approved for that use or documented as safe. **Never** use lactated Ringer's or other calcium-containing solutions in the same tubing as citrate-containing components.
- Warm blood only if clinically indicated and only with an FDA-cleared warming device (the Circular gives exchange or massive transfusion and cold-reactive antibodies as examples); rapid infusion of cold components can cause hypothermia.
- Observe the patient and record vital signs before, during and after; keep a higher index of suspicion in sedated or ventilated patients. **NICE NG24 (UK, updated February 2026) recommends monitoring the person's condition and vital signs before, during and after transfusion to detect acute reactions that may need immediate investigation and treatment (1.13.1), and observing people who are having or have had a transfusion in a suitable environment with staff able to monitor and manage acute reactions (1.13.2); it also suggests considering an electronic patient identification system (1.14.1).** NG24 gives no observation intervals or first-line actions after a reaction. The Open RN checklist (US textbook) adds: start at a slow rate per agency policy, remain with the patient during the first 15 minutes, take vital signs during that period, increase to the prescribed rate only if there is no sign of reaction so that the unit is finished within the 4-hour limit, observe respiratory status, skin and urine output periodically, inspect the site for infiltration, and continue to assess and monitor for 4 to 6 hours after the transfusion, teaching the patient and family the signs of a delayed reaction. These timings are the textbook's typical practice, not a guideline recommendation.
- **If a reaction is suspected: stop the transfusion immediately** and call for help. The Circular states the infusion should not be restarted unless the transfusion-service protocol permits it.
- For a suspected haemolytic reaction, the Circular states that the transfusion service laboratory must be notified immediately; patient and component identification, forms and labels reviewed promptly for errors; and a post-reaction blood sample, preferably from a site other than the transfusion access, sent with the implicated unit and the administration set.
- Report every adverse event, including suspected bacterial contamination, to the transfusion service under local protocol.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and work of breathing; urine colour and output; bleeding or oozing; skin (rash, urticaria, flushing); rhythm on the monitor during large or rapid transfusions (citrate and potassium effects); fluid balance; temperature of the patient during rapid infusion. Frequency is set by local policy.

| Anaphylactic | Hypotension, tachycardia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, severe dyspnoea, pulmonary or laryngeal oedema, bronchospasm or laryngospasm; rare | Emergency response; prompt intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) via the authorised anaphylaxis pathway (BSH 2023) and supportive care | Item 5 |

| TACO | New or worsening respiratory compromise with evidence suggesting circulatory overload, during or up to 12 hours after transfusion | At particular risk: cardiopulmonary or renal disease, the very young and elderly, chronic severe anaemia, positive fluid balance; small volumes can trigger it | Nonimmunologic, item 3 |
| Septic (bacterial) | High fever (rise of at least 2 °C), severe chills, hypotension or collapse during or soon after transfusion | Stop; blood cultures and examination of the bag and set; broad-spectrum antimicrobials and vasopressors if necessary are prescriber decisions | Nonimmunologic, item 2 |
| Hypothermia | Cold, rapid, large-volume transfusion can cause arrhythmia, cardiac arrest, worse coagulopathy | Consider an FDA-cleared warming device | Item 4 |
| Citrate effect | Low ionised calcium after rapid, large-volume transfusion, especially in liver disease or circulatory collapse; ventricular arrhythmia when given rapidly through central access | Ionised calcium measurement or ECG monitoring is more helpful than standard calcium | Item 5a |
| Other metabolic | Acid–base and potassium disturbances with rapid or large volumes | Particularly in pre-existing circulatory or metabolic problems | Item 5b |
| Delayed haemolytic | Unexplained fever, new positive antiglobulin test and unexplained fall in haemoglobin 2–14 days after | Patients previously sensitised | Delayed immunologic |

**Pulmonary reaction cross-check (Lifeblood and Canadian Blood Services).** TACO may become apparent during transfusion or up to 12 hours afterwards; TRALI typically develops during transfusion or within six hours of completion. These are recognition windows, not a reason to dismiss later deterioration. Do not assume fluid overload or give a diuretic reflexively. An ARDS risk factor, or previously stable mild ARDS, does not exclude TRALI type II; the clinical team assesses the preceding respiratory trend and alternative causes. Neither a positive fluid balance nor a single biomarker or blood-pressure reading establishes the diagnosis.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
During or soon after a transfusion: fever or rigors; hypotension or tachycardia; new breathlessness, hypoxaemia or cough; chest or back pain; rash, wheeze or swelling of the face or airway; dark urine; unexpected bleeding or oozing; agitation or a sense of doom; any change in a sedated patient's vital signs that has no other explanation; a bag that looks abnormal; a patient identity mismatch at any point.

## 12. Escalation criteria
Stop the transfusion immediately for any suspected reaction and assess the patient. Activate emergency response for compromised airway, breathing or circulation. Maintain venous access without flushing the implicated transfusion tubing; arrange new access if required under local protocol. Recheck patient and component identification, notify the clinical and transfusion teams, and retain the component and set for investigation as directed. Serious suspected reactions must not be restarted; any permitted resumption after a mild reaction requires authorised assessment and local policy.
**Major bleeding exception (BSH 2023).** During active major haemorrhage, new hypotension needs immediate senior assessment of ongoing blood loss versus a possible component reaction. Coordinate lifesaving resuscitation without waiting for laboratory results; the responsible team determines whether transfusion can continue or a different component is needed. Do not independently continue or restart a suspect component. Suspected anaphylaxis requires emergency help and prompt intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) through the authorised anaphylaxis pathway; do not delay urgent treatment for investigations.

SBAR — **S:** bed X, ventilated patient 20 minutes into a red-cell transfusion with a rise in temperature, a fall in blood pressure and oozing at the line sites. **B:** transfusion running as ordered, stopped by me now; maintaining venous access without flushing the implicated tubing. **A:** suspected acute transfusion reaction, possibly haemolytic or septic. **R:** please come now; I am stopping the transfusion, sending the bag and set with a post-reaction sample from another site and notifying the transfusion service.

## 13. Potential complications
Acute haemolysis with shock, DIC and kidney injury; anaphylaxis; TRALI; TACO; bacterial sepsis; hypothermia; citrate-related hypocalcaemia and arrhythmia; hyperkalaemia or hypokalaemia; delayed haemolysis; transfusion-transmitted infection; transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (rare, nearly always fatal as the Circular states).

## 14. Documentation
Order, product, unit number and expiry as local policy requires; identity check and who performed it; baseline and ongoing vital signs; start and finish times; volume given; filter and line used; any reaction with times, signs and actions; notification of the prescriber and transfusion service; items returned and samples sent; patient response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
A red-cell unit arrives at 09:00. Before starting, the nurse performs the identification check required by policy, inspects the bag for colour and clots, confirms a dedicated line with a sterile filter and no other infusions on that tubing except 0.9% sodium chloride, records baseline observations and starts at the ordered initial rate. She stays with the patient during the first 15 minutes as the Open RN checklist describes, records observations at the intervals local policy requires, and completes the unit within the time limit the Circular gives.


**Why the baseline matters.** Record pre-transfusion vital signs, oxygen device and flow, and any symptoms already present. If a symptom develops, compare it with that baseline and record the onset time relative to the start of the transfusion. This helps the team distinguish a new change from an existing abnormality.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 68-year-old woman in the ICU with heart failure and a positive fluid balance is receiving a transfusion. Forty minutes in she develops new breathlessness, her saturation falls and she has bibasal crackles. The nurse stops the transfusion, calls the doctor, and reports that the patient is fluid-positive with known cardiac disease. The team considers circulatory overload but also a lung-injury reaction and treats according to the clinical picture; the transfusion service is notified. The nurse documents times, vital signs and actions.


**What to include in the urgent handover.** Report when the transfusion started, when breathlessness began, the component and estimated volume infused, and how oxygen saturation and support changed. Include the blood pressure trend, recent fluid balance and response since stopping the transfusion. Fluid overload is a consideration in this case, and the clinical team needs to assess other causes of acute respiratory deterioration as well.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (application).** Immediately before starting a red-cell unit, the nurse notices that the bag has a cloudy appearance. What should she do?
A. Start the transfusion slowly, because the first part of the infusion is the safest time to see whether a problem appears.
B. Not use the unit, and contact the transfusion service, because the Circular says an abnormal appearance means it must not be used.
C. Warm the unit with the bedside warmer so that any particles dissolve before it is given to the patient.
D. Gently mix the unit and begin the transfusion only if the cloudiness has cleared after a few minutes.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The Circular states that if the container is not intact or the appearance is abnormal (including cloudiness) the unit must not be used and the transfusion service must follow up.
**Why not A:** Starting an abnormal unit risks a severe reaction, including septic reaction.
**Why not C:** Warming does not make an abnormal unit safe, and warming needs an FDA-cleared device and a clinical indication.
**Why not D:** Mixing is a step before use for a normal unit; it does not make an abnormal one acceptable.

**Q2 (safety).** A patient is receiving a transfusion through a line that is also being used for another infusion. Which statement agrees with the Circular?
A. A calcium-containing solution may run in the same tubing as long as the rate is kept low and the line is watched for clots.
B. Any compatible drug may be given in the same tubing as long as the nurse flushes it afterwards, because the flush clears the line.
C. A drug may be added to the unit if the pharmacist has verbally approved it at the bedside, because pharmacists know drug compatibility.
D. Only 0.9% sodium chloride may run with blood in the same tubing, unless FDA approval or documented safety exists for something else.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The Circular names 0.9% sodium chloride as the exception, and also warns against lactated Ringer's or other calcium-containing solutions with citrated components.
**Why not A:** Calcium-containing solutions should never be used with citrate-containing components.
**Why not B:** Simultaneous infusion through the same tubing is not allowed without approval or documentation.
**Why not C:** Verbal approval is not the documentation or FDA approval the Circular requires.

**Q3 (judgment).** A ventilated, sedated patient develops a rise in temperature, tachycardia and oozing at the line site during a red-cell transfusion. What should the nurse do first?
A. Stop transfusion, assess ABC and summon urgent help; retain the unit and notify the transfusion service.
B. Slow the transfusion and recheck the temperature in 15 minutes, to see if it settles.
C. Give an antipyretic and continue the transfusion, since fever is the most common reaction.
D. Wait for the end of the unit, so that the full volume is given, and then report the reaction.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Fever, tachycardia and bleeding may signal a serious hemolytic or septic reaction. Stop transfusion immediately, assess airway, breathing and circulation and activate urgent help. Lifeblood advises maintaining IV access without flushing the suspect line, using another access if needed. Recheck identity and component details, preserve the bag and set for investigation, notify the clinical team and transfusion service, and follow their sampling and reporting instructions. Do not restart this unit.
**Why not B:** Slowing leaves the patient exposed to a possibly life-threatening reaction.
**Why not C:** Fever can signal haemolytic or septic reaction; antipyretics treat symptoms only and need an order.
**Why not D:** Completing the unit delays recognition and treatment.

**Q4 (prioritization).** Four patients are receiving transfusions in the unit. Several observations require follow-up. Which finding indicates the most immediate threat to airway or circulation?
A. A patient with a mild itchy rash on the forearm and normal vital signs.
B. A patient with a temperature rise of 1 °C who feels well and has stable vital signs.
C. A patient with new severe breathlessness, hypotension and swelling of the lips.
D. A patient whose unit has been running for 3 hours and is nearly finished.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Severe respiratory compromise, hypotension and lip swelling suggest possible anaphylaxis. Stop the transfusion, activate emergency assistance and use the authorized anaphylaxis pathway without delaying urgent treatment; maintain oxygenation and circulatory support. Other severe reactions may overlap, so obtain specialist transfusion assessment rather than diagnosing from symptoms alone. Intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) is the first-line route for suspected anaphylaxis under the authorised emergency pathway; do not wait for confirmatory tests.
**Why not A:** An isolated mild urticarial reaction still requires assessment, monitoring and action according to local transfusion policy, but lacks the immediate airway/circulatory danger of option C. Do not assume all mild reactions require identical treatment or that restarting is always prohibited.
**Why not B:** Even an apparently mild fever developing during transfusion warrants stopping the transfusion for prompt assessment and notification under the reaction protocol; FNHTR is not confirmed by fever alone. Only after clinical evaluation may authorized staff decide whether restarting is appropriate. Severe airway and circulatory compromise in option C remains the highest immediate priority.
**Why not D:** Check the permitted administration time and local handling requirements, but an approaching time limit without new symptoms is not equivalent to acute airway or circulatory compromise. The cited Circular describes a four-hour limit after entry into the container.

**Q5 (understanding).** Which patient is at particular risk of transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO) according to the Circular?
A. A young adult with normal heart, lung and kidney function and a negative fluid balance.
B. An older adult with heart or kidney disease who already has a positive fluid balance.
C. A patient whose temperature fell during the transfusion because the unit was cold.
D. A patient who is anaesthetised and has no signs of volume overload or respiratory symptoms.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The Circular identifies underlying cardiopulmonary or renal disease, extremes of age and positive fluid balance as risk factors; even a small transfused volume may precipitate TACO in a vulnerable patient. Review baseline risk and monitor for new respiratory distress or overload; suspected TACO requires stopping the transfusion and urgent clinical evaluation, not an independent diagnosis based on risk alone.
**Why not A:** This patient has none of the listed risk factors.
**Why not C:** Cold units are a hypothermia risk, not a TACO risk.
**Why not D:** Anaesthesia is not on the list of TACO risk factors.

**Q6 (scope).** After a possible transfusion reaction, which decision requires authorized clinical and transfusion-service assessment rather than unilateral action by an untrained team member?
A. Stopping the transfusion when a reaction is suspected.
B. Keeping the bag and administration set for return to the transfusion service.
C. Deciding whether the transfusion can be restarted after a reaction.
D. Sending a post-reaction blood sample from a site other than the transfusion access.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Whether a transfusion can be continued or restarted depends on reaction severity, clinical assessment, and applicable transfusion-service protocol. Severe or life-threatening reactions require emergency treatment and the implicated unit must not be restarted. The bedside nurse must not independently resume a stopped transfusion without the required authorization; mild reactions may have a different authorized pathway after serious causes have been excluded.
**Why not A:** Stopping at once is a nursing action the Circular requires.
**Why not B:** Returning the unit and set is part of the nursing response.
**Why not D:** Collecting the post-reaction sample is part of the response the Circular describes.
