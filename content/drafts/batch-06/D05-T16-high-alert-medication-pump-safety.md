# High-alert medications and infusion pump safety
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. This draft explains the safety concept and the checks both sources describe; it contains no drug doses, concentrations or rates, and it is not a substitute for your unit's high-alert policy.

## 1. Overview
A high-alert medication is one that carries a heightened risk of significant patient harm **when used in error**. ISMP says such errors are not necessarily more frequent with these drugs; the consequences are worse. ISMP publishes a list of drugs and classes for acute care, built from error reports, the literature and a practitioner survey, and says organisations should use it to decide which drugs need special safeguards. Examples of categories on the 2024 list that ICU nurses meet daily include intravenous adrenergic agonists, insulin, opioids, antithrombotic agents, neuromuscular blocking agents and concentrated potassium chloride for injection; the list has many more entries and is not reproduced here. Infusion pumps are a second, separate hazard: the FDA states that performance problems have occurred across all types of external infusion pump and can cause over-infusion, under-infusion or delay in therapy. In the ICU the two overlap constantly, because many continuous infusions are high-alert drugs delivered through pumps.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Define a high-alert medication and explain why the label is about consequence, not error frequency.

4. Describe an independent double check and why ISMP says it is not always the best safeguard.
5. Describe what to do when a pump alarms unexpectedly or appears damaged, and how to report problems.
6. Separate nursing responsibilities from prescriber, pharmacist and biomedical-engineering responsibilities.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Critically ill patients receive many concentrated, titrated infusions at once. A programming slip, a wrong bag on the wrong channel or a pump fault can cause harm within minutes, and the patient may already be too unstable to tolerate it. The nurse is the last person to check what is going into the patient.

## 6. Clinical assessment
Before and during any high-risk infusion:
- **The order and the patient:** patient identity, allergies, the indication, and the order's drug, dose, route and rate against what is hung and programmed. The FDA page lists the "5 rights" (right patient, drug, dose, route and time) as the basic frame.
- **The pump:** which channel carries which drug (is the channel or the tubing labelled with the drug or fluid name?), is the drug library in use, are there active alerts or cautions, is the pump physically intact (doors, latches, casing, battery)?
- **The tubing and line:** is the tubing labelled at the point of entry, is the infusion going into the intended access, and is the line free of kinks, disconnections and clamps?
- **The patient's response:** the FDA page advises monitoring for signs of over- or under-infusion through other patient monitoring systems where applicable – it names cardiac monitoring, pulse oximetry, end-tidal CO₂ and glucose meters as examples.
- **A plan if the pump fails:** the FDA page says to have a back-up plan, including how to obtain a working pump and tubing quickly for high-acuity patients and how to handle high-risk infusions if no pump is available.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- **Verify programming** (right dosage, rate and volume to be infused) at four moments the FDA page singles out: at change of shift, when any change is made to the pump settings, when a new bag of medication or fluid is hung, and when new tubing is primed.
- **Label** pump channels and tubing at the port of entry with the drug or fluid name if the pump does not display it.
- **Use the drug library** where one exists, and respond promptly and carefully to alerts and cautions rather than silencing them.
- **Obtain an independent double check** of pump settings by a second clinician when the facility's policy requires it for high-risk drugs. The FDA page defines this as two clinicians checking separately, alone and apart from each other, then comparing results against the order. It names insulin, heparin, vasopressors, propofol, parenteral nutrition and morphine as examples of high-risk infusions. ISMP adds a caution: manual independent double checks are not always the optimal error-reduction strategy and may not be practical for every drug on the list; other strategies include standardising ordering, storage, preparation and administration, limiting access, auxiliary labels, decision support and automated alerts.
- **Consider a second method** of checking the expected volume infused (the FDA page gives a time-strip indicator or a burette as examples).
- **Use help** from clinical or biomedical engineering, unit super-users and the pump instructions when a pump misbehaves.
- **Take a faulty pump out of service:** the FDA page says to remove it from use, tag it with the specific problem and your contact information, and sequester it if it shows breakage or damage (including small chips or cracks), an unexplained alarm, or does not function as expected.
- **Report:** follow the facility's protocol for events where a pump may have caused or contributed to a death or serious injury, and report other concerns through the facility's system. The FDA encourages voluntary reports of pump problems.
- **Report medication errors and near misses** through your organisation's system; ISMP points readers to its national error-reporting programme.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Pump settings against the order at the four verification moments above; alerts and alarms; the patient's haemodynamics, rhythm, oxygen saturation, end-tidal CO₂ and blood glucose where relevant to the drug; infusion site; volume infused against expected; the interval for rechecks set by unit policy.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
An unexplained pump alarm; a pump that does not behave as expected or has visible damage; a channel with no label; a rate on the pump that differs from the order; a new bag hung without re-checking the pump; a change in the patient's rhythm, blood pressure, oxygenation, ventilation or glucose that fits over- or under-infusion; a drug library alert that is overridden without a stated reason; shift handover that does not include a pump check.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate when the pump setting does not match the order and the discrepancy cannot be resolved at the bedside, when the order itself looks unsafe, when a pump fails or alarms without explanation while a high-risk infusion is running, or when the patient's condition suggests a wrong dose or rate. For a failing pump with a high-risk infusion, the FDA page describes staying with and closely monitoring the patient while a colleague obtains a working pump.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, the pump on the vasoactive infusion has alarmed twice without a clear cause. **B:** infusion running as ordered at the last check; patient's blood pressure currently at target. **A:** possible pump fault; high-risk infusion. **R:** I am staying with the patient; please bring a replacement pump and tubing and inform the pharmacist and the nurse in charge.

## 13. Potential complications
Over-infusion and its drug-specific harm; under-infusion with loss of therapeutic effect (for example loss of blood-pressure control or sedation); delay in therapy; wrong-drug administration; free-flow; patient harm and its consequences for the nurse and team.

## 14. Documentation
Drug, concentration and rate as ordered and as programmed; time of each verification; who performed any independent double check; library used and alerts overridden with the reason; patient response; pump alarms and actions; faulty-pump tagging and incident report reference; communication with the prescriber or pharmacist.

## 15. Practical bedside example
A new bag of a high-risk infusion is hung at 02:00. The nurse reads the order, checks the label on the bag and on the tubing at the port of entry, programmes the pump from the drug library, and asks a second nurse to check the pump separately against the order before starting. After starting, she watches the patient's monitored values and records the check.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
At change of shift, the oncoming nurse sees that the rate on a pump differs from the chart. Instead of correcting it silently, she checks the order, confirms with the outgoing nurse, finds that the order was changed an hour earlier but the pump was not updated, corrects the pump with a second check, reports the near miss, and reassesses the patient.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (understanding).** What does the term "high-alert medication" mean in the ISMP list?
A. A drug that is involved in more medication errors than any other drug used in the unit.
B. A drug that may only be given when a doctor is physically present at the patient's bedside throughout the dose.
C. A drug with a heightened risk of significant harm when used in error, whatever the frequency of errors.
D. A drug that requires a pump with a built-in drug library for every single administration.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** ISMP defines high-alert drugs by the severity of harm when an error occurs and says errors may or may not be more common.
**Why not A:** ISMP says mistakes may or may not be more common with these drugs.
**Why not B:** The list is about safeguards, not a rule about doctors being present.
**Why not D:** A drug library is one possible safeguard, not part of the definition.

**Q2 (application).** A new bag of a high-risk infusion is hung on a pump that is already running. According to the FDA page, what should the nurse do?
A. Verify that the pump is programmed for the right dosage, rate and volume, because a new bag is one of the moments the page names.
B. Check only the label on the bag, because the programming was verified at the start of the shift.
C. Leave the settings as they are and recheck them at the next change of shift, to avoid interrupting the infusion that is running.
D. Ask the patient whether the new bag looks like the earlier one, and continue if the patient agrees.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The FDA page lists a new bag of medication or fluid, a settings change, new primed tubing and change of shift as moments to verify programming.
**Why not B:** A bag label does not confirm what is programmed on the pump.
**Why not C:** Waiting until the next shift leaves the new infusion unchecked.
**Why not D:** Patient recognition is not a verification method and is not valid for sedated patients.

**Q3 (judgment).** Two nurses are doing an independent double check of a vasoactive infusion. Which method matches the FDA page's description?
A. The first nurse reads out the settings and the second nurse says whether they agree.
B. Both nurses stand together at the pump and read the settings aloud in turn.
C. The second nurse signs the chart after the first nurse tells her that the settings are right.
D. Each nurse checks the settings alone against the order, and then they compare results.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The page describes two clinicians separately checking, alone and apart from each other, and then comparing results against the order.
**Why not A:** The second nurse would be influenced by what the first one reads out.
**Why not B:** Checking together is not independent.
**Why not C:** Signing on someone's word is not a check at all.

**Q4 (safety).** A pump alarms for no apparent reason during a high-risk infusion and then seems to behave normally. What is the safest action?
A. Silence it, document that the alarm cleared, and keep using the pump as it appears normal again.
B. Stay with the patient, get a working pump, and remove the pump from use and tag it for review.
C. Continue the infusion and report the alarm at the end of the shift so that the pump can be checked.
D. Switch the pump off and on again until the alarm no longer appears, then carry on with the infusion.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The FDA page says to remove a pump with an unexplained alarm from use, tag it with the problem and contact information, and to stay with a high-risk patient while a working pump is obtained.
**Why not A:** An unexplained alarm is a reason to remove the pump, whatever it does next.
**Why not C:** Delaying the report leaves a possibly faulty pump on a high-risk infusion.
**Why not D:** Repeated restarts hide the fault and may repeat the error.

**Q5 (understanding).** Why does ISMP caution against relying on manual independent double checks for every high-alert medication?
A. Double checks have been proven to cause more medication errors than they prevent in intensive care units.
B. The list only applies to oral medications, which are never suitable for a second person to check.
C. Pump libraries make all checks by a second clinician legally unnecessary in every hospital and country.
D. They are not always the optimal strategy and may not be practical for all drugs on the list.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The ISMP list introduction says independent double checks are not always the best safeguard and may be impractical for every high-alert medicine, so organisations should consider other safeguards, such as standardisation and limiting access, chosen by local policy.
**Why not A:** ISMP does not say that double checks cause errors.
**Why not B:** The list includes intravenous and other routes.
**Why not C:** Neither source says that checks become unnecessary; policy decides.

**Q6 (scope).** Which decision belongs to the prescriber, with pharmacist review, rather than to the bedside nurse?
A. Choosing the drug, concentration and dose range for a new vasoactive infusion.
B. Labelling the pump channel and the tubing at the port of entry to the patient's line.
C. Verifying pump programming against the order when a new bag is hung.
D. Removing a damaged pump from use and recording the problem.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Selecting the drug, concentration and dose is a prescribing decision with pharmacist input.
**Why not B:** The FDA page lists labelling as a clinician strategy.
**Why not C:** Verification at the bedside is a nursing check.
**Why not D:** The FDA page lists removal and tagging as a clinician action.
