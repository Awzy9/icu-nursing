# Nursing scope of practice – working within your legal and professional boundaries
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Scope of practice is set by each country's or state's law and regulator: this draft teaches the principles and the US examples in the sources read, not your local rules.

## 1. Overview
"Scope of practice" is the range of activities a nurse is legally permitted, educated and competent to perform. It is defined by law and regulators, shaped by professional standards and employer policy, and applied through individual competence. In ICU, where nurses titrate infusions, interpret monitoring and work with protocols, knowing the boundary between independent nursing actions, delegated or protocol-based actions, and prescriber decisions is a core safety skill.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Name the layers that define and limit practice: law/regulator, professional standards, employer policy, competence.
2. Explain why scope varies between countries and states and where to find the rules that apply to you.
3. Describe accountability, delegation and the chain of command at the level described in the sources.
4. Recognise situations that fall outside your competence and respond safely.
5. Use scope labels (independent nursing, collaborative, needs order/protocol, prescriber decision) consistently.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Many ICU tasks sit on a boundary (adjusting oxygen or sedation within a protocol, taking verbal orders, defibrillating, removing lines). Acting outside scope can harm patients and lead to discipline; failing to act inside scope (for example not escalating) can also be negligent.

## 6a. Regulator and professional statements added in Phase 3 (paraphrased)
- **UK – NMC Code (applies to NMC registrants; all standards apply within the registrant's professional scope of practice):** recognise and work within the limits of your competence (13): accurately identify, observe and assess signs of normal or worsening physical and mental health (13.1); make a timely referral to another practitioner when any action, care or treatment is required (13.2); ask for help from a suitably qualified and experienced professional for any action or procedure beyond your competence (13.3); take account of your own and others' safety (13.4); and complete the necessary training before carrying out a new role (13.5). Delegation (11): only delegate tasks within the other person's scope of competence and make sure they understand the instructions (11.1); make sure those you delegate to are adequately supervised and supported (11.2); and confirm that the outcome meets the required standard (11.3). Raise and, if necessary, escalate concerns about safety (16.1). Preserve safety means working within the limits of your competence and raising concerns immediately when patient or public safety is at risk.
- **US – NCSBN/ANA National Guidelines for Nursing Delegation (2019):** the "Five Rights of Delegation" – right task, right circumstance (the patient's condition must be stable; if it changes, delegation must be reconsidered), right person, right directions and communication, and right supervision and evaluation; the licensed nurse remains responsible for monitoring the delegated activity. State boards of nursing set the legal rules.
- **Limits:** these two regulators differ, neither lists which ICU tasks (for example titration, verbal orders, line removal) are within scope for a given role, and employers' policies and local law decide. Other countries' regulators (for example in the Middle East, Europe or Asia) were not read.

## 6. Clinical assessment (assessing a task against scope)
Before an unfamiliar or high-risk task ask:
- **Law and regulator:** is this task permitted for my licence/registration category in my jurisdiction? Where do I find the regulator's standards?
- **Authority:** is there an order, protocol or delegation that covers it, and is it clear who decided?
- **Competence:** have I been educated and assessed for this, and do I do it often enough to be safe?
- **Employer policy:** does local policy allow it in this unit?
- **Patient safety:** could this harm the patient, and who must be consulted first?
- **Documentation:** can I record what I did, why and who authorised it?

## 7. Nursing responsibilities (as described in the sources read)
- Follow the regulations of the body that licenses you; in the US sources this is the state Nurse Practice Act, which defines scope and can lead to licence revocation if breached.
- Work to professional standards and codes of ethics (the sources describe the ANA Scope and Standards and Code of Ethics for the US; the ICN definition describes the nurse as regulated to practise on the basis of established standards of practice and ethical codes, working autonomously and collaboratively, advocating and using evidence-informed decision-making).
- Know that employers, accreditation and safety bodies add expectations (for example the Joint Commission's safety goals include identifying patients correctly, improving staff communication, using medicines safely, using alarms safely and preventing infection).
- Accept delegated acts only where there are protocols or orders and only if you are competent; consult a provider if a delegated act may harm a patient; delegate only tasks matching the education and demonstrated ability of the person supervised, and supervise and evaluate (the chapter quotes Wisconsin's rules as an example; other places differ).
- Act within the chain of command: communicate concerns through the agency's reporting line (for example charge nurse to supervisor to director), seeking help when a situation exceeds your competence; students consult their instructor first.
- Recognise duties that can create liability: failure to assess, insufficient monitoring, failure to communicate or document ("if an assessment or action was not documented, it is considered not done" in US courts), failure to notify the provider of a change in condition, failure to follow protocols.
- Report errors and near misses and support a safety culture.
- Protect confidentiality.

## 7b. Nursing role in palliative and end-of-life ICU care

Palliative care addresses symptoms, communication and patient goals and may accompany active organ support; it is not synonymous with withdrawal of treatment. A resuscitation limitation does not, by itself, authorize withholding other indicated care. Verify the current documented orders and their exact scope under local law and policy. If uncertain or orders conflict, clarify urgently with the responsible team rather than independently changing treatment.

Support the patient in expressing preferences, and facilitate a trained interpreter, family meeting or spiritual support when wanted. For a patient unable to decide, follow the institutional process for establishing the lawful surrogate; a relative's presence alone does not establish legal authority. Escalate disagreement, uncertainty or unmet symptom needs to senior clinicians and available palliative or ethics specialists.

Where the authorized team agrees on a time-limited trial of organ support, document the agreed purpose, reassessment date, patient-centered outcomes and responsible clinician. Report changes promptly; the end of a trial does not automatically authorize stopping treatment. ESICM 2024 discusses this approach but outcome evidence is limited.

When an authorized end-of-life plan is in place, assess distress, pain, dyspnea, agitation and comfort; deliver prescribed symptom relief, dignity, mouth care and family support and reassess response. SCCM 2025 supports protocol-guided symptom management around withdrawal of life support. This is not a withdrawal or medication-dosing protocol: do not independently stop life-sustaining therapies. National law, local policy and trained clinicians govern these actions.

## 7c. Workload, competency and speaking up

Safe assignments reflect the patient's current needs, the required nursing work and available staff competence, not only the number of beds or diagnostic labels. Consider simultaneous unstable patients, time-critical medication titration, required surveillance, procedures, transfers and access to experienced help. Reassess when acuity changes. The 2024 AACN adult critical care staffing standards summary supports workload-based assignments and staff participation in staffing decisions; it does not establish a universal ratio for every jurisdiction.

If competing demands make essential care unsafe, inform the charge nurse promptly, identify the immediate risks and request a competent redistribution of work or additional support. Continue urgent care within competence and arrange explicit responsibility for any delegated task. Do not silently omit required observations or invent a completed check. Use the local chain of escalation if unresolved, and record the facts and response. A 2026 review of independent double-checking identifies system constraints, including staff availability and interruptions; individual vigilance cannot substitute for a usable safety system.

Fictional example: two patients require simultaneous time-critical care while a high-risk medication verification is due. Escalate the competing needs immediately, ask a qualified colleague to assume an identified task, and confirm who is responsible. Reassess both patients' needs and follow the local staffing and emergency policy; do not leave an unstable patient without appropriate coverage.

**Clinical judgment and accountability (Open RN Nursing Fundamentals, chapter 4).** Before carrying out a planned intervention, assess whether the patient's condition has changed and whether the intervention remains appropriate. Clarify an unsafe or unclear order with the responsible clinician, document the findings and agreed plan, and reassess the response. In an emergency, activate the authorized escalation pathway without waiting for routine clarification. The textbook describes US nursing practice; authority to delegate, modify or withhold an intervention depends on local law, competence and institutional policy. No additional legal authority is conferred by this lesson.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Being asked to perform a task you have not been trained for; an unclear or verbal order you cannot verify; pressure to "just do it" or to document something not done; unclear delegation ("someone said it was fine"); doing a task because "we always do"; a policy that conflicts with your regulator's standards; fatigue or staffing shortages that make safe practice difficult.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Declining or pausing a task you are not competent or authorised to do, and escalating | Independent nursing (professional duty) |
| Carrying out tasks under a written protocol or order within competence | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Delegating tasks to appropriately trained staff and supervising | Per regulator and employer rules |
| Diagnosing and prescribing | Prescriber (or advanced practice) decision; depends on jurisdiction |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate tasks exceeding competence or authorisation, unclear or conflicting protocols, unsafe orders, suspected errors or inadequate supervision. Verify applicable national regulation, professional registration, employer credentialing and local protocols; this educational draft does not grant authority. A protocol-directed adjustment or nurse-led action is distinct from independent prescribing and is permitted only where authorised. During deterioration, summon qualified help immediately and provide emergency measures within your competence; do not abandon the patient. Document concerns and responses.
SBAR — **S:** I have been asked to [task] and I am not competent or authorised to do this. **B:** my training/competency record shows [x]; the patient is [state]. **A:** it may cause harm or fall outside my scope. **R:** please arrange someone qualified or supervision and confirm the order and policy.

## 13. Potential complications
Patient harm; disciplinary action or loss of licence; litigation; moral distress; team conflict; incident under-reporting.

## 14. Documentation
What was ordered or delegated and by whom; what you did and when; competence or supervision arrangements; escalation and responses; reasons for declining a task; incident reports.

## 15. Practical bedside example
A colleague asks you to change a ventilator setting because "the respiratory therapist is busy". You check whether your regulator, your employer policy and a written protocol allow nurses to change that setting, whether you have been assessed, and who authorised it. If not, you explain, escalate through the chain of command, and document.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A senior colleague asks you to start a task that you have never been trained to do, saying "it is quick and I will take the blame". What is the best response?
A. Do it, because the colleague has accepted the responsibility for the outcome and is more senior than you.
B. Do it but chart that the colleague instructed you to, so that the record is clear about who decided.
C. Explain it is outside your competence, ask for supervision or a qualified colleague, and escalate if needed.
D. Refuse without any explanation and leave the task for the next shift, since it is not on your list.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Work within demonstrated competence and seek qualified assistance. The NMC Code also requires prompt action when safety is threatened. In an emergency summon help and provide the immediate measures for which you are trained; a senior colleague accepting responsibility does not extend your legal authority.
**Why not A:** Accountability cannot be transferred to a colleague.
**Why not B:** Documentation does not make an unsafe act safe.
**Why not D:** Refusing without escalation leaves the patient's need unmet.

**Q2 (judgment).** A family asks the bedside nurse to stop an ongoing prescribed therapy because a resuscitation-limitation order has just been entered. The order addresses CPR but does not mention the therapy. What is the appropriate response?
A. Continue indicated care while urgently clarifying the scope of the order with the team.
B. Pause the therapy while the family and prescriber discuss whether it is still needed.
C. Continue the therapy until the next routine round without communicating the family's concern.
D. Ask the family to sign an instruction authorizing the nurse to stop the therapy.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** A resuscitation limitation does not by itself authorize withdrawal of other treatment. Verify the current orders and clarify the patient's goals and concerns with the responsible team. Applicable law, authorized decisions and local policy govern treatment limitations; continue symptom assessment and support.
**Why not B:** Pausing a prescribed therapy without an authorized plan may cause harm and exceeds what the documented CPR limitation establishes.
**Why not C:** Continuing care is appropriate while clarification is sought, but delaying communication ignores the family's concern.
**Why not D:** A signature alone does not establish lawful surrogate authority or an authorized clinical order.

**Q3 (understanding).** Which duty is listed in the Open RN legal chapter as an example of negligent practice?
A. Taking the patient's vital signs at the interval ordered by the plan of care and recording them on the chart.
B. Reporting a medication error through the incident system so that the agency can investigate the system causes.
C. Asking a more experienced nurse for supervision before performing a procedure for the first time.
D. Failing to tell the provider about a significant change in the patient's condition, and not documenting it.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The chapter identifies failure to report important changes or document care as an example of a breach of professional duty. Whether conduct constitutes legal negligence depends on the facts and applicable law; a teaching example is not a court finding.
**Why not A:** Taking vital signs as ordered is expected practice, not a breach.
**Why not B:** Reporting errors supports safety culture and is not a breach.
**Why not C:** Seeking supervision for a first-time procedure is appropriate practice.

**Q4 (prioritization).** Two unstable ICU patients require simultaneous time-critical care. A third patient is due a high-risk infusion check that requires a second competent nurse. The assigned nurse cannot safely complete all three tasks at once. What is the best approach?
A. Complete the infusion check without a second nurse to avoid a delay, then report the staffing issue.
B. Escalate competing needs, obtain competent help and explicitly confirm task responsibility.
C. Ask an available staff member to assume the infusion check without checking their competence.
D. Finish the most familiar task first and hand over the remaining work at the next round.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Escalate immediately to the charge nurse or emergency team as appropriate. Match delegated tasks to demonstrated competence, specify responsibility and supervision, and ensure unstable patients are not left without appropriate coverage. Required verification and emergency care follow the authorized local pathway.
**Why not A:** Workload does not authorize bypassing required independent verification or create evidence that a check occurred.
**Why not C:** Availability does not establish competence; delegation requires appropriate authorization and supervision.
**Why not D:** Personal familiarity is not a reliable measure of clinical urgency; waiting may leave deterioration or safety-critical tasks unaddressed.

**Q5 (safety).** A student nurse asks why legal reviews of negligence place so much weight on documentation. Which explanation is correct?
A. Documentation is needed for billing and has no bearing on how care is judged when a case is reviewed in law.
B. Missing records can undermine evidence of care; accurate and timely documentation supports accountability.
C. Charts must be as long as possible to show effort, because courts count the number of words that were recorded.
D. Physicians' notes count as evidence, so nursing documentation is optional once the orders have been entered.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The chapter's teaching maxim is a reminder to document care, not a universal legal presumption. Missing documentation is not automatic proof of negligence or proof that care was omitted. Record actual findings, actions, times and escalation accurately using the authorized correction and late-entry procedure. The UK NMC Code section 10 emphasizes clear and accurate records.
**Why not A:** The purpose is accurate clinical and legal records.
**Why not C:** Length is not required; accuracy is.
**Why not D:** Nursing documentation counts as part of the record.

**Q6 (scope).** Which statement best distinguishes nursing scope from prescriber decisions?
A. Nurses may prescribe any treatment they think necessary if the patient becomes unstable and no prescriber is near.
B. Nurses decide diagnoses and treatments, and doctors only supervise the nurses' decisions on the unit.
C. Assess and act within competence; diagnosis and prescribing require the applicable professional authority.
D. Nurses may change any order if they feel the dose is too high, and tell the prescriber later if time allows.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Scope depends on local law, regulation, credentialing and competence. Nurses make nursing assessments and judgments; appropriately authorized advanced-practice nurses or nurse prescribers may also diagnose and prescribe within their legal scope. Ordinary bedside nurses may implement valid protocols but must clarify unsafe orders and escalate urgent concerns.
**Why not A:** Instability does not extend legal scope.
**Why not B:** The reverse of the described division.
**Why not D:** Question unsafe orders promptly and follow the authorized withholding or emergency protocol. Do not independently rewrite prescriptions without authority.
