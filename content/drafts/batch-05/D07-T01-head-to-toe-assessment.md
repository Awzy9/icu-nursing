# Systematic head-to-toe assessment and documentation
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Based on a general inpatient checklist; ICU adaptation items are cross-references, not sourced here.

## 1. Overview
A head-to-toe assessment is a systematic, organised examination that gives a baseline and detects change. The Open RN checklist describes it as a guide for a routine, general, daily assessment: begin with safety and a primary survey, take a general survey, then assess body systems in a consistent order, and document findings and report unanticipated ones. Focused assessments follow abnormal findings and specialty-unit guidance.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe the order of a systematic assessment and the safety steps around it.
2. Perform a primary survey (airway, breathing, circulation, mental status) before the detailed assessment.
3. Assess neuromuscular, head/neck/eye/ear, cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal and integumentary systems with subjective questions and objective findings.
4. Identify when to stop and seek help, and when a focused assessment is needed.
5. Document findings accurately and report unexpected findings per policy.
6. Recognise what this checklist does not cover for ICU patients.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Critical illness changes quickly and many devices and treatments hide or cause problems. A consistent head-to-toe routine makes subtle changes visible and gives comparable records between nurses and shifts. The sources read give the general structure only; ICU-specific monitoring and devices need their own topics.

## 6. Clinical assessment (the structure in the Open RN checklist)
**Preparation and safety:** gather equipment (stethoscope, penlight, watch with second hand, gloves, hand sanitiser, wound measurement tool); hand hygiene and clean stethoscope; check the room for transmission-based precautions; introduce yourself and explain; ensure privacy and dignity; identify the patient using two identifiers.
**Primary survey (is the patient stable?):** airway open, need for suctioning; breathing normal; circulation – abnormal colour or moisture such as cyanosis or diaphoresis; mental status – responsive and alert. Get emergency help if needed.
**General survey:** appearance, behaviour, mood, mobility, communication, overall nutrition and fluid status; address needs first (toileting, glasses, hearing aids); explore the chief concern (PQRSTU); take or review vital signs; assess for pain or discomfort and use a comprehensive pain assessment if present.
**Neuromuscular:** headache, dizziness, weakness, numbness, tingling, tremor, balance, falls, swallowing; level of consciousness and orientation; pupils (PERRLA); hand grasps; arm and leg strength and resistance; sensation; symmetry of face, movement and speech; fall-risk assessment per policy; report unanticipated findings.
**Head, neck, eye, ear:** teeth, gums, dentures, glasses, hearing aids; vision and hearing problems; inspect eyes, ears, mouth (lesions, tongue, moisture), swallowing of saliva; lymph nodes per policy.
**Cardiovascular:** chest pain, breathlessness, oedema, palpitations, calf pain; pallor or cyanosis of face, lips, extremities; jugular venous distension; colour, warmth and sensation of limbs; oedema and signs of deep vein thrombosis; compare radial, brachial, dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses; capillary refill; auscultate the five areas of the heart for rate and rhythm, S1 and S2 and extra sounds; apical pulse for one minute.
**Respiratory:** shortness of breath, cough (dry or productive); inspect restlessness, anxiety or confusion, breathing pattern (rate, rhythm, effort, depth), nasal flaring, accessory muscles, pursed-lip breathing, skin colour, tracheal position, chest symmetry; auscultate anterior and posterior lung fields comparing sides and noting crackles, wheeze, stridor, pleural rub; record oxygen device, flow or FiO₂; look for skin breakdown from oxygen devices; document tracheostomy site and sputum if present.
**Abdomen and elimination:** pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation, appetite, swallowing, last bowel movement, stool changes, urinary problems; inspect contour and distension; auscultate bowel sounds in four quadrants for a minute; palpate lightly for tenderness and masses; weight trend and 24-hour intake and output; enteral tube insertion site, placement (per agency policy) and feeding amounts; urinary catheter output, urine characteristics and continuing need; ostomy stoma and output.
**Integumentary:** skin concerns; colour (pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, erythema, bruising), moisture and turgor; IV site (redness, warmth, tenderness, induration) and infusions; pressure points (behind ears, occiput, elbows, sacrum, heels) and staging of any pressure injury; wounds; oedema grading; blanching of erythema.
**Finishing:** comfort, call light within reach, bed low and locked, side rails per policy, risk-free room, remove PPE, hand hygiene and clean the stethoscope, document and report.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Follow the same order each time so that changes are not missed, and adapt the techniques to age and condition.
- Start with safety and the primary survey; stop and call for help if the patient is unstable.
- Perform focused assessments for abnormal findings, according to unit guidelines.
- Document objectively and report unanticipated findings per policy.
- In ICU, add the unit's monitoring, device and infusion checks; see the related topics (ventilator values, arterial line, ETT, urine output and fluid balance, GCS, pain, skin, delirium).

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Frequency of full and focused assessments follows unit policy and patient condition (the checklist is for a routine daily assessment). The pressure-injury guide advises a comprehensive head-to-toe skin and tissue assessment soon after admission or transfer, as part of every risk assessment, periodically, when the condition worsens, at care transitions and before discharge, and inspection of visible bony areas at each repositioning.

## 9. Important findings – what to notice and report
| System | Findings that need action or escalation (from the checklist's wording: unanticipated or abnormal findings) |
|---|---|
| Primary survey | Obstructed airway or need for suctioning; abnormal breathing; cyanosis or diaphoresis; unresponsiveness – obtain emergency help |
| Neurological | New asymmetry of face, movement or speech; new weakness, numbness, confusion, difficulty swallowing |
| Cardiovascular | Absent or unequal pulses, abnormal colour, irregular rhythm or extra sounds, new oedema or signs of deep vein thrombosis |
| Respiratory | Increased work of breathing, new adventitious sounds, cyanosis, skin breakdown from oxygen devices |
| Abdominal | Distension, tenderness, vomiting, absent bowel pattern, abnormal catheter urine or stoma output |
| Skin | Pressure injury, wound or IV-site infection signs, new rash, unexplained bruising |
| Pain | Pain or discomfort needing a comprehensive pain assessment |
The checklist does not give numerical thresholds; use local early-warning and escalation policy.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Any abnormality in the primary survey; sudden change from the previous assessment; unexplained pain; new neurological findings; reduced perfusion; breathing difficulty; skin breakdown; signs of infection at devices; patient concern.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Performing, interpreting at nursing level, and documenting the assessment; reporting findings; focused follow-up assessments | Independent nursing |
| Starting protocol-driven responses to findings (oxygen titration, repositioning, wound care per protocol) | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Diagnosing causes, ordering tests and treatments | Prescriber decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Interrupt the routine head-to-toe examination for any immediate threat identified during the primary survey. Assess and address life-threatening abnormalities within scope while summoning emergency help; reassess the response before resuming the routine examination. New focal neurological signs require immediate stroke or neurological emergency pathway activation, documentation of last known well, and urgent assessment rather than waiting for the next scheduled neurological check. Check point-of-care blood glucose promptly and follow the authorised hypoglycaemia pathway if needed, in parallel with stroke escalation; neither glucose testing nor improvement in one finding should independently cancel specialist assessment. NICE NG128 and the Canadian Stroke Best Practices emergency assessment recommendations support excluding hypoglycaemia as a stroke mimic (targeted recommendation checks, not a complete stroke protocol).
SBAR — **S:** bed X, new weakness in the left arm and facial droop found during the neurological assessment. **B:** day 3 after surgery, assessed at 08:00 with normal findings at 04:00. **A:** possible acute neurological event. **R:** activate the stroke pathway now; last known well was 04:00, and symptoms were first identified at 08:00. I will continue emergency assessment and monitoring, including prompt point-of-care glucose measurement without delaying the stroke response.

## 13. Potential complications of omission
Missed deterioration, unrecognised pressure injuries and device problems, poor handover, duplicated or missed care.

## 14. Documentation
Time; patient identification method; primary-survey result; vital signs and pain; system-by-system findings in objective terms; oxygen device and flow; devices and sites; skin findings; focused assessments; abnormal findings reported (who, when, response); safety measures at the end.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At the start of the shift you introduce yourself, identify the patient with two identifiers, check the airway, breathing, circulation and responsiveness, then work from head to toe in the same order: neurological, head and neck, cardiovascular, respiratory, abdomen, skin. You record device settings and sites, note unexpected findings, tell the nurse in charge about a new swollen calf, and document.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 70-year-old woman on day 5 after surgery is assessed head to toe. The nurse finds a new swollen, tender left calf, a heart rate of 108, and a red sacral area that does not blanch. She reports the calf and pulse promptly, offloads the sacrum, records the findings and asks for review.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (understanding).** At the start of a head-to-toe assessment, which step comes first according to the checklist?
A. Safety steps and a primary survey of airway, breathing, circulation and mental status, with emergency help if needed.
B. Auscultating bowel sounds in all four quadrants for a full minute before anything else is examined or the patient is approached.
C. Measuring the weight trend and the 24-hour intake and output totals before approaching the patient.
D. Staging any pressure injury found on the sacrum or heels, because skin findings are the most urgent.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The checklist begins with safety steps and a primary survey to ensure medical stability.
**Why not B:** Abdominal assessment belongs to the later system-by-system stage.
**Why not C:** Weight and balance review come after the primary survey.
**Why not D:** Skin staging is part of the integumentary assessment, later in the sequence.

**Q2 (application).** A nurse is carrying out the cardiovascular part of a head-to-toe assessment. Which technique matches the checklist?
A. Listen over one area of the chest for ten seconds and record the rate, then move on to the respiratory system.
B. Palpate a single pulse on one limb, then record that the pulses are present and move on to the next system.
C. Check the pulses on the dominant side only, because the other side will be the same in nearly every patient.
D. Compare pulses on both sides and take an apical pulse for one minute, listening in the five auscultation areas.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The checklist directs comparing pulses on both sides and taking an apical pulse for one minute across the five auscultation areas. The other options are too brief, too limited or ignore asymmetry, which is a key finding.
**Why not A:** Too brief and too limited.
**Why not B:** One pulse does not compare perfusion side to side.
**Why not C:** Asymmetry is a key finding and requires both sides.

**Q3 (judgment).** While assessing a patient, the nurse finds a new droop of one side of the face and weakness in one arm. What should the nurse do?
A. Record the finding and reassess at the next routine round, since the patient is otherwise comfortable.
B. Obtain emergency assistance and report the finding immediately according to the agency policy.
C. Wait for the patient's family to confirm that this is unusual before telling anyone else on the team.
D. Complete the rest of the assessment first and then mention the finding at the end-of-shift handover.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The checklist directs the nurse to note unanticipated neurological findings and obtain emergency assistance as needed.
**Why not A:** Delay risks harm to the patient.
**Why not C:** Family confirmation is not required before reporting.
**Why not D:** Handover is too late for a possible acute neurological event.

**Q4 (scope).** Which statement about the Open RN checklist is accurate?
A. It is a validated ICU assessment standard for critical care and defines the frequency of ICU assessments for each patient.
B. It sets numeric thresholds for escalation in every body system and for every group of patients, including children and pregnant patients.
C. It guides a routine, general, daily assessment by an entry-level nurse; focused and specialty assessments follow unit guidelines.
D. It replaces the unit's monitoring and early-warning policies when a patient is in a critical care area or an isolation room.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The checklist presents itself as a routine, general, daily assessment for an entry-level nurse. It does not replace focused or specialty assessments, which follow unit guidelines, and it is not an ICU-specific standard.
**Why not A:** The checklist is a general inpatient tool, not an ICU-specific standard, so this does not follow.
**Why not B:** It does not give numeric thresholds.
**Why not D:** It supplements, not replaces, local policies.

**Q5 (documentation).** A nurse is documenting the respiratory assessment of a patient on oxygen. Which entry best documents the oxygen device?
A. "Patient on oxygen", because the equipment itself is visible to the next nurse who enters the room.
B. "Oxygen on, patient comfortable", because a short note is quicker and the saturation is already charted.
C. The device name and the flow rate or FiO₂, with the condition of the skin under the device.
D. Only the saturation reading, since the device and the flow can be seen on the equipment.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The checklist directs documentation of the device and flow or FiO₂ and a check for skin breakdown.
**Why not A:** Too vague to reproduce the treatment.
**Why not B:** Gives no device or rate and no objective data.
**Why not D:** The device and rate are needed to interpret the saturation.

**Q6 (understanding).** At the end of an assessment, which actions does the checklist list before leaving the room?
A. Leave the bed raised for convenience and move the call light out of reach so that the patient can rest.
B. Call light in reach, bed low and locked, rails per policy, risk-free room, PPE removed, hand hygiene.
C. Document the findings later at the end of the shift, whatever the time, and leave the room as it is.
D. Move the patient's belongings away from the bedside to clear space, including the call light and drink.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** These safety measures are listed at the end of the checklist.
**Why not A:** A raised bed and unreachable call light are unsafe.
**Why not C:** Documentation should follow the assessment and abnormal findings be reported per policy.
**Why not D:** Needed items such as the call light should remain within reach.
