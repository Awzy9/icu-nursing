# Sedation monitoring and targeted light sedation in ventilated adults
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. The scale definitions themselves are not taught here; this draft covers monitoring principles and safety.

## 1. Overview
Most mechanically ventilated adults are managed with an analgesia-first, light-sedation approach with regular assessment using a validated sedation scale and a documented target. The nurse performs the assessments, titrates within a protocol when authorised, carries out daily sedation interruptions (spontaneous awakening trials) when ordered, watches for over-sedation, agitation and drug effects, and reports. Choosing sedatives and targets is a prescriber decision.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Explain the aim of light sedation and what SCCM says about nurse-protocolised and interruption-based approaches.
2. State what "awake" means in the SCCM definition (scale ranges, objective actions).
3. Describe the assessment-driven approach: pain first, then sedation, with validated tools.
4. Recognise over-sedation, agitation and drug-related risks and escalate.
5. Describe cautions about physical restraints and delirium.
6. Separate nursing, protocol-based and prescriber decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Excess sedation prolongs ventilation, hides neurological change and increases delirium risk; inadequate sedation causes distress, patient–ventilator problems and unplanned device removal. The nurse is the person who assesses sedation depth most often.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Sedation depth:** use the unit's validated scale at the frequency set by protocol and before and after sedation changes; compare with the written target. SCCM's definition of an awake, arousable patient during a daily interruption uses objective actions – opening the eyes to voice, following simple commands – and scale ranges (Sedation-Agitation Scale 4–7; Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale −1 to +1).
- **Pain first:** SCCM good-practice statement: manage pain on routine assessment and treat it before considering a sedative; use a protocol-based, stepwise approach (see D07-T03).
- **Agitation causes:** pain, hypoxia, delirium, withdrawal, full bladder, device discomfort, ventilator asynchrony, sleep loss, anxiety; delirium screen with a validated tool (see D03-T10).
- **Objective sedation monitoring:** SCCM states that bispectral index monitoring appears best suited to sedative titration during deep sedation or neuromuscular blockade and may help when a sedation scale cannot be used.
- **Safety:** airway and breathing, haemodynamics, temperature, signs of drug effects, skin and pressure areas, restraint use and checks.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Assess pain and sedation at the ordered frequency with validated tools; record the target and the actual score; report persistent deviation.
- Where a protocol allows, titrate sedatives to the prescribed target – SCCM describes "nurse-protocolised targeted sedation" as an established protocol implemented by nurses at the bedside to determine sedative choices and titrate to prescription-targeted scores.
- Perform the daily sedation interruption or spontaneous awakening trial when ordered and the patient is suitable, with the team's safety checks: SCCM defines it as a period each day when sedative medication is stopped so the patient can wake up and show arousal by objective actions; watch for agitation, distress, desaturation or haemodynamic change and follow the protocol for stopping or restarting.
- Reassess after every dose change and procedure; document.
- Keep safety first when the patient is agitated: look for and treat causes, protect lines and tubes, call for help; use physical restraints only under policy, as a last resort, with regular checks (SCCM notes restraints are frequently used despite no studies showing efficacy and with safety concerns such as unplanned extubation and greater agitation).
- Avoid disturbing sleep unnecessarily: use noise and light reduction strategies (SCCM suggestion).
- Support mobilisation when stable (SCCM: vasoactive infusions or mechanical ventilation are not barriers if the patient is otherwise stable).

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Sedation scale score and target; pain score; delirium screen; respiratory rate, SpO₂, ETCO₂ and ventilator synchrony; heart rate, blood pressure; temperature; urine output; infusion type and rate as ordered; time and result of daily interruptions; skin under devices and restraints; hours awake.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Over-sedation (cannot be roused, no response to voice, respiratory depression, loss of cough); sudden agitation with desaturation or rising pressures; ventilator asynchrony; sedation target not met for hours; rapid dose escalation; new neurological signs hidden by sedation; pressure injury; restraint injury; withdrawal signs.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Assessing pain, sedation and delirium; documenting; safety measures; non-drug comfort; reporting; positioning | Independent nursing |
| Titrating within the nurse-protocolised target; performing the ordered daily interruption; restraint use per policy | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Choosing sedatives, doses and targets; starting neuromuscular blockade; antipsychotics or other drugs for agitation | Prescriber / pharmacist decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate for: unarousable patient or respiratory depression; agitation not controlled after treating causes; failure of the awakening trial; sedation target repeatedly missed; need for increasing doses; suspected withdrawal or delirium; restraint-related injury.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, target is light sedation but the patient is deeply sedated and unresponsive to voice. **B:** day 3 ventilated, infusion running at [rate], pain score [x], no recent boluses. **A:** over-sedation; neurological change cannot be assessed. **R:** please review the sedation plan now; do you want a trial of interruption?

## 13. Potential complications
Prolonged ventilation, delirium, pressure injury, immobility and weakness, withdrawal, unplanned extubation, hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, sleep disruption, distress and later psychological problems.

## 14. Documentation
Target and actual sedation score; scale used; pain score; infusion and bolus doses as given; daily interruption (time, result, restart); agitation causes sought; delirium screen; restraint use with reason, checks and release; sleep and mobility; escalation and response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At the hourly check you assess pain with the behavioural tool, then the sedation scale (target: light sedation). The score is deeper than the target, so you report it, and within your protocol reduce the infusion as ordered, reassess in 30 minutes, and record. At the planned time you perform the daily interruption with the team, monitor closely, and restart at a lower rate if the protocol says so.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 66-year-old ventilated woman is agitated and trying to pull at her tube. The nurse checks pain, bladder, oxygenation and ventilator synchrony, treats pain per protocol, reorients and reduces stimulation, and calls the prescriber about persistent agitation instead of adding restraints. She documents all steps.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A ventilated patient who should be lightly sedated is deeply sedated and does not respond to voice. What is the best nursing action?
A. Report the over-sedation, check pain and breathing, and reduce sedatives only within the protocol or as ordered.
B. Increase the infusion further, since quiet and deeply sedated patients are easier to ventilate and to nurse safely.
C. Ignore the score until the scheduled ward round, because the doctors will review the sedation plan at that time.
D. Stop all sedatives and analgesics yourself at once, without an order, so that the patient wakes as quickly as possible.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Over-sedation should be assessed and reported, with changes made only within the protocol or order.
**Why not B:** Deeper sedation is contrary to the target and has risks.
**Why not C:** Delay prolongs over-sedation.
**Why not D:** Stopping analgesia without an order may cause pain and agitation.

**Q2 (understanding).** According to SCCM, what is the key benefit of daily sedation interruption protocols and nurse-protocolised targeted sedation?
A. They guarantee a shorter hospital stay for every patient who receives the protocol, whatever their condition.
B. They can achieve and maintain a light level of sedation in intubated adults, according to the SCCM statement.
C. They remove the need for the nurse to assess sedation with a validated scale, since the protocol decides the dose.
D. They are legally required in every hospital in every country that cares for mechanically ventilated patients.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** This is the SCCM statement.
**Why not A:** No such guarantee is stated.
**Why not C:** Scales remain necessary because they give a structured, repeatable measure of sedation depth.
**Why not D:** The statement is not a legal requirement.

**Q3 (prioritization).** An agitated, ventilated patient has a high pain score with signs of bladder distension, and the family asks for restraints. What should the nurse do first?
A. Apply wrist restraints at once, since the agitation threatens the tube and the lines, and then look for the cause.
B. Give a benzodiazepine bolus because the chart says "as needed for agitation", then reassess the patient in an hour.
C. Look for and treat causes (pain, bladder, hypoxia), reorient, escalate, and use restraints only as a last resort under policy.
D. Leave the patient alone until the agitation settles by itself, and check again at the next routine round of observations.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** SCCM states pain should be treated before sedatives and that restraints have no proven efficacy and safety concerns.
**Why not A:** Restraints are a last resort and can worsen agitation.
**Why not B:** Benzodiazepines are associated with delirium, and a cause should be sought first.
**Why not D:** An agitated patient with a tube is at risk and needs attention.

**Q4 (safety).** During a daily sedation interruption the patient becomes distressed and desaturates. What is the correct response?
A. Continue the interruption until the planned time to complete the trial, since distress is usual on waking.
B. Hold the patient down firmly to prevent any movement, so that the tube and the lines are not disturbed.
C. Widen the oxygen alarm limits so that the alarm stops sounding and the trial can be completed as planned.
D. Stop the trial per protocol, restore sedation and oxygenation as ordered, assess, and report to the team.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Safety criteria to stop the trial are part of the protocol; the nurse restores safety and reports.
**Why not A:** Continuing despite distress and desaturation is unsafe.
**Why not B:** Physical force is inappropriate and unsafe.
**Why not C:** Changing alarm limits hides the problem.

**Q5 (application).** Which tool statement is accurate according to SCCM?
A. Vital signs are the best way to monitor sedation, because they change before the patient moves or speaks.
B. Bispectral index suits sedative titration in deep sedation or neuromuscular blockade, and may help when no scale can be used.
C. Bispectral index replaces sedation scales in all patients, so scales need no longer be recorded in the chart.
D. Sedation scales are unnecessary in ventilated patients because the ventilator reports the depth of sedation directly.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** This is the SCCM statement.
**Why not A:** Vital signs are not valid indicators of pain and are not the sedation measure.
**Why not C:** BIS is not a general replacement.
**Why not D:** Scales are central to targeted sedation.

**Q6 (scope).** Which decision belongs to the prescriber rather than the nurse?
A. Choosing the sedative agent and setting the target level of sedation for the patient.
B. Performing the ordered sedation scale assessment at the interval that is set out in the plan of care.
C. Reporting over-sedation or agitation that is not responding to the measures in the protocol.
D. Documenting restraint checks and the reasons for the use of the restraints on the chart.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Drug choice and targets are prescriber decisions (with protocol-based titration by the nurse).
**Why not B:** A nursing assessment task.
**Why not C:** A nursing responsibility.
**Why not D:** A nursing documentation task.
