# Preventing CLABSI
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Recommendations differ between the 2011 and 2022 documents; reviewers must settle which the platform teaches.

## 1. Overview
A central venous catheter (CVC) gives microbes a direct route from skin and connectors to the bloodstream. Most prevention is routine nursing work done every shift: hand hygiene, disinfecting connectors before access, intact and clean dressings, daily site checks, and asking every day whether the line is still needed.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Explain how organisms reach the bloodstream (insertion site, hubs and connectors, contaminated infusate).
2. Perform and document the daily maintenance elements within nursing scope.
3. Recognise early signs of line infection and act on them.
4. Describe which practices changed between the 2011 CDC guideline and the 2022 SHEA update.
5. Distinguish nursing actions from prescriber decisions (insertion, removal, cultures, antimicrobials).

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Bloodstream infection from a line raises mortality, length of stay and cost. Both guidelines list consistent nurse-delivered care as central, and the 2022 update lists adequate nurse-to-patient ratios and limiting float-pool staffing among essential practices.

## 5. Relevant pathophysiology (brief)
Skin organisms migrate along the outside of a short-term catheter; contaminated hubs and connectors seed the lumen; biofilm forms on the catheter. Risk rises with line days, multiple lumens, femoral site, emergency insertion and breaks in aseptic technique.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Every shift:** look at the dressing (intact, dry, not lifting, dated), the site (redness, swelling, discharge, pain), the line (securement, kinks, connectors capped, labels, tubing dates).
- **Palpation through the dressing** for tenderness (2011 guideline, Category II); do not routinely lift opaque dressings if there are no signs of infection.
- **Patient:** temperature trend, rigors, new haemodynamic instability, unexplained confusion, new organ dysfunction.
- **Necessity:** what is each lumen being used for today?

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Only trained, competent staff handle central lines.
- Hand hygiene before handling the dressing, hubs or tubing.
- Disinfect hubs, needleless connectors and injection ports with an appropriate antiseptic before every access (2011 Category IA; 2022 essential practice) and use sterile devices only.
- Keep the dressing sterile, dry and intact; change when damp, loose or soiled; change at the interval in your policy. Documents disagree on detail (see table below).
- Change administration sets and add-on devices at the interval in policy; label with date and time.
- Report and ask about lines that look unnecessary.
- Report any breach of technique so it can be corrected.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Temperature and haemodynamic trend; dressing/site check each shift (more often if the dressing is a concern); line days; tubing/dressing due dates; local CLABSI surveillance feedback.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Fever or rigors, hypothermia, new tachycardia or hypotension, new confusion; redness, swelling, tenderness or discharge at the site; blood in the dressing; line malfunction or leakage; wet or lifting dressing; missing caps; disconnected tubing; rigors during flush or infusion.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Hand hygiene, connector disinfection, dressing integrity, daily site check, tubing labels | Independent nursing |
| Dressing change with sterile technique | Independent nursing (competence required; per policy) |
| Raise "is this line still needed?" | Independent nursing (communication) |
| Blood cultures (central and peripheral), lactate, other tests | Needs order or protocol |
| Decision to remove, exchange or insert a line; antimicrobials; lock therapy | Prescriber decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate promptly for: fever/rigors with a line in place; site changes with systemic signs; instability; suspected disconnection or air entry; line occlusion not resolved by routine checks. Escalate organisational problems (staffing, lack of supplies) to the charge nurse.
SBAR — **S:** CVC patient, bed X, new fever 38.9, rigors, tachycardia. **B:** line day 6, site slightly red, last dressing change [time]. **A:** possible line infection or sepsis. **R:** please review now; consider cultures and the plan for the line; I will repeat observations every [interval].

## 13. Potential complications
Bloodstream infection and sepsis, septic thrombophlebitis, endocarditis, catheter occlusion, accidental disconnection or air embolism, and skin injury from antiseptics or adhesives.

## 14. Documentation
Site assessment each shift; dressing and tubing change date/time and reason; line necessity review; connector disinfection per local standard; escalation, who was told and response; breaches reported.

## 15. Practical bedside example
Before opening a line: hand hygiene, gather a sterile syringe and flush, scrub the connector with the unit antiseptic for the time set in policy, let it dry, access, flush as per policy, re-cap. If the dressing edge has lifted, do not tape over it: arrange an aseptic change with hand hygiene and a new dressing, then document.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 55-year-old, line day 7 after bowel surgery, becomes febrile with rigors and a heart rate rising from 90 to 118. The dressing is dry; the site is mildly tender on palpation. The nurse repeats observations, tells the intensivist using SBAR, asks whether the line is still needed, prepares for cultures if ordered, and records the timeline. The nurse does not remove the line or start antimicrobials without an order.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (monitoring).** A patient with a central line has a transparent dressing that is dry and intact but one corner is curling at the edge. The patient is afebrile. What should the nurse do?
A. Tape over the corner and re-check at the next shift because the site looks clean.
B. Arrange an aseptic dressing change using hand hygiene and the unit antiseptic, as the integrity is compromised.
C. Leave it until the scheduled change day, since the dressing is dry and there are no signs of infection.
D. Remove the dressing completely and leave the site open to air to check it properly.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** A lifting dressing is a breach of the barrier and should be replaced aseptically.
**Why not A:** Tape does not restore a sterile barrier and traps moisture.
**Why not C:** Waiting for the schedule leaves a break in the barrier.
**Why not D:** Leaving an open site increases contamination risk.

**Q2 (judgment).** A new fever of 38.8 °C, rigors and a rising heart rate occur in a patient with a central line on day 6. The site looks clean. What is the best action?
A. Give paracetamol per the standing protocol, repeat the temperature in four hours and review at handover.
B. Remove the dressing, pull the line yourself and send the tip to the laboratory.
C. Note the fever in the chart and wait for the next medical round because the site is clean.
D. Reassess observations, escalate using SBAR, mention the line, and prepare for cultures if ordered.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Systemic signs with a line require prompt assessment and escalation even if the site is clean.
**Why not A:** Treating the fever alone delays sepsis recognition.
**Why not B:** Removing a line is a prescriber decision and may harm the patient.
**Why not C:** Waiting delays potentially life-threatening care.

**Q3 (application).** Which sequence best reduces contamination when giving an intermittent injection through a needleless connector?
A. Hand hygiene, scrub the connector with the approved antiseptic and let it dry, then access with a sterile device.
B. Wipe once with a dry gauze, access, and re-cap with the used cap if it looks clean.
C. Apply gloves, flush through the connector without disinfecting, then wipe afterwards.
D. Disinfect the connector, access using a clean non-sterile syringe, and flush later in the shift.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Disinfection before every access with sterile devices is recommended by both documents.
**Why not B:** Dry gauze is not disinfection and reusing caps is contamination.
**Why not C:** Disinfection must come before access, not after.
**Why not D:** Only sterile devices should access the system.

**Q4 (prioritization).** At the start of a shift, four central-line patients each have an issue. Which should be addressed first?
A. A tubing label that is missing its date, for a patient who is stable.
B. A dressing due for a routine change tomorrow, with the site clean and dry.
C. A patient with a new fever, rigors and a hypotensive trend, line day 8.
D. A request from the family to change the dressing earlier for cosmetic reasons.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Possible bloodstream infection with instability is time-critical.
**Why not A:** Needs fixing but is not urgent.
**Why not B:** Routine, not urgent.
**Why not D:** Non-clinical; can be addressed after safety priorities.

**Q5 (judgment).** On a ward round, the team plans to keep a central line "just in case" although it has not been used for three days. What is the nurse's best contribution?
A. Ask whether each lumen still has an indication and whether the line can be removed, leaving the decision to the prescriber.
B. Remove the line after the round because it is the nurse's responsibility to prevent infection.
C. Keep quiet, as challenging the plan is outside the nurse's role.
D. Replace the line with a new one at a different site to reduce infection risk.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Both guidelines favour removing nonessential lines; the nurse raises the question and the prescriber decides.
**Why not B:** Removal needs a prescriber's decision or a protocol.
**Why not C:** Daily necessity review is a shared nursing and medical responsibility.
**Why not D:** Routine replacement is not recommended and adds risk.

**Q6 (safety).** A colleague says "the dressing guideline from 2011 says no chlorhexidine dressings are needed". How should the nurse respond?
A. Agree, since the 2011 guideline is the authoritative reference and has not changed.
B. Say the 2011 text was updated by CDC in 2017 and that later guidance favours CHG-containing dressings, then follow the unit policy and ask the infection-prevention team.
C. Suggest ignoring both documents because dressing type does not affect infection risk.
D. Change dressings every day to be safe, whatever the type, since more changes mean less infection.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The 2011 statements on CHG dressings were superseded; local policy and infection prevention decide.
**Why not A:** The 2011 recommendation on CHG dressings was replaced.
**Why not C:** Evidence shows dressing choice matters in ICU patients.
**Why not D:** Unnecessary changes break the barrier and are not recommended.

## 20. Evidence, applicability and open questions
- Source quality: SHEA/IDSA/APIC 2022 is the more recent, graded document; CDC 2011 is dated but widely cited. Neither covers all regions; INS standards and national infection-prevention guidance were not accessible.
- International applicability: practices are generally transferable; availability of CHG products and nurse staffing assumptions vary. The 2022 update is US-centred in some details.
- Conflicts/dated items: CDC 2011 CHG-dressing statements are superseded; "essential vs additional" classification changed in 2022; the 2011 subclavian wording differs from the 2022 positive recommendation.
- Open questions for reviewers (IPC, NE, BN): which document the platform follows; whether to teach CHG bathing and CHG dressings as standard; how to present the "dwell/dry time" for antiseptics (not given in sources read).
