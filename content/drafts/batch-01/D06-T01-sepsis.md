# Recognising possible sepsis in adults and escalating
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Section 9 separates UK-specific rules from internationally applicable principles.

## 1. Overview
Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection. It often presents vaguely, and the first person to notice is usually the nurse. The nurse's job is to suspect it, assess with a structured set of observations, grade urgency using the tool the organisation uses, escalate early and reassess. Diagnosis, antimicrobial choice and fluid prescription belong to the prescriber or team.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe non-specific presentations and explain why concerns raised by families and carers matter.
2. List patient factors that raise risk or make sepsis easy to miss.
3. Separate internationally applicable principles from NICE/NEWS2-specific rules.
4. Interpret an early-warning score as a prompt for judgment, not a replacement for it.
5. Escalate with SBAR and repeat observations at a frequency matched to risk.
6. Describe the nursing role in time-critical orders (cultures, antimicrobials, fluids) without making prescribing decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Patients may arrive from wards, theatres or the community already septic, and ICU patients with lines, catheters and ventilators develop new infections. Delays in recognition and treatment are associated with worse outcomes, and subtle changes (new confusion, falling urine output, mottled skin) often appear at the bedside first.

## 4. Basic physiology
Infection triggers inflammation. If regulation fails, vasodilation, capillary leak and impaired microcirculation reduce tissue perfusion; organs (brain, kidneys, lungs, liver, coagulation system) fail. Compensation (fast breathing, fast heart rate) can hide severity until late.

## 5. Relevant pathophysiology
Septic shock is generally described (Sepsis-3 consensus definition, cited by the SSC guideline but not read here) as sepsis with circulatory and metabolic failure, including raised lactate; this draft gives no definition criteria. Management belongs to the shock topics (D02-T09). Hypoperfusion shows as mottling, delayed capillary refill, low urine output and altered mentation.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **History and risk:** possible source (chest, urine, abdomen, wound, skin, lines, devices, surgery or invasive procedures in recent weeks); immune suppression (illness or drugs, including recent anticancer treatment); age (NICE lists 75 or over); frailty; multiple chronic conditions; indwelling catheters; repeated antibiotic use; skin breakdown; substance misuse; social disadvantage; communication barriers.
- **Observations:** respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and any new oxygen need, blood pressure (and fall from the person's usual), heart rate and rhythm, temperature (both fever and low temperature), level of consciousness or new confusion, urine output (catheterised patients: per hour), capillary refill.
- **Skin and perfusion:** mottled or ashen appearance, cyanosis of skin/lips/tongue, non-blanching rash, cold peripheries.
- **Communication:** ask family and carers about changes from usual behaviour; use an interpreter when needed; take extra care when history is hard to obtain.
- **Trend:** compare to previous observations; a slow drift matters.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Ask "could this be sepsis?" whenever infection is possible; remember that people may not have a fever.
- Take a full, timed set of observations and calculate the early-warning score your unit uses.
- Escalate according to policy and the nurse's concern, even when the score looks reassuring.
- Prepare for and promptly carry out time-critical orders: blood cultures before antimicrobials where this does not delay treatment (per policy), lactate sampling, IV access, antimicrobials and fluids as prescribed.
- Reassess regularly; escalate again if there is no improvement or deterioration.
- Document times (recognition, call, review, first antimicrobial, response).

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Frequency is set by risk and local policy. NICE links NEWS2-based risk to re-calculation intervals: about every 30 minutes (high risk), hourly (medium), every 4–6 hours (low), and routine observations (very low). Recalculate whenever the person deteriorates or something unexpected changes. In ICU, continuous monitoring is usual; the principle is that higher risk means more frequent, documented reassessment.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
New confusion or drowsiness; respiratory rate rising; new oxygen requirement; systolic pressure falling or much lower than usual; heart rate rising or new arrhythmia; low or absent urine output; temperature high or low; mottled/ashen skin, blue lips, non-blanching rash; severe pain at an infected site; "feels very unwell", family says "not himself".

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Full observations, early-warning score, escalation, reassessment, documentation | Independent nursing |
| Position, oxygen delivery per protocol thresholds, IV access, sample collection per protocol | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Blood cultures, lactate, imaging requests | Needs order or protocol |
| Antimicrobial choice, fluid prescription, vasopressor start, source control decisions | Prescriber decision |
| Administering ordered antimicrobials promptly and checking allergies | Independent nursing (administration) after prescriber order |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for: score in a high-risk band for your tool; a single parameter at the maximum weight; two or more red flags; nurse concern even if the score is low; deterioration despite treatment; no review within the time set by policy (use the chain of command).
SBAR — **S:** "I'm the nurse for bed 6. His early-warning score is [x], he is newly confused and urine output is low." **B:** "Day 2 after bowel surgery, on [current treatment], temperature [x], lactate [if available]." **A:** "I think he may be septic and getting worse." **R:** "Please review within [time]; do you want cultures and lactate before antimicrobials? I will repeat observations every [interval]."

## 13. Potential complications
Septic shock, acute kidney injury, ARDS, delirium, disseminated intravascular coagulation, pressure injury, delayed antimicrobial delivery, fluid overload from over-resuscitation.

## 14. Documentation
Time of recognition; full observations and score; who was informed, when, by what method; response time; orders received and times of cultures, antimicrobials, fluids; reassessments; family communication.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At 02:00 a patient with a urinary catheter has a heart rate rising from 88 to 112 and a respiratory rate of 24. The nurse completes the observations, checks urine output (30 mL in the last two hours), notes the patient is drowsy, calculates the early-warning score, calls using SBAR, prepares blood-culture bottles and a lactate sample while waiting, then repeats observations after 30 minutes and documents each step.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 78-year-old woman, three days after hip surgery, is slightly confused and says she "just feels wrong". Temperature is normal, blood pressure 108/60 (usual 150/85), heart rate 104, respiratory rate 22, and urine output is low. Her early-warning score is 4, but the nurse recognises a large drop from her usual pressure, new confusion and a worried daughter. The nurse escalates as a possible sepsis case with SBAR, requests review within the time set by policy, and prepares for cultures and a lactate. The doctor decides on antimicrobials and fluids.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A 78-year-old woman, three days after hip surgery, has suspected wound infection and new confusion. Her NEWS2 score is 4. Her systolic pressure has fallen from 150 to 110 mmHg, and her daughter reports a major change from baseline. What is the best nursing action?
A. Repeat the observations in four hours, because a score of 4 falls in the low-risk band for this tool.
B. Encourage oral fluids and ask the family to stay and watch her through the night.
C. Document the daughter's concern and hand over to the morning team, since she has no fever.
D. Escalate now as possible sepsis, citing the fall from her usual pressure and the new confusion.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** NICE NG253 uses clinical judgement alongside NEWS2. New confusion contributes 3 points and requires high-priority clinical review to determine whether infection caused it. The fall in blood pressure and the family’s report support prompt escalation.
**Why not A:** An aggregate score of 4 cannot be interpreted in isolation when a single parameter scores 3 or there is additional clinical concern.
**Why not B:** Oral fluids do not address possible sepsis and cannot replace clinical review; fluids are prescribed.
**Why not C:** Fever is not required for sepsis, and waiting for the morning delays treatment.

**Q2 (prioritization).** Four patients are due observations. Which one needs attention first?
A. A patient with suspected pneumonia, a score of 8, new confusion and a systolic pressure of 88 mmHg.
B. A patient with a score of 2 who is eating well and is waiting for a wound dressing change.
C. A patient with a score of 0 who is waiting for discharge paperwork to be finished.
D. A patient with a score of 3 and a temperature of 37.8 °C, who feels well, has normal urine output and is eating and drinking.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The score is in the highest band with several danger signs, so the patient needs immediate escalation.
**Why not B:** A low score in a stable patient can wait for the routine task.
**Why not C:** Very low risk and no clinical concern.
**Why not D:** This patient needs monitoring but is not time-critical compared with the others.

**Q3 (application).** The prescriber orders blood cultures and an antimicrobial for suspected sepsis. What should the nurse do?
A. Give the antimicrobial first and take the cultures at the next routine blood round, once the patient has settled.
B. Hold both until the lactate result is back so that treatment can be targeted to the patient's condition.
C. Prepare and collect the cultures without delaying the antimicrobial, check allergies, and give the first dose promptly.
D. Ask the pharmacist to decide the drug and the timing, because nurses cannot take part in antimicrobial treatment or sepsis care.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** SSC 2026 recommends collecting blood cultures as soon as possible and ideally before antimicrobials (strong), and timely antimicrobials; the nurse prepares, collects, checks allergies and gives the ordered dose promptly.
**Why not A:** Taking cultures first is preferable when it causes no delay, and later sampling lowers their yield.
**Why not B:** Waiting for a result delays time-critical treatment.
**Why not D:** The prescriber chooses the drug; the nurse administers it safely and promptly.

**Q4 (understanding).** According to the current 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guideline, how should qSOFA be used to screen acutely ill hospital patients for possible sepsis?
A. Use it as the preferred single screening tool for all hospital wards.
B. Prefer NEWS, NEWS2, MEWS or SIRS over qSOFA as a single screening tool.
C. Use it in place of clinical assessment when the ward is short of staff and observations are delayed.
D. Use it only for pregnant patients and children.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** SSC 2026 strongly recommends NEWS, NEWS2, MEWS or SIRS rather than qSOFA as a single sepsis screening tool for acutely ill hospital patients (moderate certainty). No screening score alone confirms or excludes sepsis.
**Why not A:** SSC 2026 prefers other screening tools over qSOFA alone.
**Why not C:** No score replaces clinical assessment.
**Why not D:** The guideline does not limit its statement to those groups.

**Q5 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber rather than the nurse?
A. Taking a full set of observations and calculating the early-warning score.
B. Phoning for urgent review when the score is in the high-risk band.
C. Recording the times of the call, the review and the first antimicrobial dose.
D. Choosing the antimicrobial and deciding the first fluid prescription.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Choice of antimicrobial and fluid prescription are prescribing decisions.
**Why not A:** Observations and scoring are independent nursing tasks.
**Why not B:** Escalation is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not C:** Documentation is a nursing responsibility.

**Q6 (safety).** A hospital outside the UK uses a locally approved early-warning system. A new nurse says NICE NEWS2 thresholds apply unchanged to all patients. What is the best reply?
A. NICE thresholds are an international standard, so they must be applied in every hospital, whatever early-warning score the unit has adopted for its own wards and patients.
B. Early-warning scores are used only in the UK, so units elsewhere do not need any structured tool for spotting deterioration.
C. NICE NEWS2 thresholds are specific to its defined populations and settings; use this hospital's approved escalation policy and clinical judgement.
D. NEWS2 bands can be applied without any change to pregnant patients and to children, because the same thresholds suit every group.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** NICE NG253 recommends NEWS2 for defined settings and patients aged 16 or over who are not and have not recently been pregnant. Its numeric bands should not automatically be transferred to a different scoring system. Follow the locally approved tool, escalation protocol and clinical assessment.
**Why not A:** NICE guidance is national, not an international standard.
**Why not B:** Many countries use early-warning systems and sepsis screening.
**Why not D:** NICE excludes pregnancy and refers to separate guidance for it.
