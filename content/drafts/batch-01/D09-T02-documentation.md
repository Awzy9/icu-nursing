# Nursing documentation
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Legal expectations vary by country; this draft cannot state them.

## 1. Overview
The patient record is a clinical communication tool and a legal document. Good documentation lets the next clinician continue care safely, supports quality review and shows what happened and why. In the ICU it is dense: serial observations, infusions, ventilator settings, interventions, responses and escalations.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Apply core principles: objective, factual, timely, signed, dated and timed.
2. Explain why charting before or without doing a task is falsification.
3. Correct documentation errors in paper and electronic records appropriately.
4. Document escalation and communication clearly.
5. Recognise confidentiality and security responsibilities when using electronic records.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Rapid change, many handovers and multiple team members make unclear records dangerous. Incomplete records have been linked to missed deterioration, duplicated or omitted care, and difficulty reconstructing events after incidents.

## 6. Clinical assessment (what the record should reflect)
Assessment findings and trends; interventions; the patient's response; orders received and carried out; communication and escalation; patient and family education; safety events; the reasoning behind any decision not to follow a routine practice.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Chart only what you personally assessed, observed or did; attribute information from others.
- Use objective wording (measurements, observed behaviour, quotes) rather than opinions or labels.
- Use proper terminology and approved abbreviations only (many organisations restrict abbreviations).
- Include date, time and identification of the person documenting.
- Document promptly after the task; never in advance. Never record uncompleted tasks as completed.
- Correct errors transparently: paper – a single line through the error, "mistaken entry" and initials, never erase or obscure; electronic – use the system's correction method so the original and the correction remain visible in the audit trail.
- Avoid blank lines on paper records so entries cannot be added later.
- Protect confidentiality: access only records you need to care for the patient; log out; never share credentials.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Check at each shift: are observations, intake/output, infusions, lines and devices, skin and mobility documented; are pending orders or results noted; is the plan of care current; are late entries clearly labelled.

## 9. Important findings – formats and typical content
| Format | When used | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Flowsheet / observation chart | Vital signs, fluid balance, scores | Check trends; incomplete rows hide changes |
| Narrative or progress note | Explaining events and reasoning | Keep factual; avoid copying forward old text |
| Focused (for example data–action–response) | Specific problems | Link action to response |
| Medication / treatment administration record | What was given and when | Record at the time, with reasons for non-administration |
| Escalation note | Calls for deterioration | Include time, who, what was said, response |

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Gaps in observations during deterioration; copied entries that do not match the patient; entries made before the task; entries by someone who did not do the task; undocumented verbal orders; shared passwords; late entries not marked as late; information in the wrong patient's record.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Accurate, timely, signed entries and corrections per policy | Independent nursing |
| Reading back and recording verbal orders per policy *(not covered by the section read; local policy governs)* | Collaborative (per order or policy) |
| Medical diagnoses and orders | Prescriber decision |
| Changing or deleting another person's entries | Not permitted; use the correction process |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate to the charge nurse or manager when: documentation is missing for a safety-critical task; you discover an entry in the wrong record; another person asks you to alter documentation inappropriately; you suspect inappropriate access to records.

## 13. Potential complications of poor documentation
Missed or delayed treatment, duplication of care (for example repeated medication), inability to defend care decisions, regulatory action, privacy breaches.

## 15. Practical bedside example
You are interrupted while giving a prescribed treatment and cannot chart it immediately. Make brief notes if needed, finish the task, then chart with the real time the task was done, noting that the entry is made later if the system requires it. Never copy a colleague's time or chart ahead to "save time".

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (safety).** A nurse is interrupted before recording a dose that she has just given. What is the correct action?
A. Wait until the end of the shift, then enter all the doses together at their scheduled times.
B. Ask the colleague who saw her give the dose to record it for her, so that it is charted close to the right time.
C. Record it as soon as possible with the actual time of administration, marked as a late entry if the system requires.
D. Leave it unrecorded, because the pharmacy record already shows that the dose was dispensed.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Timely, accurate recording by the person who did the task, with the real time.
**Why not A:** Batching entries and backdating them blur the true time and risk repeat doses.
**Why not B:** Only the person who did the task should document it; others cannot attest to her actions.
**Why not D:** Dispensing is not administration, and the record must show what was given.

**Q2 (judgment).** Which entry is most appropriate in the record?
A. "Patient was agitated and difficult overnight, and the family were demanding."
B. "03:10 grimaced and said 'my chest hurts', pain 7/10, HR 112; doctor called 03:12."
C. "Patient is probably just anxious; no action needed at this time."
D. "Patient was fine all night according to the other nurse who looked after him."
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** It is objective and time-stamped, with a quotation, measurements and the action taken.
**Why not A:** It uses subjective labels with no observable detail.
**Why not C:** It is an opinion without any recorded assessment findings.
**Why not D:** It reports another person's observation as if it were the writer's own, without attribution.

**Q3 (application).** A nurse notices she wrote an incorrect value on a paper chart. What should she do?
A. Cover the value with correction fluid and write the right figure over it so that the page stays neat and readable.
B. Cross it out repeatedly so that nobody can read the original value.
C. Replace the whole sheet with a neat rewritten copy and discard the original page so that the chart is easy to read.
D. Draw one line through it, write "mistaken entry" with her initials, and add the correct entry.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The error stays legible and the correction is attributable.
**Why not A:** Obscuring the original suggests concealment and is not allowed.
**Why not B:** The original entry must remain readable.
**Why not C:** Discarding part of the record is inappropriate; entries must be preserved.

**Q4 (prioritization).** At the end of a busy shift the nurse has time to complete only one of these. Which is most important?
A. Completing the record of a new oxygen requirement and the call to the doctor for a patient who worsened.
B. Updating the care plan for a stable patient who is waiting for discharge, so that it is up to date for the receiving ward.
C. Rewriting the handwriting in an earlier entry so that it is neater and easier for colleagues to read.
D. Completing an education note for a patient who is already well informed and has no outstanding questions.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Safety-critical changes and escalation must be recorded promptly for the next team.
**Why not B:** Valuable, but not time-critical for patient safety.
**Why not C:** A cosmetic change that adds nothing to safety.
**Why not D:** Not safety-critical and not urgent.

**Q5 (monitoring).** Which finding on chart review should prompt follow-up?
A. A signed and dated correction of a mistaken entry with the original still visible.
B. A progress note that includes times, measurements and what the patient said.
C. Hourly observations with a five-hour gap during a period of falling blood pressure.
D. A medication record that gives the reason a dose was withheld.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** A gap during deterioration suggests missed monitoring or missed recording and needs action.
**Why not A:** This is appropriate correction practice.
**Why not B:** This is good documentation practice.
**Why not D:** Recording the reason for a withheld dose is expected practice.

**Q6 (scope).** A colleague asks you to change the time on her earlier entry because she charted it late. What is the best response?
A. Change it for her, since you both know the true time and nothing is being hidden from anyone reviewing the chart.
B. Delete the entry so that she can rewrite it with the correct time.
C. Tell her to leave it as it is, because entries can never be altered once made.
D. Advise her to use the correction or late-entry process so the original stays visible, and report any concern.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Corrections go through the proper process, made by the author, with an audit trail.
**Why not A:** Altering another person's entry is inappropriate, even with good intentions.
**Why not B:** Deleting entries is not allowed because the record must stay complete.
**Why not C:** Records can be corrected through approved processes; refusing to correct leaves an inaccuracy.
