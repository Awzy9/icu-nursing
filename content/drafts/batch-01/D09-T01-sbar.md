# SBAR, clinical handover and escalation of deterioration
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated.

## 1. Overview
SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation/Request) is a structured way to pass on information about a patient's condition, whether to a colleague at handover or to a physician when the patient is getting worse. AHRQ's TeamSTEPPS describes it as adaptable: use the parts that fit the situation. It helps nurses speak up clearly and briefly. It is a communication tool, not an assessment or a clinical decision tool.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. State what belongs in each SBAR element.
2. Prepare the data needed before making an escalation call.
3. Make a clear recommendation or request, with a time frame, and use repeat-back to close the loop.
4. Escalate again when concern remains, using the chain of command.
5. Adapt the structure for handover and for urgent calls.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Patients change quickly and information crosses many hands. Vague messages ("he doesn't look right") are easy to under-prioritise; structured messages with numbers and a request are easier to act on. Communication failures are a recurrent theme in patient-safety incidents.

## 6. Clinical assessment (prepare before you call)
Have ready: patient identity and location; the vital signs and early-warning score, with trend; what changed and when; recent interventions and their effect; relevant history, allergies, code status; recent results; current treatments and lines/devices; what you have already done. If safe, have the chart and observation chart at hand.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Introduce yourself and the unit; do not assume the person knows you.
- Give the **situation** first: who the patient is and the problem in one or two sentences.
- Add only the **background** needed to understand it.
- State your **assessment** in your own words, including how worried you are and what you think the problem is; mention uncertainty honestly.
- Make a specific **recommendation or request** and a time frame ("please come to the bedside within 10 minutes"); then repeat back the answer.
- If the response is not enough for the risk, escalate through the chain of command per policy.
- Document the call.

## 8. Monitoring
After escalation, reassess at the frequency the risk requires, and report changes using the same structure ("update since my call").

## 9. Important findings – SBAR content guide
| Element | Contents | Example phrase |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Who you are, patient identifiers, current problem | "This is the nurse in ICU bay 4 about Mr A, 68 years old; his blood pressure has dropped." |
| Background | Relevant history, symptoms, test results, treatments | "Day 2 after surgery; on [treatment]; urine output low for 3 hours." |
| Assessment | What you think is happening, objective data, trend | "Heart rate 118, systolic 84, new confusion. I am worried he is getting septic." |
| Recommendation / Request | What you need, by when; repeat-back | "Please review within 10 minutes. Do you want cultures started? I will repeat observations every 15 minutes." |

SBAR can also be modified for patients and families to share concerns; for handover, order can follow unit policy.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags (communication)
No answer to a page or call; request for review not acknowledged; unclear plan after the call ("let's see"); your concern is dismissed while the patient worsens; handover omitting allergies, code status, pending results or isolation status.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Structured call, repeat-back, chain-of-command escalation, documentation | Independent nursing |
| Starting protocol-driven actions in the interim (oxygen, position, repeat observations) | Collaborative (per protocol) |
| Orders, diagnosis, treatment changes | Prescriber decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate when the early-warning score or any single parameter reaches the policy trigger, when the nurse is worried, or when a requested review does not occur or does not address the problem. Use the next level of the chain of command rather than waiting.

## 13. Potential complications of poor communication
Delayed treatment, duplicated or missed tasks, wrong-patient errors, medication errors, loss of trust within the team.

## 14. Documentation
Time of call and who was contacted; the information given (summarised in SBAR form); the advice or order and time; actions taken; further escalation and outcome.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (application).** Which statement is the best example of the "Assessment" element when calling a physician about a deteriorating patient?
A. "I think he is getting worse and may be septic: his heart rate is 118, systolic 84, and he is newly confused."
B. "He is a 68-year-old man in bed 4 who had bowel surgery two days ago and has a urinary catheter and a central line."
C. "Please come and see him within ten minutes, and tell me whether you want cultures and a lactate."
D. "I have already written the latest observations on the chart and flagged them for the next round."
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Assessment states the nurse's interpretation together with the data that support it.
**Why not B:** This is situation and background information.
**Why not C:** This is the recommendation or request.
**Why not D:** This is documentation, not an assessment, and gives the doctor nothing to act on.

**Q2 (prioritization).** A patient's blood pressure has fallen and he is newly confused, while a colleague asks for help with a routine task. What should the nurse do first?
A. Finish the routine task first, then collect all the observations and make the call afterwards so that it is complete.
B. Send a text message saying "please review bed 4" and carry on with the routine task.
C. Assess the patient quickly, gather the key data and call with SBAR, giving a request and a time frame.
D. Ask the family to find a doctor while the nurse helps the colleague.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Deterioration is time-critical, and a structured call with a request speeds up action.
**Why not A:** Delaying the call delays treatment.
**Why not B:** A vague message lacks data and a time frame, and may not be read promptly.
**Why not D:** Escalation is the nurse's responsibility, and relatives should not be relied on for it.

**Q3 (judgment).** After an SBAR call the doctor says "keep an eye on him", but the nurse's concern remains. What is the best response?
A. Accept the advice and document it, because the doctor has already reviewed the situation by telephone and the nurse should not repeat requests.
B. Repeat the specific concern with new data, ask for bedside review within a stated time, and use the chain of command if needed.
C. Wait until the next scheduled round to raise the concern again, to avoid seeming to challenge the doctor in front of the team.
D. Reduce the observation frequency so that the team is not disturbed by further alarms.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** A persisting concern should be voiced clearly with data and a request, and escalation policy supports going further.
**Why not A:** Documenting alone does not resolve the risk to the patient.
**Why not C:** Waiting wastes time while the patient may be deteriorating.
**Why not D:** More frequent observation, not less, is appropriate when the nurse is worried.

**Q4 (understanding).** According to the AHRQ TeamSTEPPS page, which statement about SBAR is accurate?
A. It must always be delivered in a fixed order and include all four parts, even in an emergency, or it is not SBAR.
B. It is meant only for physicians and is not designed for nurses.
C. It replaces the written record once a call has been made.
D. It is adaptable: the parts used can fit the situation, and a repeat-back confirms the response agreed.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The page describes SBAR as adaptable, like a menu, and describes repeat-back for the request.
**Why not A:** The page stresses flexibility.
**Why not B:** It is for any team member and can even be adapted for patients and families.
**Why not C:** The written record remains a separate requirement.

**Q5 (safety).** Which handover omission is most likely to cause harm?
A. Not mentioning that the family usually visits after lunch and would like an update.
B. Not mentioning a severe penicillin allergy and a culture result that is still pending.
C. Not mentioning that the patient was moved to this bed from another bay on admission to the unit.
D. Not mentioning that the day-shift nurse updated the bedside whiteboard with the plan for the day.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Allergy and pending results directly affect immediate treatment safety.
**Why not A:** Useful for family communication, but it carries little immediate safety risk.
**Why not C:** Bed history rarely changes immediate care, unless it relates to isolation or infection control.
**Why not D:** Information that is already visible at the bedside and not critical to a safe handover.

**Q6 (scope).** In an SBAR call, what is the best use of the "Recommendation/Request" element?
A. State the diagnosis the nurse believes is present and name the drug the nurse wants prescribed so that time is saved.
B. Repeat the full history in more detail so that the doctor does not need to read the chart.
C. Ask for what is needed, such as review or tests, within a time frame, and say what the nurse will do meanwhile.
D. Give last week's early-warning scores to show how the patient has changed over time.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** A clear request with timing guides action while respecting prescriber roles.
**Why not A:** Nurses give assessments and suggestions, but prescribing remains the prescriber's decision.
**Why not B:** More history belongs in background and risks losing the message.
**Why not D:** Old data does not state what is needed now.
