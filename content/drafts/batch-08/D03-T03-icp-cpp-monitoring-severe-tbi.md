# ICP and cerebral perfusion pressure monitoring – severe traumatic brain injury (guideline population only)
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. THE GUIDELINE POPULATION IS SEVERE TRAUMATIC BRAIN INJURY IN ADULTS. Its thresholds are not general rules for all neurological patients, and treatment decisions belong to the clinical team.

## 1. Overview
Intracranial pressure (ICP) is the pressure inside the skull. Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) is the difference between the mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) and the ICP; it can only be calculated when the ICP is known. In severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) the Brain Trauma Foundation (BTF) 4th edition guideline makes recommendations on whether and how monitoring is used and on thresholds. It stresses that it is **not monitoring itself that changes outcomes but using the information to direct treatment**, and that in practice monitoring is combined with ongoing clinical assessment. The nurse's role is to maintain safe monitoring, observe the patient and the numbers together, recognise deterioration and escalate promptly. Interpretation, treatment, thresholds for the individual patient and decisions about drainage, drugs, ventilation or surgery belong to the clinical team.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Secondary injury after TBI is driven by raised ICP, low perfusion and low blood pressure. The guideline notes that hypotension is strongly associated with mortality in severe TBI and that CPP is believed to be the pressure metric to which cerebral autoregulation responds. The bedside nurse sees the patient's neurological state and the numbers continuously.

## 6. Clinical assessment
**Population.** Severe TBI (the monitoring statement refers to a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 3–8 after resuscitation). The guideline is for adults; recommendations for children and other conditions are not covered here.

**Monitoring (BTF statements; levels as printed):**
- **ICP monitoring:** management of severe TBI patients using information from ICP monitoring is recommended to reduce in-hospital and 2-week post-injury mortality (**Level IIB**). The guideline restates, without current-standard evidence, earlier indications: ICP should be monitored in all salvageable severe TBI patients with an abnormal CT scan (haematoma, contusion, swelling, herniation or compressed basal cisterns), and in severe TBI with a normal CT if two or more of age over 40, motor posturing, or systolic blood pressure below 90 mm Hg are present. Descriptions of monitor technologies were removed from this edition.
- **CPP monitoring:** management of severe TBI patients using guideline-based recommendations for CPP monitoring is recommended to decrease 2-week mortality (**Level IIB**).
- **Advanced cerebral monitoring** (for example jugular bulb oxygen measures): insufficient evidence for Level I or II; Level III statements only; the earlier brain tissue oxygen recommendation was removed because of contradictory higher-quality evidence.

**Thresholds:**
- **ICP:** treating an ICP above **22 mm Hg** is recommended because values above it are associated with increased mortality (**Level IIB**); a combination of ICP values with clinical and brain CT findings may be used to make management decisions (**Level III**).
- **CPP:** the recommended target for survival and favourable outcomes is between **60 and 70 mm Hg** (**Level IIB**); whether 60 or 70 is the minimum optimal threshold is unclear and may depend on the patient's autoregulatory status; avoiding aggressive attempts to keep CPP above 70 with fluids and vasopressors may be considered because of the risk of adult respiratory failure (**Level III**).
- **Blood pressure:** maintaining systolic pressure at 100 mm Hg or higher (patients 50–69 years) or 110 mm Hg or higher (patients 15–49 or over 70) may be considered to decrease mortality (**Level III**).
- **Jugular venous saturation** below 50 % may be a threshold to avoid (**Level III**).

**Treatments the guideline addresses (clinician decisions):** CSF drainage (an external ventricular drain zeroed at the midbrain with continuous drainage may be considered, Level III); hyperosmolar therapy (insufficient evidence to support a specific agent or recommendation); ventilation therapies (prolonged prophylactic hyperventilation to a PaCO₂ of 25 mm Hg or less is not recommended, Level IIB; hyperventilation as a temporising measure and avoidance in the first 24 hours are restated from the prior edition); sedation and anaesthetic agents (barbiturates and propofol statements with cautions); steroids (not recommended; high-dose methylprednisolone contraindicated, Level I); decompressive craniectomy; early nutrition; DVT prophylaxis, seizure prophylaxis and infection prophylaxis.

**What the guideline does not say (to avoid over-reading):** it gives no nursing instructions for head position, transducer levelling, waveform interpretation, pupil checks, neuro-observation frequency, hourly care bundles or drain management; any such nursing content must come from other sources, not read here.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities (author's translation; flagged for clinician review)
- **Know the ordered targets for your patient** (ICP, CPP or MAP ranges, and the escalation limits set by the team). The BTF numbers are guideline statements, not orders; the team may set individualised targets.
- **Observe the patient and the numbers together.** The guideline itself says clinical assessment continues alongside monitoring; record neurological observations (for example GCS – see D03-T01) at the interval ordered, together with ICP, MAP, CPP and trends.
- **Keep monitoring safe and accurate** per unit procedure (position of transducers, zeroing and levelling, checking waveforms and connections): **technique is not described in the BTF guideline and is unresolved here** – follow local policy and training.
- **Protect perfusion:** report hypotension, falling CPP and rising ICP promptly; the guideline links hypotension to worse outcomes and recommends clinician-set blood pressure thresholds.
- **Report immediately** a new or worsening neurological sign, a sustained ICP above the ordered limit, a CPP outside the ordered range, a sudden change in the waveform or loss of the signal, or any problem with a drain or monitor.
- **Do not change ventilation, osmotic therapy, sedation, drain settings or blood pressure support unless ordered or covered by a protocol you are trained in;** these are clinician-directed treatments.
- **Support lung and haemodynamic safety:** the guideline cautions against prolonged hyperventilation and against aggressive efforts to push CPP above 70; report ventilation or blood gas concerns promptly.
- **Document** values, times, neurological observations, interventions and communications.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
ICP value and waveform where monitored; MAP and calculated CPP (MAP minus ICP); systolic blood pressure; neurological status (GCS, pupils and limb movement as per unit policy – not itemised in the guideline); oxygenation and ventilation (SpO₂, PaCO₂ or end-tidal CO₂); temperature; sodium and osmolality if hyperosmolar therapy is used; drain output if an external ventricular drain is in use; sedation; seizure activity. Frequency by unit policy and orders.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Falling GCS or a change in pupils or limb movement (unit-defined); sustained rise in ICP; falling CPP or MAP; hypotension; abnormal or lost ICP waveform; vomiting, headache, agitation or posturing (general clinical knowledge, flagged); seizure; falling SpO₂ or raised CO₂; sudden drain output change or leak; monitor or transducer fault; sedation or ventilation changes followed by a rise in pressure.

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for neurological deterioration, a sustained ICP above the ordered limit, CPP outside the ordered range, hypotension, loss of monitoring, seizures, or any drain problem.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, severe TBI patient with ICP rising over the last 20 minutes and GCS falling. **B:** day 2 after injury; ICP monitor in place; ordered CPP range 60 to 70. **A:** deteriorating neurology with rising ICP and falling CPP. **R:** please come now; I am checking the airway, ventilation, blood pressure and monitor trace and need orders.

## 13. Potential complications
Secondary brain injury, herniation, cerebral hypoperfusion, hypotension, respiratory failure from excessive CPP support (guideline, Level III caution), complications of treatments (the guideline notes morbidity with high-dose propofol and barbiturate haemodynamic effects), infection with drains. Other complications are general clinical knowledge not traced to the sections read.

## 14. Documentation
Time; ICP, MAP, CPP and trends; neurological observations; interventions; drain output; ventilation and sedation status; orders and targets; escalations and responses.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At the start of the shift the nurse reads the ordered ICP and CPP limits, checks the monitor trace and connections, records baseline neurological observations and MAP, calculates CPP from MAP minus ICP as shown on the monitor, and tells the team about any trend toward the limits.

A 28-year-old with severe TBI has an ICP of 18 that rises to 26 over 20 minutes while MAP falls; the displayed CPP drops below the ordered range and the GCS falls by one point. The nurse checks airway, ventilation and the trace, reports the trend and the patient's change at once, and records all values; the team decides on treatment.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (application).** A patient with a subarachnoid haemorrhage has an ICP of 24 mm Hg. A colleague says "the guideline says treat above 22, so we must treat now". What is the best response?
A. Agree, because the 22 mm Hg threshold applies to patients with raised intracranial pressure from any cause.
B. Agree, if the patient is older than 40 years, because age is the criterion for using the threshold.
C. Start a hyperosmolar drug at once, because the guideline recommends it for every pressure above the threshold.
D. Explain that the BTF threshold is for severe TBI, and report the value so the team can apply the ordered target.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The guideline population is severe TBI; thresholds are not generalised to other neurological conditions and treatment is a clinician decision.
**Why not A:** The statement is specific to severe TBI.
**Why not B:** Age is not the criterion for the threshold.
**Why not C:** Starting drugs needs an order, and the guideline gives no specific agent recommendation.

**Q2 (understanding).** How does the BTF guideline define cerebral perfusion pressure?
A. The sum of the intracranial pressure and the mean arterial pressure, measured at the same moment.
B. The difference between mean arterial blood pressure and intracranial pressure, which needs the ICP to be known.
C. The systolic blood pressure alone, because the systolic value drives blood flow to the brain tissue.
D. The intracranial pressure divided by the heart rate, which is displayed continuously on the monitor screen.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The guideline states CPP is the difference between MAP and ICP and can only be calculated when ICP is known.
**Why not A:** It is a difference, not a sum.
**Why not C:** Systolic pressure alone is not CPP.
**Why not D:** No such ratio is defined.

**Q3 (judgment).** A severe TBI patient's displayed CPP has fallen below the range ordered by the team and MAP has dropped. What is the most appropriate nursing action?
A. Report at once, check the airway, blood pressure source and monitor connections, and keep the team informed.
B. Increase the vasopressor rate on your own judgment of the pressure, since a higher pressure is always better.
C. Wait ten minutes to see whether the pressure recovers before telling anyone about the fall in the value.
D. Lower the head of the bed to raise the blood pressure, without an order, since position changes are harmless.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** A fall in CPP and MAP needs prompt reporting; targets and treatment are the team's decisions.
**Why not B:** Titration outside an order or protocol is unsafe.
**Why not C:** Delay risks secondary injury.
**Why not D:** Positioning changes for pressure management need a policy or order, and head position is not addressed by the guideline.

**Q4 (understanding).** What does the guideline say about the evidence for the CPP target of 60 to 70 mm Hg?
A. It is a Level I recommendation based on several large randomised trials that all agreed on the same range.
B. It is a recommendation for all brain-injured patients regardless of the cause of the injury or the age.
C. It is a Level IIB recommendation, and the best minimum may depend on the patient's autoregulatory status.
D. It was withdrawn in the 4th edition, so the guideline no longer gives any target range for the pressure.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The guideline gives a Level IIB recommendation for 60–70 and notes autoregulatory status.
**Why not A:** The guideline states there was insufficient evidence for Level I.
**Why not B:** The population is severe TBI.
**Why not D:** The recommendation is present.

**Q5 (safety).** A nurse on a neurocritical care unit reads that the guideline advises caution about CPP above 70 mm Hg. Why?
A. High CPP improves outcomes, so no caution is needed when a clinician pushes the pressure up with fluids or drugs.
B. High CPP is thought to be more dangerous in older patients, and carries no risk in younger adults.
C. Aggressive efforts to push CPP above 70 with fluids and pressors may risk adult respiratory failure.
D. High CPP is harmful if the patient has no ICP monitor in place, because it cannot then be measured.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The guideline's Level III statement cautions that aggressive attempts to push CPP above 70 with fluids and pressors may risk adult respiratory failure. It is a caution about harm at higher values, not about age or monitor presence.
**Why not A:** The guideline cites harm at higher values.
**Why not B:** Age is not the stated basis.
**Why not D:** The caution does not depend on monitor presence.

**Q6 (scope).** Which of these belongs to the clinical team rather than the bedside nurse?
A. Deciding to start hyperosmolar therapy or drainage in response to a raised intracranial pressure.
B. Recording ICP, MAP and CPP values and the trends in each, at the interval the unit sets.
C. Reporting a fall in the patient's Glasgow Coma Scale score to the team immediately, with the other findings.
D. Checking that the monitor connections are intact and the trace is present on the display at every check.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Treatment decisions belong to prescribers; the guideline gives no recommendation for a specific hyperosmolar agent.
**Why not B:** Recording is a nursing task.
**Why not C:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not D:** Checking equipment is a nursing responsibility.

## 20. Evidence, applicability and open questions
