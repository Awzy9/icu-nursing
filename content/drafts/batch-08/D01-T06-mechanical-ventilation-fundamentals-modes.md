# Mechanical ventilation fundamentals and modes – what the nurse needs to interpret and escalate
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Built from an educational review and a respiratory-therapy guideline; ventilator-setting decisions belong to the prescriber and respiratory therapist, not to this draft.

## 1. Overview
Mechanical ventilation supports gas exchange while the underlying illness is treated. The educational review describes it as a set of physiological principles rather than a collection of brand names, and offers a brand-neutral way of describing any breath: how it is **initiated** (by the ventilator or the patient), how it is **delivered** (a set pressure or a set flow and volume), how it is **cycled** (ended) and how the patient **breathes out**. Modes are described by three attributes: the control variable (pressure or volume), the breath sequence (spontaneous, assisted, intermittent mandatory or mandatory) and the targeting scheme (how the ventilator adapts). The AARC guideline stresses that the focus of a patient-ventilator assessment is the patient rather than the ventilator. The nurse understands what the monitored numbers and waveforms mean, observes the patient and the interaction with the ventilator, recognises deterioration, documents and escalates. Choosing and changing modes and settings belongs to the prescriber and respiratory therapist.

**Source status:** a UK educational review (not a guideline) plus a US society guideline. Neither tells nurses what to do; see section 20.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe the four phases of a ventilator breath in brand-neutral terms.
2. Contrast pressure-controlled and volume-controlled breaths, and identify the controlled variable and which other variables may vary.
3. Explain PEEP, auto-PEEP, peak pressure, plateau pressure, resistance and compliance at the level the review gives.
4. Describe spontaneous, assisted, intermittent mandatory and mandatory breath sequences.
5. List what the AARC guideline says should be assessed at the bedside, and what each finding may suggest.
6. Recognise deterioration and patient–ventilator mismatch, document, and escalate; separate nursing from prescriber and respiratory-therapist roles.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
The review notes that about a third of adult general critical care admissions in the UK national audit data involve mechanical ventilation. Nurses are with the patient continuously and are the first to see distress, a change in the monitored values, or a mismatch between the patient and the machine.

## 6. Clinical assessment
**The breath cycle (review):**
- **Initiation:** ventilator-triggered (time) or patient-triggered by a change in pressure or flow, or by a diaphragm electrical signal in neurally adjusted modes. The trigger must be sensitive enough to detect effort but not so sensitive that leaks, cardiac oscillations or circuit artefact cause false (auto) triggering; an insensitive trigger increases work of breathing and may worsen patient–ventilator asynchrony.
- **Delivery:** in *volume control* the ventilator targets a set tidal volume with a chosen flow pattern while **airway pressure varies** with the patient's mechanics. The measured volume reaching or leaving the patient may differ if a pressure limit interrupts inspiration or if there is a leak; in *pressure control* the ventilator delivers a set inspiratory pressure for a set time, so **tidal volume varies** with compliance, resistance and effort and is not guaranteed.
- **Cycling:** ends inspiration by time, by delivered volume, or when inspiratory flow falls to a set fraction of its peak (usually 25 % in pressure support).
- **Expiration:** usually passive; short expiratory time in airflow limitation can trap gas and cause intrinsic PEEP.

**Key terms (review):**
- **PEEP** keeps alveoli open at end-expiration; there is no single optimal value, and it is patient-specific because the benefits (recruitment, oxygenation) must be balanced against overdistension and haemodynamic effects. **Auto-PEEP** is extra end-expiratory pressure from gas trapping; total PEEP = set PEEP + auto-PEEP.
- **Peak inspiratory pressure** is the highest airway pressure in inspiration and depends on both airway resistance and elastic properties. **Plateau pressure** is measured during an end-inspiratory pause with no flow and approximates alveolar pressure under passive conditions.
- **Resistance** is opposition to gas flow; it rises with asthma, COPD, bronchospasm, secretions or tube obstruction. In passive conditions it is estimated from the difference between peak and plateau pressure divided by flow. **Compliance** (the inverse of elastance) is how easily the system distends; the review gives static compliance as tidal volume divided by plateau pressure minus total end-expiratory pressure, and notes that the same airway pressure may mean different things depending on chest wall and lung mechanics.

**Breath sequences and modes (review):** *spontaneous* (patient triggers and cycles, ventilator provides PEEP or CPAP); *assisted* (the ventilator augments each patient-triggered breath, for example pressure support, which is central to weaning); *intermittent mandatory ventilation* (set number of mandatory breaths with spontaneous breaths between them; synchronised forms give the mandatory breath within a window); *mandatory* (all breaths delivered regardless of effort, which in practice often requires substantial sedation and sometimes neuromuscular blockade; the review says neuromuscular blockade should not be viewed as a routine adjunct). **Targeting schemes** include set-point, dual, servo (assistance proportional to effort), adaptive (the ventilator adjusts between breaths to a target volume) and closed-loop or automated forms. The review's trade-name examples (for example PRVC, ASV, SmartCare) differ between manufacturers; **do not assume any named mode exists on your ventilator or works as described**. Advanced modes such as airway pressure release ventilation are described but are specialist.

**Evidence overview (review, selective, author's interpretation):** lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volumes based on predicted body weight and limited plateau pressure (30 cm H₂O or less in the ARDSNet trial) is the central approach in acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure; effect sizes outside ARDS are smaller; routine higher PEEP in all ARDS was not recommended by ESICM, and the ATS recommends higher PEEP only in moderate-to-severe ARDS; no automated weaning system has been shown superior to skilled, protocolised weaning.

**What the AARC guideline says is assessed (strong or conditional as printed):** plateau pressure to ensure lung-protective settings (strong; high certainty); tidal volume (4–8 mL/kg PBW (predicted body weight)) and documenting it as mL/kg predicted body weight (strong; high); PEEP and auto-PEEP (strong; high); driving pressure (conditional; low); FiO₂ to ensure normoxaemia (conditional; very low); skin around artificial airways and NIV interfaces (strong; high); cuff pressure with a manometer (strong; high); position and securement of the airway (conditional; very low); humidification (conditional; very low to low). A patient-ventilator assessment is a comprehensive assessment by a clinician at regular intervals, including physical examination, bedside physiological data, the airway, ventilator settings and monitoring, and humidification, with documentation.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities (translation, not guideline text)
- **Look at the patient first:** breathing effort and pattern, chest movement, use of accessory muscles, colour, SpO₂, heart rate and rhythm, blood pressure, consciousness, comfort, secretions, and whether the patient appears in sync with the machine.
- **Read the display in context** and record the values the unit requires (set mode and settings, exhaled tidal volume, respiratory rate, minute ventilation, peak and plateau pressures when measured, PEEP, FiO₂). Use the **mL/kg predicted body weight** convention for tidal volume when the unit does (AARC strong recommendation to document this way).
- **Notice trends:** the review defines resistance from the difference between peak and plateau pressure and says it rises with secretions, tube obstruction, bronchospasm and airway disease, and defines static compliance from tidal volume and plateau pressure. From those definitions a widening gap between peak and plateau suggests a resistance problem, and a rising plateau suggests stiffer lungs or chest wall; this reading is the author's translation (flagged for clinician review), not a statement in the review. The nurse reports the pattern and the patient's state; **diagnosis and any setting change are for the prescriber and respiratory therapist**.
- **Watch for patient–ventilator mismatch** (the review: an insensitive trigger increases work of breathing; an over-sensitive trigger causes auto-triggering from leaks or cardiac oscillations): the patient fights the ventilator, breaths do not match effort, repeated alarms or unexplained changes. Check the patient and the circuit, then report.
- **Support lung protection within your role:** keep the circuit and airway secure; maintain cuff pressure checks and skin care per unit policy (AARC); report rising pressures or falling volumes promptly; avoid disconnections.
- **Never change modes, targets or alarm limits unless a written protocol or order permits and you are trained**; ventilator-setting decisions are prescriber and respiratory-therapist decisions.
- **Escalate** early; see section 12. **Document** the assessment, trends, interventions and communications.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Patient: respiratory rate and effort, SpO₂, end-tidal CO₂ if used, heart rate, blood pressure, perfusion, consciousness, sedation and comfort, secretions, chest wall movement, skin under devices. Ventilator: mode and settings, exhaled tidal volume, minute ventilation, peak and plateau pressures, PEEP and auto-PEEP, FiO₂, driving pressure where displayed, waveforms where the unit teaches them, alarms. Airway: tube position and securement, cuff pressure, humidification. Frequency is set by unit policy.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Increasing respiratory effort or distress; falling SpO₂ or rising CO₂; patient fighting the ventilator; sudden change in exhaled tidal volume or minute ventilation; rising peak or plateau pressures; new or repeated alarms; haemodynamic instability after a change in ventilation or PEEP; secretions or a blocked tube; displaced or leaking airway; sedation difficulty; a mismatch between what the display says and what the patient looks like.

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for distress, desaturation, loss of ventilation, a displaced airway or haemodynamic collapse; summon help and support effective ventilation under emergency protocol without delaying for alarm investigation. Escalate promptly for rising pressures, falling measured volumes, persistent mismatch or concern without a clear cause. Compare actual delivery with prescribed settings and refer unexpected discrepancies to the respiratory team.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, ventilated patient with increasing effort; exhaled tidal volume has fallen and the peak pressure has risen over the last 15 minutes. **B:** day 2 on mechanical ventilation, thick secretions at the last suction. **A:** possible airway or circuit problem or worsening mechanics; patient looks distressed and SpO₂ is falling. **R:** please come now; I am checking the tube, circuit and secretions and need help to assess and decide on any change to the ventilation.

## 13. Potential complications
The review mentions ventilator-induced lung injury, atelectrauma, dynamic hyperinflation with intrinsic PEEP, haemodynamic effects of positive pressure, patient–ventilator asynchrony, and complications of deep sedation and prolonged paralysis (ICU-acquired weakness). Other complications are general clinical knowledge not traced to the sources read and are flagged for clinician review.

## 14. Documentation
Time; mode and settings as set; monitored values and trends; patient assessment; airway position, cuff pressure and securement; humidification; secretions; sedation; actions; who was informed and responses.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At the start of the shift the nurse checks the airway position and securement, reads the set mode and the monitored values, compares exhaled tidal volume and pressures with the previous shift's record, looks for synchrony between the patient's effort and the machine, notes secretions, and reports any change to the respiratory therapist and the doctor.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (application).** A patient is on a mode where the clinician sets the inspiratory pressure. The nurse sees the exhaled tidal volume fall from the usual value while the set pressure is unchanged. What does the review's description of pressure control suggest?
A. The ventilator is delivering the set tidal volume, so the fall is only a display error that can be ignored safely.
B. Tidal volume is not guaranteed in pressure control, so a fall may mean worse mechanics or less effort: look for causes and report.
C. The set pressure has automatically been raised by the ventilator to compensate, which explains why the tidal volume has become lower.
D. The patient has switched to a different breath sequence without any change having been made to the ventilator settings.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** With a fixed pressure-controlled breath, delivered volume varies with respiratory mechanics, inspiratory time and patient effort. A leak can also reduce measured exhaled volume. Assess ventilation and oxygenation, check the circuit and escalate an unexplained fall promptly; pressure-targeted volume-adaptive modes are a separate case.
**Why not A:** Volume is not what the ventilator guarantees in pressure control.
**Why not C:** The set pressure does not rise on its own in a simple pressure-controlled breath.
**Why not D:** The observed fall does not establish a change in breath sequence; some modes have backup or adaptive behavior, so confirm the mode and settings.

**Q2 (judgment).** On a volume-controlled mode the peak airway pressure has risen over an hour. The patient is coughing and the tube sounds secretion-laden. What is the best nursing response?
A. Reduce the set tidal volume so that the displayed pressure comes down, and then see whether the alarms stop sounding.
B. Switch the mode to pressure control to avoid the high pressures, since that mode never produces a pressure alarm at all.
C. Silence the alarm and review the pressure again at the next hourly check, since a single rise in pressure is common.
D. Assess the patient and airway, manage secretions per policy, and report the pressure trend for team review.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Increased resistance can raise peak pressure; reduced compliance is another possible cause. Assess the patient and airway promptly, summon help for ineffective ventilation and involve trained staff. Compare peak and plateau pressures only when measurement conditions are appropriate.
**Why not A:** Changing set tidal volume is a prescriber and respiratory-therapist decision.
**Why not B:** Changing the mode is outside nursing scope.
**Why not C:** Waiting delays recognition of a blocked airway.

**Q3 (understanding).** Which statement about plateau pressure is consistent with the review?
A. It is measured in an end-inspiratory pause with no flow and approximates alveolar pressure when conditions are passive.
B. It is exactly the same as the peak inspiratory pressure in a healthy patient who is breathing without any effort.
C. It reflects the resistance of the endotracheal tube and does not depend on the lungs or the chest wall at all.
D. It is measured while the patient is coughing, because coughing produces the highest pressure that the system can reach.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Plateau pressure is read during an end-inspiratory pause with no flow, when it approximates alveolar pressure if the patient is passive. Coughing or active effort invalidates the pause, and the gap between peak and plateau reflects resistance.
**Why not B:** Peak and plateau differ by the resistive pressure.
**Why not C:** Plateau pressure reflects elastic, not resistive, load.
**Why not D:** A passive pause is needed; coughing invalidates it.

**Q4 (safety).** A patient on an assisted mode appears to fight the ventilator, and the monitor shows repeated breaths that do not match the patient's effort. The circuit has a small leak. What explanation does the review offer, and what should the nurse do?
A. The patient is simply anxious and needs a bolus of sedative before anything else is checked or reported to the team on duty.
B. The ventilator mode is unsuitable and should be changed by the nurse to a mandatory mode without waiting for any order.
C. Leaks and trigger sensitivity can cause false triggering and asynchrony, so the nurse checks the patient and circuit and reports.
D. The tidal volume must be too low, so the nurse should increase it on the ventilator to settle the patient down quickly.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The review says an overly sensitive trigger may cause false triggering from leaks, and that trigger problems can cause asynchrony.
**Why not A:** Sedating without assessment may mask a treatable cause.
**Why not B:** Mode changes are not nursing decisions.
**Why not D:** Setting changes are prescriber decisions.

**Q5 (understanding).** Which statement about the educational review is correct?
A. It is a clinical practice guideline that nurses should follow without any modification at the bedside in every unit.
B. It is a trade-name operating manual written for a single ventilator model used in one hospital.
C. It is an educational review with a selective overview and the authors' own interpretation, not a clinical guideline.
D. It prescribes the ventilator settings that should be used for every patient who needs mechanical ventilation.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The review itself says it is a selective overview and states the author's interpretation.
**Why not A:** It is not a guideline: the authors describe it as a selective overview with their interpretation.
**Why not B:** It is brand-neutral and gives trade names only as examples.
**Why not D:** It says optimal settings are patient-specific.

**Q6 (scope).** Which decision requires specific authorization or a credentialed protocol rather than routine bedside nursing assessment?
A. Recording the exhaled tidal volume and pressures at the interval the unit sets.
B. Checking that the tube is secure and the circuit connections are tight.
C. Reporting a patient who appears out of sync with the ventilator.
D. Choosing to change from a pressure-controlled to a volume-controlled mode.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** A mode change requires an authorized clinician or a credentialed practitioner acting under a valid protocol. Responsibilities differ internationally; routine bedside surveillance is not authority to independently change settings.
**Why not A:** Recording is a nursing task.
**Why not B:** Checking is a nursing task.
**Why not C:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
