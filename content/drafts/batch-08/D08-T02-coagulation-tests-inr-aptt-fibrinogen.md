# Coagulation tests – INR, aPTT and fibrinogen: specimens, pitfalls and what to report
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Built from a hospital laboratory handbook, a reference-laboratory educational page and a nursing textbook – not from a clinical guideline. Reference ranges and monitoring pathways are local.

## 1. Overview
A coagulation screen commonly includes the prothrombin time (PT) with its international normalised ratio (INR), the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT or APTT, sometimes as a ratio) and fibrinogen (the Sussex laboratory uses the Clauss method). The nurse's role is to take a good specimen, recognise what could make a result misleading, interpret results only as a prompt (not a diagnosis), watch trends and the patient, and report bleeding or concerning results promptly. Ordering, interpreting and acting on the results – including anticoagulant dose changes and reversal – belong to prescribers, pharmacists and the laboratory under local pathways.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe, at the level the sources allow, what INR, aPTT and fibrinogen are used for.
2. Describe correct specimen collection and the delay limit given in the laboratory handbook, and other causes of misleading results.
3. Explain why a single isolated abnormality is limited, and why trends and the whole picture (platelets, fibrinogen, D-dimer, clinical state) matter.
4. Describe how common anticoagulants and other conditions affect the tests, at the level of the ARUP resource.
5. State why reference intervals and monitoring pathways vary by institution.
6. Recognise bleeding concerns and escalate; separate nursing from prescriber, pharmacist and laboratory roles.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Critically ill patients often have abnormal clotting from sepsis, liver disease, anticoagulant drugs, vitamin K deficiency, trauma or consumption. The same number can mean different things in different patients. The nurse sees the bleeding, the lines and sites and the specimen collection.

## 6. Clinical assessment
**What the sources say the tests are for (limited):**
- **INR and PT:** INR is monitored with warfarin (Open RN: warfarin requires close laboratory monitoring with PT or INR; the textbook gives INR ranges that depend on the indication). PT prolongation is part of the ISTH overt DIC score (ARUP).
- **aPTT (PTT):** IV unfractionated heparin is titrated by hospital protocols against a PTT result (Open RN); the aPTT is prolonged by unfractionated heparin (ARUP).
- **Fibrinogen:** an acute phase reactant that can remain normal for a long time after DIC onset despite consumption; repeated measurement adds information (ARUP).
- **Clotting times in general:** the ARUP resource says PT and aPTT are prolonged in most cases of DIC but can be normal or shortened early in the course or in chronic DIC, and that clotting times may also be prolonged with anticoagulant drugs, liver disease, vitamin K deficiency or other causes.

**Specimen (Sussex handbook, one laboratory):** trisodium citrate blood in a blue-capped tube (3.5 mL tube; smaller tubes for children) taken with minimal stasis and **filled to the line**; the sample must be analysed or processed within **4 hours** of venepuncture; turnaround was one working day, or 2 hours if identified as urgent. The page does not explain why these rules exist.

**Potential causes of misleading results (what the sources support):**
- Delay or poor handling beyond the laboratory's limit (Sussex 4-hour statement).
- Under-filled tube or excessive stasis (Sussex instruction; the page does not state the effect).
- **Anticoagulants:** ARUP's table says unfractionated heparin prolongs the aPTT, warfarin prolongs the PT/INR, and the effect of direct oral anticoagulants on PT and aPTT depends on the drug, concentration and reagent; common anticoagulants generally do not affect the D-dimer; some assays can be affected by the reagent's heparin neutraliser at high drug levels. Do not infer more than this.
- **Disease:** liver disease, vitamin K deficiency, DIC and consumption (ARUP).
- **Sampling from a line that has been flushed or used for heparin** and other pre-analytical errors are general laboratory knowledge **not traced to the sources read** and are flagged for clinician and laboratory review.

**Limits of isolated abnormalities and the role of trends (ARUP DIC):** DIC is diagnosed with the ISTH scoring system using platelets, D-dimer, fibrinogen and PT in a patient with an underlying disorder, with a score of 5 or more compatible with overt DIC; repeat testing is important because the picture changes over time. A low platelet count is key but not specific; D-dimer is the best single test but is not used in isolation (a normal D-dimer has excellent negative predictive value; levels also rise with trauma or venous thromboembolism); in thrombotic microangiopathies such as TTP and HUS, coagulation tests are often normal or near normal. The ARUP resource is US-based and does not set local action thresholds.

**Reference intervals vary.** The Sussex page lists "locally determined" ranges (INR 0.8–1.2; aPTT 24.4–33.7 s; aPTT ratio 0.84–1.16; PT 11–17 s; fibrinogen 1.8–4.5 g/L) as that trust's values; other laboratories use different reagents and ranges. **Always use the range printed on your own laboratory report.**

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- **Collect correctly** following your laboratory's instructions: correct tube, correct fill to the line, minimal stasis, correct labelling and prompt transport within the laboratory's time limit.
- **Note on the request and tell the team** about anticoagulants given (including heparin infusions, flushes and recent doses), liver disease, vitamin K deficiency and recent transfusion where known, because they affect interpretation.
- **Report new or worsening bleeding** (oozing from lines, wounds or mucosa, bruising, blood in urine, stool or secretions, sudden changes in vital signs such as falling blood pressure with rising heart rate); the Open RN anticoagulant text advises notifying the prescriber immediately of new signs of bleeding or bruising or sudden changes that suggest internal bleeding, and following bleeding precautions.
- **Report critical or unexpected results** promptly using the laboratory's critical-value process and local escalation pathway.
- **Look at trends and the patient,** not only the latest number, and note the results next to platelet count, fibrinogen, D-dimer and the clinical picture as the team requests.
- **Do not adjust anticoagulant infusions or give reversal agents** except within an order or written protocol and training; the Open RN text names protamine for heparin reversal and vitamin K for warfarin reversal as prescriber-directed measures (doses not given here).
- **Know the local anticoagulant monitoring pathway** (which test, how often, who adjusts the dose); it varies by institution.
- **Watch for HIT features** such as a falling platelet count after heparin exposure (Open RN: a fall of 30 % or more from baseline is described) and report immediately.
- **Document** specimen time, results and trends, bleeding observations, drugs affecting clotting and communications.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
INR, aPTT (or ratio) and fibrinogen as ordered; platelet count; D-dimer if ordered; haemoglobin; bleeding at sites, lines, drains, wounds and mucosa; vital signs; urine output; anticoagulant drugs and times; liver function if relevant; the time of each sample and whether it was within the laboratory's time limit.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
New oozing from lines, wounds or mucosa; unexplained bruising; blood in urine, stool, vomit or secretions; sudden fall in blood pressure with rising heart rate; severe headache or falling consciousness in an anticoagulated patient (general clinical knowledge, flagged); a result far outside the laboratory's range; a rapid change between consecutive results; falling platelets or fibrinogen; a specimen that was delayed, under-filled or taken from a line used for anticoagulants; a patient with sepsis, liver disease or trauma and abnormal clotting.

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for active bleeding, haemodynamic change suggesting bleeding, critical results as defined locally, a rapid worsening trend, suspected HIT, or any problem with the specimen that could delay care.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, oozing from the central line site and a new bruise; the morning aPTT is much longer than the previous value. **B:** on an unfractionated heparin infusion since yesterday; platelets were normal. **A:** possible over-anticoagulation or another cause of bleeding. **R:** please review now; please advise on the infusion and further tests; the sample was taken correctly and within the time limit.

## 13. Potential complications
Bleeding, thrombosis, over- or under-anticoagulation, inappropriate treatment from a misleading result, HIT. The sources give limited detail; other complications are general clinical knowledge not traced to the sources read.

## 15. Practical bedside example
Before a morning coagulation screen the nurse checks the tube, fills it to the line, avoids prolonged tourniquet time, labels it, sends it straight to the laboratory, and notes on the request that the patient is on a heparin infusion; when the result is reported she compares it with the previous results and the patient's bleeding observations before telling the doctor.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 58-year-old with sepsis has oozing at venepuncture sites. The platelet count has fallen over two days, the INR and aPTT are longer than the laboratory's ranges, and the fibrinogen is still within range. The nurse reports the bleeding and the trend; the team recognises that normal fibrinogen does not exclude DIC, sends repeat tests and decides on management.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (application).** A nurse draws a citrate tube for a coagulation screen but fills it only halfway because the vein was difficult. According to the handbook she should know that:
A. The handbook says that half-filled tubes are always acceptable, so the sample can be sent as it is without comment.
B. The handbook says to fill to the line, so an under-filled tube is a specimen problem to report or repeat.
C. The result will be the same whatever the volume, so nobody needs to be told that the tube was only half full.
D. The sample should be topped up from a different tube that was taken earlier from the same patient for another test.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The handbook says the tube must be filled to the line; the page does not state the effect, so the safe action is to follow the instruction and tell the laboratory.
**Why not A:** The handbook does not say this.
**Why not C:** Unknown effects mean the result may be unreliable.
**Why not D:** Mixing tubes is not described and could cause error.

**Q2 (judgment).** A patient with sepsis has a prolonged INR and aPTT but the fibrinogen is within the laboratory's range, and platelets are falling. Which interpretation is consistent with the ARUP resource?
A. Normal fibrinogen proves that the patient does not have DIC, so the other abnormal results can be ignored by the team.
B. Clotting times are always shortened in DIC, so the prolonged INR and aPTT must point to another cause entirely.
C. A single normal fibrinogen means the other tests should be ignored, because fibrinogen is the only reliable marker of DIC.
D. Fibrinogen can stay normal for a long time after DIC begins, so the whole picture and trend matter, and the team decides.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** ARUP says fibrinogen is an acute phase reactant that can remain normal despite consumption and that DIC is judged by combined results and repeat testing.
**Why not A:** A normal fibrinogen does not exclude DIC.
**Why not B:** Clotting times are prolonged in most cases but can be normal or shortened early.
**Why not C:** No single test should be used alone.

**Q3 (safety).** A patient on a heparin infusion has a very prolonged aPTT and new oozing at the line site. What is the best nursing action?
A. Report the bleeding and result at once, check the specimen details, and wait for orders about the infusion.
B. Stop the heparin infusion on your own and give the reversal agent kept on the unit without asking anyone first.
C. Repeat the test tomorrow morning and record the oozing in the notes so that the day team can see it then.
D. Increase the pressure on the dressing and tell the doctor about it at the end of the shift or at handover.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Heparin prolongs the aPTT; new bleeding must be reported immediately, and infusion or reversal decisions need an order or protocol.
**Why not B:** Reversal and infusion changes need an order or protocol.
**Why not C:** Waiting risks harm.
**Why not D:** Bleeding must be reported promptly.

**Q4 (understanding).** Why can a reference interval for the INR or aPTT not be applied universally?
A. All laboratories use identical reagents and ranges, so no variation exists between hospitals or between countries at all.
B. The Sussex page says its ranges are locally determined, so ranges differ and the range on your report applies.
C. Ranges are decided by the nurse at the bedside for each patient, depending on how unwell the patient looks that day.
D. Ranges are the same everywhere for adults, and only differ for children because of their smaller blood volume.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The handbook states "locally determined"; ranges depend on laboratory methods.
**Why not A:** The page states the opposite.
**Why not C:** The laboratory sets ranges.
**Why not D:** Ranges are local for adults too.

**Q5 (application).** A patient is on warfarin. Which statement matches the Open RN anticoagulant text?
A. Warfarin does not need any laboratory monitoring, because its effect is predictable and does not change between patients.
B. Warfarin is monitored by the PTT and its effect is reversed with protamine, which is given by slow infusion.
C. Warfarin needs close monitoring with PT or INR, and vitamin K is the reversal agent (a prescriber decision).
D. Warfarin is monitored by the platelet count alone, which is checked once a week in every patient who takes it.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The textbook says warfarin requires close monitoring with PT or INR and names vitamin K as the reversal agent.
**Why not A:** It requires monitoring.
**Why not B:** That describes heparin.
**Why not D:** Platelets do not monitor warfarin.

**Q6 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber, pharmacist and laboratory rather than the bedside nurse?
A. Filling the citrate tube to the line and labelling it correctly with the patient's identifiers and the time.
B. Reporting new bleeding to the responsible clinician immediately, together with the latest results and trends.
C. Noting on the request form that the patient is receiving a heparin infusion and when the last dose was given.
D. Setting the target, interpreting results diagnostically and deciding on reversal, with pharmacist advice.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Interpretation, targets and reversal are prescriber and pharmacist decisions with laboratory advice.
**Why not A:** Specimen collection is a nursing task.
**Why not B:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not C:** Providing clinical information is a nursing responsibility.
