# Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) in AKI – principles and introductory circuit monitoring
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. KDIGO 2012 informs prescribing principles; selected filter-pressure concepts come from a single hospital teaching resource. This is NOT a CRRT operating procedure, device-specific alarm guide or competency sign-off.

## 1. Overview
Renal replacement therapy (RRT) replaces kidney function in AKI. CRRT runs slowly and continuously and is one of the modalities KDIGO describes. KDIGO's Section 5 gives recommendations on when to start and stop RRT, anticoagulation, dialysis catheter access, membranes, choice between continuous and intermittent therapy, buffer and fluid quality, and dose. Decisions on whether, when and how to give RRT belong to the nephrologist or intensivist; the nurse's role in CRRT is to manage the patient and the delivery at the bedside. Because KDIGO is a prescribing guideline, this draft explains what the recommendations mean for nursing observation and safety, not how to operate a machine.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. State the KDIGO 2012 statements on when RRT is started and stopped.
2. Explain why CRRT is suggested for haemodynamically unstable patients and patients with raised intracranial pressure.
3. Describe the KDIGO statements on anticoagulation, including the HIT statement and its nursing implications.
4. Describe the KDIGO statements on dialysis catheter insertion and care that affect nursing (ultrasound, chest radiograph before first use, no topical antibiotics or antibiotic locks).
5. Explain why the delivered dose must be assessed frequently.
6. Separate nursing, prescriber and pharmacist responsibilities; state clearly what this draft does not cover.
7. Explain conceptually what changing filter-pressure trends can and cannot establish.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Patients on CRRT are among the sickest in the ICU. The therapy changes fluid balance, electrolytes, acid–base, drug clearance and bleeding risk continuously, and interruptions mean under-delivery. Nurses see the patient and the machine together and are best placed to notice problems.

## 6. Clinical assessment (KDIGO 2012 Section 5 statements)
- **Timing.** Initiate RRT emergently when life-threatening changes in fluid, electrolyte and acid–base balance exist (not graded). Consider the broader clinical context, conditions that RRT can modify and trends in laboratory results, rather than single urea or creatinine thresholds (not graded).
- **Stopping.** Discontinue RRT when it is no longer required because kidney function has recovered enough or because RRT no longer fits the goals of care (not graded). KDIGO suggests not using diuretics to enhance recovery or shorten RRT (2B).
- **Modality.** Use continuous and intermittent RRT as complementary therapies (not graded); KDIGO suggests CRRT rather than standard intermittent RRT for haemodynamically unstable patients (2B) and for AKI with acute brain injury or other causes of raised intracranial pressure or generalised brain oedema (2B).
- **Anticoagulation.** The decision should rest on the patient's bleeding risk and benefit. Without increased bleeding risk or impaired coagulation and not on other anticoagulation, KDIGO recommends using anticoagulation during RRT (1B) and, for CRRT, suggests regional citrate anticoagulation rather than heparin if there is no contraindication to citrate (2B), with unfractionated or low-molecular-weight heparin if citrate is contraindicated (2C). With increased bleeding risk, it suggests regional citrate rather than no anticoagulation (2C) and avoiding regional heparinisation (2C). **In heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), all heparin must be stopped and a direct thrombin inhibitor or Factor Xa inhibitor is recommended (1A).** The KDIGO statement addresses established HIT; a new platelet-count fall warrants prompt team-led probability assessment rather than assuming all thrombocytopenia is HIT.
- **Vascular access.** KDIGO suggests starting RRT via an uncuffed non-tunnelled catheter (2D); vein preference (not graded): right jugular first, femoral second, left jugular third, subclavian last; it recommends ultrasound guidance for insertion (1A), a chest radiograph promptly after placement and before first use of an internal jugular or subclavian catheter (1B), and suggests not using topical antibiotics at the insertion site (2C) or antibiotic locks (2C) for non-tunnelled dialysis catheters in ICU patients.
- **Membrane, buffer and fluids.** Biocompatible membrane suggested (2C); bicarbonate rather than lactate buffer is suggested in AKI (2C), recommended in circulatory shock (1B), suggested in liver failure or lactic acidaemia (2B); dialysis and replacement fluids should at least comply with AAMI standards for bacteria and endotoxin (1B).
- **Dose.** The dose to be delivered should be prescribed before starting each session (not graded), and KDIGO recommends frequent assessment of the **actual delivered dose** in order to adjust the prescription (1B). It also states goals of electrolyte, acid–base, solute and fluid balance that meet the patient's needs. Its numeric dose targets are not quoted here.

## 6b. Introductory circuit-monitoring concepts (LHSC local teaching page; NOT machine instructions)
- **Transmembrane pressure (TMP)** reflects the pressure difference across the dialysis membrane. Under comparable flow conditions, a rising trend may suggest protein accumulation, membrane fouling or clotting; it is not a specific diagnosis or an instruction to change the filter.
- **Pressure drop across the filter** is another trend clinicians may use when considering blood-path resistance. Do not infer filter clotting from a single reading; the flow rate, current prescription, access and return lines and other alarms influence interpretation.
- **A sudden change** in these trends requires timely patient and circuit assessment with a trained CRRT operator. The LHSC page mentions possible effluent-line obstruction and changes in flow as alternative explanations for changing TMP. The nurse should document the observations and escalate under the local protocol, rather than blindly clearing alarms or altering pump settings.
- **Applicability:** the LHSC web page reflects a specific centre and its equipment. Its numerical alarm thresholds, access manoeuvres, priming, anticoagulation and prescribed treatment changes are deliberately NOT taught here. For device-specific concepts see skill draft SK-17; actual bedside actions require the unit's current approved procedure and manufacturer manual.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities (implications; not instructions for operating a machine)
- **Know the prescription** (modality, dose, fluid balance goal, anticoagulation) and check that the delivered treatment matches it; report interruptions and under-delivery because KDIGO recommends frequent assessment of the delivered dose.
- **Recognise and communicate pressure trends** (TMP and filter-pressure drop) alongside patient status, therapy settings, delivered treatment and downtime; do not use borrowed numerical thresholds or undertake unsupervised CRRT troubleshooting (LHSC educational source; see section 6b).
- **Check access before use:** make sure the chest radiograph required after internal jugular or subclavian catheter placement has been done and reviewed before first use, as KDIGO recommends; report if it is missing.
- **Catheter care:** follow the unit's dialysis catheter care policy; KDIGO suggests no topical antibiotics at the site and no antibiotic locks for non-tunnelled catheters in ICU, so do not add them without an order. Infection prevention content is in D06-T04.
- **Anticoagulation safety:** know which anticoagulant is ordered; monitor for bleeding and the coagulation tests ordered; **if HIT is considered likely on urgent clinical probability assessment or is confirmed, escalate immediately for discontinuation of *all* heparin sources, including circuit anticoagulation, line flushes and catheter locks, and for clinician-directed alternative anticoagulation.** A platelet fall alone does not confirm HIT. Current ASH guidance distinguishes low-probability suspected HIT (where empiric heparin discontinuation is generally not recommended) from intermediate/high-probability cases; the treating team applies the diagnostic pathway and orders. A local protocol determines the implementation and replacement product, **not whether heparin flushes count as heparin exposure.**
- **Monitor the patient:** haemodynamics, temperature, fluid balance, electrolytes and acid–base results, bleeding, mental state, access site; report trends. The guideline's goals (electrolyte, acid–base, solute and fluid balance) are set by the team.
- **Fluid and buffer:** use only the fluids ordered; the choice of bicarbonate versus lactate buffer is a prescriber decision, and fluids must meet AAMI-type quality standards (pharmacy and manufacturer responsibility).
- **Document** prescription, delivered dose and downtime, fluid balance, anticoagulation, site checks, alarms and actions, results and communications.
- **Not covered and flagged:** machine set-up, priming, connecting and disconnecting, circuit and filter clotting, alarms and troubleshooting, citrate and ionised-calcium monitoring, temperature loss, drug dosing changes during CRRT, nutrition, family communication, equipment-specific training. These require manufacturer documentation and clinical review.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Haemodynamics; temperature; urine output (if any) and net fluid balance against the prescription; electrolytes (potassium, calcium, phosphate, magnesium), acid–base and glucose as ordered; coagulation tests and platelet count as ordered; bleeding; mental state; dialysis catheter site and function; downtime and delivered dose; alarm history; trends in TMP and filter-pressure drop at comparable settings, interpreted by a trained operator.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Bleeding or oozing at the catheter site or elsewhere; a fall in platelet count (HIT concern); haemodynamic instability on starting or during therapy; arrhythmia or electrolyte abnormality; new confusion; catheter site redness, discharge or fever; frequent or prolonged interruptions to the therapy; fluid balance that does not match the prescription; line disconnection or air (device-specific emergency steps are not covered).

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate for bleeding, haemodynamic instability, suspected HIT, arrhythmia, abnormal electrolytes, catheter problems, repeated downtime, or any discrepancy between the prescription and the delivered treatment.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, CRRT stopped three times in the last four hours and the net fluid balance is behind the prescription. **B:** acute kidney injury in septic shock; prescription written this morning. **A:** under-delivery of the prescribed therapy. **R:** please review the prescription and the access and tell me whether to change the plan; the delivered dose needs to be reassessed.

## 13. Potential complications
Bleeding (anticoagulation), catheter-related infection, HIT, haemodynamic instability, electrolyte disturbance, under-delivery of the prescribed dose. KDIGO's statements address these in prescribing terms; other complications are not described in the sources read.

## 14. Documentation
Prescription and any changes; start, stop and interruption times; delivered dose and fluid balance; anticoagulant used and its monitoring; catheter site and checks; laboratory results; alarms and actions; escalations; patient response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At handover the nurse reviews the CRRT prescription and the anticoagulation order, checks that the access position was confirmed on a chest radiograph before first use, reviews the last hours' downtime and net fluid balance, and checks the platelet count and electrolyte results due, reporting anything that does not match the plan.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (understanding).** For which patients does KDIGO 2012 suggest using CRRT rather than standard intermittent RRT?
A. Patients who are well, walking and eating, so that the treatment can be carried out outside the unit.
B. Haemodynamically unstable patients, and patients with acute brain injury or raised intracranial pressure.
C. Patients who have no urine output and no sign of any other organ dysfunction in the ICU.
D. Paediatric patients, because CRRT is not used in adults who have acute kidney injury.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** KDIGO suggests CRRT for haemodynamically unstable patients (2B) and for brain injury or raised intracranial pressure (2B).
**Why not A:** Stable, well patients are not a group the guideline singles out for CRRT.
**Why not C:** Urine output alone is not the criterion.
**Why not D:** The guideline addresses adults with acute kidney injury.

**Q2 (safety).** A patient receiving heparin during CRRT develops a rapid platelet-count fall, and the treating team considers heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) likely. What does KDIGO 2012 recommend?
A. Continue heparin at a higher rate to keep the circuit open while the cause of the fall is investigated.
B. Heparin may continue in the circuit itself but must be stopped in the flushes and locks.
C. No change is needed because HIT does not occur during renal replacement therapy in an ICU patient.
D. All heparin must be stopped, and a direct thrombin or Factor Xa inhibitor is recommended instead.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** KDIGO 2012 recommendation 5.3.4 (1A) states that all heparin must be stopped in HIT and that a non-heparin direct thrombin or Factor Xa inhibitor should be used for RRT anticoagulation. Its rationale adds that when HIT is considered *likely*, all heparin exposures, including catheter locks, need urgent review and discontinuation. Drug selection and treatment decisions require the responsible clinical team and local HIT pathway; a falling platelet count alone does not establish HIT.
**Why not A:** Heparin worsens HIT, so continuing or increasing it would harm the patient.
**Why not B:** The statement is that all heparin must be stopped, not part of it.
**Why not C:** HIT can occur with heparin exposure in RRT.

**Q3 (application).** A new right internal jugular dialysis catheter has been inserted and the team plans CRRT. According to KDIGO **2012** recommendation 5.4.4 (rather than a universal protocol for every modern placement technique), what imaging does that guideline recommend before first use?
A. A chest radiograph promptly after placement and before first use.
B. An antibiotic lock instilled in the catheter before the first use, to prevent infection of the new line.
C. A topical antibiotic ointment applied to the insertion site before the first use of the catheter.
D. No checks of any kind, because ultrasound guidance makes any later radiograph unnecessary.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** KDIGO 2012 recommendation 5.4.4 (1B) specifies a chest radiograph promptly after placing a jugular or subclavian dialysis catheter and before first use. This is an accurate statement of the cited 2012 guideline; current institution-specific verification pathways and newer evidence require separate review before translating it into a universal bedside rule.
**Why not B:** KDIGO suggests not using antibiotic locks (2C).
**Why not C:** KDIGO suggests not using topical antibiotics (2C).
**Why not D:** Ultrasound guidance is recommended in addition to, not instead of, the radiograph.

**Q4 (judgment).** On a unit running CRRT, the prescription is not being delivered because the therapy has been interrupted several times. What is the best action?
A. Say nothing about it, since the prescribed dose is only a target and not really important to the patient.
B. Increase the machine settings yourself to make up the lost time, without waiting for an order to do so.
C. Document and report the interruptions and the amount delivered so that the prescription can be reviewed.
D. Stop the therapy for the rest of the shift to avoid more interruptions to the treatment.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** KDIGO recommends frequent assessment of the actual delivered dose in order to adjust the prescription (1B).
**Why not A:** Under-delivery affects the patient's treatment.
**Why not B:** Changes to the prescription require an order.
**Why not D:** Stopping the therapy without an order is unsafe.

**Q5 (understanding).** Which statement matches KDIGO 2012 on antibiotic use for non-tunnelled dialysis catheters in ICU patients?
A. Topical antibiotics should be applied to every catheter insertion site routinely to prevent infection of the site.
B. Antibiotic locks are recommended for all non-tunnelled dialysis catheters in every ICU patient with AKI.
C. It suggests not using topical antibiotics at the insertion site or antibiotic locks to prevent catheter infection.
D. A prophylactic course of systemic antibiotics should be given for the whole duration of CRRT in all patients.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** KDIGO suggests not using topical antibiotics at the insertion site and not using antibiotic locks to prevent infection of non-tunnelled dialysis catheters. Each distractor misstates one of these suggestions.
**Why not A:** The suggestion is against topical antibiotics.
**Why not B:** The suggestion is against antibiotic locks.
**Why not D:** The guideline makes no such statement in the sections read.

**Q6 (scope).** Which decision belongs to the prescriber (nephrologist or intensivist) rather than the bedside nurse?
A. Reporting that the delivered dose is lower than the prescription to the nurse in charge.
B. Deciding the anticoagulation strategy, such as regional citrate or heparin.
C. Documenting downtime and the net fluid balance.
D. Checking that the catheter site is clean and dry.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Choosing the anticoagulation strategy for CRRT, such as regional citrate or heparin, is a prescribing decision. Reporting, documenting and checking the site are nursing responsibilities.
**Why not A:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not C:** Documentation is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not D:** Site checks are a nursing responsibility.
