# Potassium disorders – acute hyperkalaemia in hospital (hypokalaemia only briefly)
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Covers hyperkalaemia from a UK guideline; hypokalaemia management is NOT supported by a guideline read and is left to local policy. Drug doses are intentionally absent.

## 1. Overview
Hyperkalaemia – a raised serum potassium (K⁺) – is a common medical emergency when it presents acutely. The UK Kidney Association (UKKA) guideline defines it as K⁺ above 5.5 mmol/L (adopting the European Resuscitation Council definition) and grades it as mild (5.5–5.9), moderate (6.0–6.4) and severe (6.5 or above). Severe hyperkalaemia risks arrhythmias and cardiac arrest. The nurse recognises the risk, makes sure the result is reliable, obtains and monitors the ECG and rhythm, helps give and monitors the ordered treatments, watches for treatment complications such as hypoglycaemia and rebound, and escalates. Diagnosis, treatment choices and doses are prescriber decisions, with pharmacist input.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. State the UKKA definition and severity bands, and explain why the numbers alone do not set the urgency.
2. Describe how nurses help ensure a potassium result is reliable (pseudohyperkalaemia, haemolysis, sampling site).
3. Describe the ECG and rhythm monitoring the guideline recommends, and what an ECG can and cannot show.
4. Describe the five-step treatment framework at a level a nurse needs (protect the heart, shift potassium into cells, remove it from the body, monitor, identify the cause and prevent recurrence), without drugs' doses.
5. Describe the monitoring after treatment: repeat potassium, glucose, rhythm.
6. Separate nursing responsibilities from prescribing; state what these sources do not cover (hypokalaemia, replacement).

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Patients in ICU often have kidney injury, acidosis, potassium-raising drugs and tissue injury. The guideline notes in-hospital mortality is higher with hyperkalaemia (18.1 % in one cohort) than with hypokalaemia or normokalaemia, and above 30 % has been reported at 6.5 mmol/L or more. Arrhythmias can occur without warning, and the nurse at the monitor is often the first to see them.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Confirm the number.** Pseudohyperkalaemia is a laboratory artefact. UKKA lists prolonged transit, poor storage, difficult venepuncture, haemolysis, high platelet count, contamination with EDTA through the wrong order of draw, and sampling from an arm where potassium-containing fluid is infusing as causes. It recommends paired lithium-heparin and clotted samples from a large vein with gentle traction and prompt analysis when pseudohyperkalaemia is suspected (1A), a lithium-heparin sample when rapid results are needed (1B), and a point-of-care blood gas analyser result for potassium in emergencies while the laboratory result is awaited (1B). Haemolysis is a confounder, especially in whole-blood samples.
- **Urgent clinical assessment** with an early warning score (1C) and a history and examination to find the cause (1B), including the medicines the patient takes.
- **ECG.** UKKA recommends a 12-lead ECG in hospitalised patients with a high potassium and continuous ECG monitoring (at minimum 3-lead) for patients with potassium of 6.5 mmol/L or more, with hyperkalaemic features on a 12-lead ECG, or with potassium 6.0–6.4 who are unwell or likely to rise rapidly, ideally in a higher-dependency setting (1C). **Inconsistency on the guideline website:** the summary page states the 12-lead threshold as 6.5 mmol/L (guideline 14.1), while the detailed recommendation page states 6.0 mmol/L. The draft does not choose; use the local protocol.
- **What the ECG shows and does not.** The rationale describes progressive changes (peaked T waves, flattening of P waves, PR and QRS prolongation, bradycardia, sine-wave pattern) but notes that peaked T waves alone are rarely a sign of life-threatening hyperkalaemia, that changes do not always occur in sequence, and that the ECG can be normal or atypical (for example a pseudo-STEMI pattern) in severe hyperkalaemia. It reports that the ECG can help rule in but cannot rule out hyperkalaemia, and that its value depends on the interpreter. ECG signs most closely correlated with adverse events were QRS prolongation, bradycardia below 50 and junctional rhythms. Arrhythmias can occur at any time without prior toxic ECG changes. Patients with pacemakers are not protected: high potassium can widen the paced QRS and raise capture thresholds.
- **BRASH syndrome:** bradycardia, renal failure, AV-node-blocking drugs, shock and hyperkalaemia – described as refractory bradycardia and instability, often at potassium below 6.5.
- **Symptoms:** the guideline stresses the ECG and rhythm rather than symptoms. Weakness and palpitations are not covered in the pages read.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- **Act on a critical potassium result immediately** and tell the responsible clinician; do not wait for the next round.
- **Put the patient on continuous cardiac monitoring** as the local protocol and the thresholds above require, and obtain a 12-lead ECG as ordered; know what changes to report (widening QRS, bradycardia, new block, wide-complex rhythm, sine-wave pattern).
- **Check the sample:** know how the sample was taken (site, delay, haemolysis, infusion arm) and tell the clinician if the result is doubtful; do not delay treating a patient who has ECG changes while the result is repeated.
- **Prepare for the five-step approach** the guideline recommends (1B): (1) protect the heart with intravenous calcium when ECG changes are present; (2) shift potassium into cells with insulin and glucose infusion, with salbutamol as an adjunct, not alone; (3) remove potassium from the body with potassium binders or dialysis (the guideline's step 3 text read names binders; dialysis is covered in its separate recommendations); (4) monitor with blood tests; (5) identify the cause and prevent recurrence – the guideline's rationale says treatment is not complete until the cause is identified and steps are taken to prevent recurrence, and that one should never walk away without completing all five steps. UKKA recommends calcium chloride in cardiac arrest or peri-arrest and calcium gluconate otherwise (1C). Calcium salts can cause tissue necrosis if they extravasate (more with the chloride salt), hypotension, flushing and bradycardia or arrhythmias, mainly after too rapid administration; check the line, the site and the rhythm and report adverse effects.
- **Prevent hypoglycaemia after insulin and glucose.** UKKA recommends blood glucose measurement at regular intervals after the infusion in all patients (at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300 and 360 minutes; 1C) and a glucose infusion afterwards when the pre-treatment glucose is below 7.0 mmol/L (2B). Hypoglycaemia is the most common adverse event; it occurred in around one in five patients in the studies cited and the risk lasts up to six hours. Its signs (sweating, palpitations, tremor, hunger, then confusion or coma) may be masked in sedated patients, so rely on the measurements.
- **Repeat potassium** – UKKA suggests at least 1, 2, 4, 6 and 24 hours after treatment of moderate or severe hyperkalaemia (2C) to check the effect and to detect rebound as the shifting effect wears off (1B). Peak efficacy of several drugs is at 1–2 hours.
- **Avoid unneeded treatments:** UKKA recommends calcium resonium (cation-exchange resin) is no longer routinely used (2B) and suggests intravenous sodium bicarbonate is not used routinely (2C); these are prescriber decisions the nurse should understand.
- **Dialysis patients:** UKKA recommends urgent dialysis for severe hyperkalaemia in haemodialysis patients (1A), with calcium given for toxic ECG changes even when dialysis is immediately available (1C). For cardiac arrest, it recommends disconnection from dialysis equipment before defibrillation unless the machine is defibrillator-proof (1C).
- **Cardiac arrest:** hyperkalaemia should be considered as a reversible cause in every cardiac arrest (1A).
- **Escalate:** severe hyperkalaemia should be referred urgently to renal or critical care (2C); patients with airway, breathing, circulation or conscious-level problems go to critical care first (1C); referral should be made by a senior member of the team if escalation is needed from the outset or the patient fails to respond (1B). Decisions on dialysis for life-threatening hyperkalaemia are taken urgently by a nephrologist or critical care specialist (1C).
- **Prevention in hospital:** medicines that raise potassium are reviewed in the context of the illness and renal function (1B); dietary measures after non-dietary causes (constipation, acidosis, poorly controlled diabetes) are dealt with (1C); community monitoring is arranged on discharge (1B). These are team actions; the nurse reports problems and educates.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Continuous ECG and rhythm; 12-lead ECG as ordered; heart rate and blood pressure; serum potassium at the intervals ordered (guideline suggestion above); blood glucose at the intervals above after insulin and glucose; urine output and renal function; arterial or venous blood gas if ordered; line and site checks during calcium infusion; pacemaker capture if the patient has one; mental state and signs of hypoglycaemia.

## 8b. Potassium replacement safety (Phase 3; expert consensus, not a guideline; hypokalaemia treatment still unsupported)
A 2026 study of 440 potassium-related incident reports from Finnish ICUs over 11 years found that **34 % involved a wrong concentration and 24 % a wrong medication**; 26 % were reported to have caused consequences for patients (for example electrolyte disturbance, extravasation, infiltration or skin irritation), and the expert panel rated most errors in a sample as more serious than the original classifiers did. The introduction notes international bodies' advice to use ready-to-use potassium solutions rather than concentrates on conventional wards, with concentrates allowed in critical care only after rigorous risk assessment. The panel's **consensus safeguards** across the medication process included: prescribing in the electronic system by a physician (verbal prescribing only in emergencies with closed-loop communication); standardised potassium replacement concentrates and decision-support alerts that consider the type of cannula and the potassium content; generic names or TALLman lettering; storing concentrates separately with high-risk labels; barcode-enabled dispensing; clear "contains potassium" labels, a "via central line" label on strong solutions and labels at both ends of the tubing; oral or enteral syringes that cannot be connected to intravenous lines; smart pumps with a drug library and restrictions; barcode checks when changing syringes or bags; tidy pump areas with unnecessary infusions removed; digital checklists at shift change; pharmacist education and ward-round presence; and adequate nurse staffing to avoid preparing infusions in a rushed way at the bedside. **Nursing implications (author's translation):** check drug, concentration, route and access type against the order, use the labelling and pump library, never prepare or give concentrate outside local rules, and report incidents and near misses. **Not supported by any source read:** hypokalaemia recognition thresholds, replacement rates and routes, monitoring during replacement, and magnesium correction; these remain flagged for clinician and pharmacist authorship.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Potassium of 6.5 mmol/L or more; any ECG change of hyperkalaemia (widened QRS, bradycardia, loss of P waves, sine-wave pattern); new bradycardia or heart block, especially in a patient on AV-nodal drugs; wide-complex tachycardia; a rising potassium in a patient with impaired kidney function (the guideline names impaired kidney function as the commonest cause); acidosis as a non-dietary cause the guideline says to address (recommendation 21.2); other causes not detailed in the pages read are flagged for clinician review; hypoglycaemia after treatment; rebound potassium after the first response.

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for severe hyperkalaemia, ECG changes, arrhythmia, bradycardia, deterioration, failure to respond to treatment, or a need for dialysis.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, potassium 6.8 mmol/L with a widening QRS and a heart rate of 44. **B:** acute kidney injury, on an ACE inhibitor, oliguric; sample was not haemolysed. **A:** severe hyperkalaemia with ECG changes and bradycardia. **R:** please attend now; I have connected the monitor, obtained a 12-lead ECG and prepared for calcium and insulin and glucose per your orders; please decide on dialysis.

## 13. Potential complications
Bradycardia, heart block, sine-wave rhythm, ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac arrest; treatment complications named by UKKA: hypoglycaemia after insulin and glucose, rebound hyperkalaemia, tissue necrosis from calcium extravasation, hypotension, flushing, bradycardia from rapid calcium.

## 14. Documentation
Result with time and sample type; sample quality concerns; ECG findings and times; monitoring started; drugs given with times, rates as ordered and double-check; line and site checks; blood glucose values; repeat potassium results; communication and escalation times; patient response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
A laboratory calls a potassium of 6.7 mmol/L in a patient with acute kidney injury. The nurse connects the cardiac monitor, records a 12-lead ECG, checks the sample type and whether the patient has an infusion in the sampling arm, tells the doctor and the nurse in charge, prepares the line and monitor for the ordered treatments, and sets reminders for the timed glucose and potassium samples.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 59-year-old woman with acute kidney injury has a potassium of 6.9 mmol/L and a new wide-complex bradycardia. The nurse calls for help, the team treats her per the algorithm, and the nurse monitors the rhythm, checks glucose at the listed times, finds a glucose of 3.4 mmol/L at 90 minutes, reports it at once and the doctor orders a rescue, then documents all values.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (understanding).** How does the UKKA guideline classify a serum potassium of 6.2 mmol/L?
A. Mild hyperkalaemia, because the guideline regards only values above 7.0 mmol/L as moderate or severe in practice.
B. Severe hyperkalaemia, because every value above 6.0 mmol/L is classed in the guideline's severe band without exception.
C. Moderate hyperkalaemia, because the 6.0–6.4 band is the guideline's moderate category.
D. Normal, because the guideline's threshold for calling a serum potassium level hyperkalaemia is a value above 6.5 mmol/L.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The guideline defines hyperkalaemia as above 5.5 mmol/L and classes 6.0–6.4 as moderate.
**Why not A:** The mild band is 5.5–5.9.
**Why not B:** Severe starts at 6.5 mmol/L or more.
**Why not D:** The threshold for hyperkalaemia is 5.5, not 6.5.

**Q2 (judgment).** A potassium of 7.1 mmol/L is reported on a sample that took four hours to reach the laboratory, in a patient who looks well with a normal ECG. What is the best nursing action?
A. Report it, explain the sample delay and ECG, and help repeat the potassium (for example by point-of-care analysis), while keeping the patient monitored.
B. Ignore the result, because a delayed sample is wrong and the patient looks well and has a normal ECG.
C. Start treatment without telling anyone, because a potassium above 7.0 mmol/L is an emergency whatever the sample looks like.
D. Delay the report until the next scheduled blood test, to avoid disturbing the team about a result that may be spurious.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Prolonged transit is a recognised cause of pseudohyperkalaemia, but a normal ECG does not exclude real hyperkalaemia, so the nurse reports, rechecks and monitors.
**Why not B:** The result cannot be dismissed without checking.
**Why not C:** Treatment decisions belong to the prescriber and need an order.
**Why not D:** A critical result must be reported promptly.

**Q3 (safety).** A patient has been treated with insulin and glucose infusion for severe hyperkalaemia. Which monitoring does UKKA recommend?
A. Blood glucose if the patient has diabetes, because people with diabetes become hypoglycaemic after insulin.
B. Blood glucose once, at the end of the infusion, and then if the patient reports feeling unwell afterwards.
C. No further blood tests, because the potassium has already been shifted into cells and rebound does not occur.
D. Regular blood glucose measurements at set intervals after the infusion, and repeat potassium at set times.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** UKKA recommends glucose at regular intervals (0 to 360 minutes) in all patients and repeat potassium (1, 2, 4, 6, 24 hours) because of hypoglycaemia and rebound risk.
**Why not A:** The guideline notes adverse outcomes of hypoglycaemia regardless of diabetic status.
**Why not B:** A single check misses later hypoglycaemia, up to six hours after treatment.
**Why not C:** The guideline specifically warns of rebound hyperkalaemia.

**Q4 (application).** On the monitor a patient with a potassium of 7.4 mmol/L has a heart rate falling to 40 and a widening QRS. What is the most appropriate immediate nursing response?
A. Note it on the chart and recheck the rhythm at the next routine observation, since the patient has no symptoms yet.
B. Call for urgent help and report the rhythm and potassium, as ECG changes mean treatment is needed.
C. Ask the family to come in so that they can be told that the monitor is faulty and that the numbers can be disregarded.
D. Silence the alarm, since bradycardia is common in hospital patients and usually does not need any immediate action.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Bradycardia and QRS widening are the ECG signs most associated with adverse events; severe hyperkalaemia with ECG change needs urgent treatment.
**Why not A:** Waiting could allow progression to arrest.
**Why not C:** Nothing suggests a fault and it delays care.
**Why not D:** Silencing it ignores a high-risk rhythm.

**Q5 (understanding).** Which statement about the ECG in hyperkalaemia matches the UKKA rationale?
A. A normal ECG proves that the potassium is safe, so no treatment is ever needed.
B. Peaked T waves alone are a reliable sign of life-threatening hyperkalaemia in every patient.
C. The ECG can help rule hyperkalaemia in but cannot rule it out, and can be normal in severe cases.
D. The ECG always shows changes in a fixed sequence as the potassium rises.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The rationale says the ECG changes can be absent or atypical, and that its sensitivity is low while specificity is high.
**Why not A:** The ECG may be normal even in severe hyperkalaemia.
**Why not B:** Peaked T waves alone are rarely a sign of life-threatening hyperkalaemia.
**Why not D:** The changes do not always occur in sequence.

**Q6 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber with pharmacist input rather than the bedside nurse?
A. Choosing the calcium salt, the dose of insulin and glucose, and whether to add a binder.
B. Checking the line and site during an ordered calcium infusion and reporting swelling.
C. Measuring blood glucose at the timed intervals after insulin and glucose.
D. Connecting the cardiac monitor and recording a 12-lead ECG as ordered.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Selecting drugs and doses is a prescribing decision.
**Why not B:** Site checks and reporting are nursing actions.
**Why not C:** Timed monitoring is a nursing task.
**Why not D:** Monitoring and ECG are nursing tasks.

## 20. Evidence, applicability and open questions
- Source quality: the UKKA guideline is a national clinical practice guideline (GRADE-style 1A–2C grading), updated July 2026, with extensive rationale. The website notes that the evidence for IV calcium is poor (ILCOR 2025 found no evidence for or against) and that recommendation grades were adjusted accordingly. It is a UK guideline for adults.
- Internal inconsistency: threshold for 12-lead ECG (6.5 versus 6.0 mmol/L) differs between pages of the same website.
- Limits: HYPOKALAEMIA management, potassium replacement protocols (only error and safeguard data were added in Phase 3), paediatric hyperkalaemia and chronic hyperkalaemia in the community were not drawn on for nursing content; the Open RN chapter is a general textbook only. ISMP lists concentrated potassium chloride injection as a high-alert drug (see D05-T16) but supplies no safeguards.
- Not covered: appendices' drug dosing tables (omitted), laboratory procedures, dietary advice details.
- Open questions for reviewers (NEPH, CCP, PH, NE, BN): local potassium thresholds for monitoring; hypokalaemia and replacement content; the 12-lead ECG threshold; the nurse's role in glucose monitoring protocols.
