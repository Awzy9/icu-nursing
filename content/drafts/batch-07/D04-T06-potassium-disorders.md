# Potassium disorders – hyperkalaemia and introductory hypokalaemia safety
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Hyperkalaemia is based on UKKA 2026; introductory hypokalaemia recognition and safety are drawn from UK NHS SPS 2024 specialist pharmacy advice. Treatment orders follow local policy. No drug doses are provided.

## 1. Overview
Hyperkalaemia – a raised serum potassium (K⁺) – is a common medical emergency when it presents acutely. The UK Kidney Association (UKKA) guideline defines it as K⁺ ≥ 5.5 mmol/L (adopting the European Resuscitation Council definition) and grades it as mild (5.5–5.9), moderate (6.0–6.4) and severe (6.5 or above). Severe hyperkalaemia risks arrhythmias and cardiac arrest. The nurse recognises the risk, makes sure the result is reliable, obtains and monitors the ECG and rhythm, helps give and monitors the ordered treatments, watches for treatment complications such as hypoglycaemia and rebound, and escalates. Diagnosis, treatment choices and doses are prescriber decisions, with pharmacist input.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. State the UKKA definition and severity bands, and explain why the numbers alone do not set the urgency.
2. Describe how nurses help ensure a potassium result is reliable (pseudohyperkalaemia, haemolysis, sampling site).
3. Describe the ECG and rhythm monitoring the guideline recommends, and what an ECG can and cannot show.
4. Describe the five-step treatment framework at a level a nurse needs (protect the heart, shift potassium into cells, remove it from the body, monitor, identify the cause and prevent recurrence), without drugs' doses.
5. Describe the monitoring after treatment: repeat potassium, glucose, rhythm.
6. Separate nursing responsibilities from prescribing; explain which potassium replacement decisions are outside this educational draft.
7. Recognize common hypokalaemia severity categories, magnesium associations and concentrated-potassium safety concerns without using this material as a replacement prescription.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Patients in ICU often have kidney injury, acidosis, potassium-raising drugs and tissue injury. The guideline notes in-hospital mortality is higher with hyperkalaemia (18.1 % in one cohort) than with hypokalaemia or normokalaemia, and above 30 % has been reported at 6.5 mmol/L or more. Arrhythmias can occur without warning, and the nurse at the monitor is often the first to see them.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Confirm the number.** Pseudohyperkalaemia is a laboratory artefact. UKKA lists prolonged transit, poor storage, difficult venepuncture, haemolysis, high platelet count, contamination with EDTA through the wrong order of draw, and sampling from an arm where potassium-containing fluid is infusing as causes. It recommends paired lithium-heparin and clotted samples from a large vein with gentle traction and prompt analysis when pseudohyperkalaemia is suspected (1A), a lithium-heparin sample when rapid results are needed (1B), and a point-of-care blood gas analyser result for potassium in emergencies while the laboratory result is awaited (1B). Haemolysis is a confounder, especially in whole-blood samples.
- **Urgent clinical assessment** with an early warning score (1C) and a history and examination to find the cause (1B), including the medicines the patient takes.
- **ECG — distinguish two decisions.** The detailed UKKA July 2026 hospital **Guideline 14.1 (1B)** recommends an **urgent 12-lead ECG for K⁺ ≥ 6.0 mmol/L**. The hospital summary inconsistently prints ≥ 6.5 mmol/L for that same Guideline 14.1; this discrepancy should be flagged, **not used to postpone assessment**. Separately, detailed **Guideline 14.2 (1C)** recommends **continuous ECG monitoring (at least 3 leads)** for K⁺ ≥ 6.5 mmol/L, any features of hyperkalaemia on the 12-lead ECG, or K⁺ 6.0–6.4 mmol/L when clinically unwell or a rapid rise is expected. Follow local escalation policy and the responsible team's orders, and escalate concerning clinical or ECG findings immediately regardless of a numeric band. These are UK adult hospital recommendations, not universally approved bedside orders.
- **What the ECG shows and does not.** The rationale describes progressive changes (peaked T waves, flattening of P waves, PR and QRS prolongation, bradycardia, sine-wave pattern) but notes that peaked T waves alone are rarely a sign of life-threatening hyperkalaemia, that changes do not always occur in sequence, and that the ECG can be normal or atypical (for example a pseudo-STEMI pattern) in severe hyperkalaemia. It reports that the ECG can help rule in but cannot rule out hyperkalaemia, and that its value depends on the interpreter. ECG signs most closely correlated with adverse events were QRS prolongation, bradycardia below 50 and junctional rhythms. Arrhythmias can occur at any time without prior toxic ECG changes. Patients with pacemakers are not protected: high potassium can widen the paced QRS and raise capture thresholds.
- **BRASH syndrome:** bradycardia, renal failure, AV-node-blocking drugs, shock and hyperkalaemia – described as refractory bradycardia and instability, often at potassium below 6.5.
- **Symptoms:** the guideline stresses the ECG and rhythm rather than symptoms. Weakness and palpitations are not covered in the pages read.

## 6b. Acute hypokalaemia in adults — assessment and medication-safety framework (NHS SPS 2024)
Hypokalaemia is not the same emergency as hyperkalaemia: the treatment pathways and ECG manifestations differ, and nursing education must distinguish them.
- **Recognize:** the NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service (SPS) defines hypokalaemia as serum potassium below 3.5 mmol/L; it classifies 3.0–3.5 as mild, 2.5–2.9 as moderate, and less than 2.5 as severe. These are UK guidance categories, not an automatic prescribing protocol. Symptoms, arrhythmias, ongoing losses, trend, renal function and context determine urgency.
- **Possible contributors:** gastrointestinal losses, diuretic-related or renal losses, magnesium depletion, reduced intake and potassium redistribution with medications such as insulin or beta-agonists. Recheck the medication list, urine output and relevant laboratory trend.
- **Nursing observations and escalation:** assess weakness and new rhythm changes, review the ECG as indicated by the responsible team, communicate severe or symptomatic results promptly, and monitor potassium, magnesium and renal function while replacement is prescribed. Significant hypomagnesaemia can make correction of potassium difficult; notify the prescriber rather than treating the potassium value in isolation.
- **IV potassium safety:** concentrated potassium chloride is a high-alert medication; SPS advises organisational control of concentrate and use of ready-to-use diluted infusions where appropriate. Verify formulation, approved administration route and pump programming per hospital policy, monitor the infusion site and ECG when clinically indicated, and escalate discrepancies or new symptoms immediately.
- **Prescriber/pharmacist boundary:** this draft deliberately contains **no potassium replacement dose, concentration, administration rate, compounding instruction or standardised recheck interval**. It does not tell nurses to independently administer or alter potassium replacement.
Source limit: NHS SPS 2024 specialist medication advice for UK adults, not a critical-care nursing operating procedure. Reuse permission and local applicability require checking before publication.

## 6c. ECG assessment versus continuous monitoring — reconcile the July 2026 UKKA threshold conflict
These **two separate questions** must not be collapsed into one bedside trigger.

- **Urgent 12-lead ECG (detailed hospital Guideline 14.1, 1B):** the detailed July 2026 UKKA page uses a potassium result **at or above 6.0 mmol/L**. The website's abbreviated hospital summary gives **6.5 mmol/L** for the same 14.1 recommendation. Both versions were rechecked on 2026-11-24, and the discrepancy remains. The detailed value is recorded for evidence trace; it is not a replacement for the institution's urgent-response instructions.
- **Continuous rhythm monitoring (detailed Guideline 14.2, 1C):** the decision is based on a different combination of factors: **severe hyperkalaemia**, abnormal ECG features, or a clinically unwell patient or expected rapid rise in the moderate range. It is **not equivalent** to the 12-lead threshold. Follow the treating team's escalation and monitoring policy.
- **Fictional nursing example:** for an otherwise stable adult with a new potassium result of 6.2 mmol/L, the detailed UKKA 14.1 page supports requesting an urgent 12-lead ECG. Whether continuous monitoring is also indicated depends on ECG findings, clinical status, trend and local policy. If rhythm changes or instability emerge, call for urgent help immediately regardless of the exact threshold. A reassuring ECG does not rule out clinically significant hyperkalaemia.
- **Pre-analytic check:** suspected sample haemolysis or contamination needs the laboratory/team to confirm the result, but do not delay clinical evaluation or ECG when the patient is unstable or the risk is high.

**Evidence limitation:** the UKKA organisation has not resolved the inconsistency between its summary and detailed 14.1 text in the pages reviewed. Nurses must not use this draft as a standalone protocol; trained renal/critical-care staff should reconcile local pathways with the source owner.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- **Act on a critical potassium result immediately** and tell the responsible clinician; do not wait for the next round.
- **Arrange prompt ECG assessment and monitoring:** report K⁺ ≥ 6.0 mmol/L promptly and follow the detailed Guideline 14.1 recommendation for an urgent 12-lead ECG; use continuous monitoring when indicated by Guideline 14.2 and local policy. Report widening QRS, bradycardia, new block, wide-complex rhythm, sine-wave pattern and clinical deterioration immediately. Ordering and implementation follow the local care team and protocols.
- **Check the sample:** know how the sample was taken (site, delay, haemolysis, infusion arm) and tell the clinician if the result is doubtful; do not delay treating a patient who has ECG changes while the result is repeated.
- **Prepare for the five-step approach** the guideline recommends (1B): (1) protect the heart with intravenous calcium when ECG changes are present; (2) shift potassium into cells with insulin and glucose infusion, with salbutamol as an adjunct, not alone; (3) remove potassium from the body with potassium binders or dialysis (the guideline's step 3 text read names binders; dialysis is covered in its separate recommendations); (4) monitor with blood tests; (5) identify the cause and prevent recurrence – the guideline's rationale says treatment is not complete until the cause is identified and steps are taken to prevent recurrence, and that one should never walk away without completing all five steps. UKKA recommends calcium chloride in cardiac arrest or peri-arrest and calcium gluconate otherwise (1C). Calcium salts can cause tissue necrosis if they extravasate (more with the chloride salt), hypotension, flushing and bradycardia or arrhythmias, mainly after too rapid administration; check the line, the site and the rhythm and report adverse effects.
- **Prevent hypoglycaemia after insulin and glucose.** UKKA recommends blood glucose measurement at regular intervals after the infusion in all patients (at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300 and 360 minutes; 1C) and a glucose infusion afterwards when the pre-treatment glucose is below 7.0 mmol/L (2B). Hypoglycaemia is the most common adverse event; it occurred in around one in five patients in the studies cited and the risk lasts up to six hours. Its signs (sweating, palpitations, tremor, hunger, then confusion or coma) may be masked in sedated patients, so rely on the measurements.
- **Repeat potassium** – UKKA suggests at least 1, 2, 4, 6 and 24 hours after treatment of moderate or severe hyperkalaemia (2C) to check the effect and to detect rebound as the shifting effect wears off (1B). Peak efficacy of several drugs is at 1–2 hours.
- **Avoid unneeded treatments:** UKKA recommends calcium resonium (cation-exchange resin) is no longer routinely used (2B) and suggests intravenous sodium bicarbonate is not used routinely (2C); these are prescriber decisions the nurse should understand.
- **Dialysis patients:** UKKA recommends urgent dialysis for severe hyperkalaemia in haemodialysis patients (1A), with calcium given for toxic ECG changes even when dialysis is immediately available (1C). For cardiac arrest, it recommends disconnection from dialysis equipment before defibrillation unless the machine is defibrillator-proof (1C).
- **Cardiac arrest:** hyperkalaemia should be considered as a reversible cause in every cardiac arrest (1A).
- **Escalate:** severe hyperkalaemia should be referred urgently to renal or critical care (2C); patients with airway, breathing, circulation or conscious-level problems go to critical care first (1C); referral should be made by a senior member of the team if escalation is needed from the outset or the patient fails to respond (1B). Decisions on dialysis for life-threatening hyperkalaemia are taken urgently by a nephrologist or critical care specialist (1C).
- **Prevention in hospital:** medicines that raise potassium are reviewed in the context of the illness and renal function (1B); dietary measures after non-dietary causes (constipation, acidosis, poorly controlled diabetes) are dealt with (1C); community monitoring is arranged on discharge (1B). These are team actions; the nurse reports problems and educates.

- **For low potassium results (NHS SPS):** report symptomatic or severe hypokalaemia promptly, review ECG and magnesium, renal function, trends and sources of potassium loss as directed by the clinical team; verify that any IV replacement is an authorised, correctly prepared high-alert infusion, not a ward-made shortcut.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Continuous ECG and rhythm; 12-lead ECG as ordered; heart rate and blood pressure; serum potassium at the intervals ordered (guideline suggestion above); blood glucose at the intervals above after insulin and glucose; urine output and renal function; arterial or venous blood gas if ordered; line and site checks during calcium infusion; pacemaker capture if the patient has one; mental state and signs of hypoglycaemia.

## 8b. Potassium replacement safety (expert consensus plus NHS SPS clinical pharmacy guidance; no regimen taught)
A 2026 study of 440 potassium-related incident reports from Finnish ICUs over 11 years found that **34 % involved a wrong concentration and 24 % a wrong medication**; 26 % were reported to have caused consequences for patients (for example electrolyte disturbance, extravasation, infiltration or skin irritation), and the expert panel rated most errors in a sample as more serious than the original classifiers did. The introduction notes international bodies' advice to use ready-to-use potassium solutions rather than concentrates on conventional wards, with concentrates allowed in critical care only after rigorous risk assessment. The panel's **consensus safeguards** across the medication process included: prescribing in the electronic system by a physician (verbal prescribing only in emergencies with closed-loop communication); standardised potassium replacement concentrates and decision-support alerts that consider the type of cannula and the potassium content; generic names or TALLman lettering; storing concentrates separately with high-risk labels; barcode-enabled dispensing; clear "contains potassium" labels, a "via central line" label on strong solutions and labels at both ends of the tubing; oral or enteral syringes that cannot be connected to intravenous lines; smart pumps with a drug library and restrictions; barcode checks when changing syringes or bags; tidy pump areas with unnecessary infusions removed; digital checklists at shift change; pharmacist education and ward-round presence; and adequate nurse staffing to avoid preparing infusions in a rushed way at the bedside. **Nursing implications (author's translation):** check drug, concentration, route and access type against the order, use the labelling and pump library, never prepare or give concentrate outside local rules, and report incidents and near misses. **Scope update:** NHS SPS 2024 now supports the separate section 6b on hypokalaemia severity, risk factors and monitoring needs. Exact replacement route selection, doses, rates, preparation and magnesium correction treatment remain prescriber/pharmacist and local-protocol responsibilities.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Potassium of 6.5 mmol/L or more; any ECG change of hyperkalaemia (widened QRS, bradycardia, loss of P waves, sine-wave pattern); new bradycardia or heart block, especially in a patient on AV-nodal drugs; wide-complex tachycardia; a rising potassium in a patient with impaired kidney function (the guideline names impaired kidney function as the commonest cause); acidosis as a non-dietary cause the guideline says to address (recommendation 21.2); other causes not detailed in the pages read are flagged for clinician review; hypoglycaemia after treatment; rebound potassium after the first response.

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for severe hyperkalaemia, ECG changes, arrhythmia, bradycardia, deterioration, failure to respond to treatment or urgent dialysis assessment. Activate emergency response for instability without waiting for a repeat potassium or a numeric threshold. Obtain a diagnostic ECG and continuous monitoring as indicated by the local pathway; the two are different assessments. The July 2026 UKKA detailed and summary ECG threshold discrepancy remains unresolved here; follow the authorised local pathway and do not use it to delay escalation. After prescribed insulin-glucose treatment, monitor glucose and potassium for delayed hypoglycaemia and rebound under the protocol (see MD-15).
SBAR — **S:** bed X, potassium 6.8 mmol/L with a widening QRS and a heart rate of 44. **B:** acute kidney injury, on an ACE inhibitor, oliguric; sample was not haemolysed. **A:** severe hyperkalaemia with ECG changes and bradycardia. **R:** please attend now; I have connected the monitor, obtained a 12-lead ECG and prepared for calcium and insulin and glucose per your orders; please decide on dialysis.

## 13. Potential complications
Bradycardia, heart block, sine-wave rhythm, ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac arrest; treatment complications named by UKKA: hypoglycaemia after insulin and glucose, rebound hyperkalaemia, tissue necrosis from calcium extravasation, hypotension, flushing, bradycardia from rapid calcium.

## 14. Documentation
Result with time and sample type; sample quality concerns; ECG findings and times; monitoring started; drugs given with times, rates as ordered and double-check; line and site checks; blood glucose values; repeat potassium results; communication and escalation times; patient response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
A laboratory calls a potassium of 6.7 mmol/L in a patient with acute kidney injury. The nurse connects the cardiac monitor, records a 12-lead ECG, checks the sample type and whether the patient has an infusion in the sampling arm, tells the doctor and the nurse in charge, prepares the line and monitor for the ordered treatments, and sets reminders for the timed glucose and potassium samples.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 59-year-old woman with acute kidney injury has a potassium of 6.9 mmol/L and a new wide-complex bradycardia. The nurse calls for help, the team treats her per the algorithm, and the nurse monitors the rhythm, checks glucose at the listed times, finds a glucose of 3.4 mmol/L at 90 minutes, reports it at once and the doctor orders a rescue, then documents all values.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A patient with AKI has a potassium of 6.2 mmol/L, rising from 5.3 mmol/L over two hours, and is newly unwell. Which interpretation and response best fits UKKA guidance?
A. The value is moderate, so defer ECG surveillance until it reaches the severe category.
B. The rapid rise makes the numerical classification severe and confirms an unstable rhythm.
C. The value is moderate, but the rapid rise and illness warrant urgent assessment and ECG surveillance.
D. The value is mild if the ECG has not yet shown changes, so repeat at the routine interval.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** UKKA classifies 6.0–6.4 mmol/L as moderate hyperkalaemia. The clinical urgency is not determined by the category alone: UKKA recommends continuous ECG monitoring for clinically unwell patients in this range or when a rapid rise is anticipated. Obtain urgent clinical assessment and authorized treatment as indicated.
**Why not A:** Clinical illness and a rapidly rising result can warrant continuous surveillance below the numerical severe threshold.
**Why not B:** A rapid rise increases concern but does not change the numerical severity category or establish a rhythm diagnosis.
**Why not D:** The numerical category does not depend on ECG findings, and a normal ECG cannot exclude dangerous hyperkalaemia.

**Q2 (judgment).** A potassium of 7.1 mmol/L is reported on a sample that took four hours to reach the laboratory, in a patient who looks well with a normal ECG. What is the best nursing action?
A. Escalate the critical result now, maintain ECG monitoring and arrange an urgent repeat sample.
B. Ignore the result, because a delayed sample is wrong and the patient looks well and has a normal ECG, so nothing needs to be done.
C. Start treatment without telling anyone, because a potassium above 7.0 mmol/L is an emergency whatever the sample looks like or how the patient is.
D. Delay the report until the next scheduled blood test, to avoid disturbing the team about a result that may be spurious and could cause unnecessary alarm.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Delayed sample transit can cause pseudohyperkalemia, but a normal ECG cannot exclude dangerous true hyperkalemia. Escalate immediately, obtain a fresh sample using the approved pathway and maintain continuous monitoring. UKKA recommends ECG surveillance for severe elevations. The responsible clinician decides urgent treatment while the result is clarified; do not allow repeat sampling to delay necessary emergency treatment.
**Why not B:** The result cannot be dismissed without checking.
**Why not C:** Treatment decisions belong to the prescriber and need an order.
**Why not D:** A critical result must be reported promptly.

**Q3 (safety).** A patient has been treated with insulin and glucose infusion for severe hyperkalaemia. Which monitoring does UKKA recommend?
A. Blood glucose if the patient has diabetes, because people with diabetes become hypoglycaemic after insulin.
B. Blood glucose once, at the end of the infusion, and then if the patient reports feeling unwell afterwards.
C. No further blood tests, because the potassium has already been shifted into cells and rebound does not occur.
D. Regular blood glucose measurements at set intervals after the infusion, and repeat potassium at set times.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** UKKA specifies glucose checks through six hours in all patients receiving insulin-glucose, with closer surveillance early and longer monitoring for patients at risk of late hypoglycemia, such as those receiving repeated treatment. Repeat potassium to assess response and rebound; document timing and hand over outstanding checks.
**Why not A:** The guideline notes adverse outcomes of hypoglycaemia regardless of diabetic status.
**Why not B:** A single check misses later hypoglycaemia, up to six hours after treatment.
**Why not C:** The guideline specifically warns of rebound hyperkalaemia.

**Q4 (application).** A patient with potassium 7.4 mmol/L develops a falling heart rate of 40 and widening QRS complexes during bedside monitoring. The patient is still responsive. Which action takes priority?
A. Obtain a repeat potassium sample and await the result before activating the emergency response for the rhythm change.
B. Call for urgent help and report the rhythm and potassium, as ECG changes mean treatment is needed.
C. Obtain a diagnostic ECG and wait for formal rhythm interpretation before notifying the critical-care team.
D. Focus on checking electrode contact and monitor settings before assessing circulation or requesting emergency assistance.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Severe hyperkalemia with bradycardia and widening QRS is a life-threatening emergency. Assess circulation, activate immediate help and prepare authorized emergency treatment while monitoring. IV calcium, when indicated, stabilizes the cardiac membrane but does not lower potassium; shifting and removing potassium require separate measures. If arrest occurs, follow the resuscitation protocol.
**Why not A:** Repeat sampling may be useful in parallel but must not delay help for severe hyperkalaemia with new conduction changes.
**Why not C:** A diagnostic ECG supports assessment but formal interpretation must not delay immediate escalation.
**Why not D:** Verify the trace while assessing circulation and mobilising help; equipment checks are not the first priority.

**Q5 (judgment).** Four hours after insulin-glucose treatment for hyperkalaemia, a patient becomes sweaty and less responsive. The repeat potassium has improved and an earlier glucose measurement was satisfactory. What should the nurse prioritize?
A. Arrange the next scheduled potassium check before assessing the patient's new symptoms.
B. Treat the improvement in potassium as evidence that post-treatment surveillance can end.
C. Assess urgently, check glucose promptly and follow the authorized hypoglycaemia pathway.
D. Request a repeat insulin-glucose treatment because new symptoms suggest potassium rebound.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Hypoglycaemia can occur after insulin-glucose treatment despite an earlier satisfactory glucose measurement. Assess ABC, obtain a prompt bedside glucose measurement and activate the appropriate emergency or hypoglycaemia pathway if indicated. Continue the prescribed glucose and potassium surveillance, including checks for late hypoglycaemia and potassium rebound. A normal or atypical ECG cannot exclude severe hyperkalemia; potassium monitoring remains separate from glucose assessment.
**Why not A:** New altered responsiveness warrants immediate assessment, rather than waiting for a scheduled electrolyte sample.
**Why not B:** An improved potassium does not establish that insulin-related hypoglycaemia risk has ended.
**Why not D:** Symptoms do not diagnose potassium rebound; repeating insulin without an authorized decision could worsen hypoglycaemia.

**Q6 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber with pharmacist input rather than the bedside nurse?
A. Choosing the calcium salt, the dose of insulin and glucose, and whether to add a binder.
B. Checking the line and site during an ordered calcium infusion and reporting swelling.
C. Measuring blood glucose at the timed intervals after insulin and glucose.
D. Connecting the cardiac monitor and recording a 12-lead ECG as ordered.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Selecting the calcium salt, the insulin and glucose amounts and any binder is drug and dose selection, a prescriber decision with pharmacist input. Timed monitoring, ECG and reporting are nursing actions.
**Why not B:** Site checks and reporting are nursing actions.
**Why not C:** Timed monitoring is a nursing task.
**Why not D:** Monitoring and ECG are nursing tasks.
