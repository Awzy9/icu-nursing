# Sedatives in the ICU – classes, key risks and nursing safety (partial evidence)
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. PARTIAL: benzodiazepines and barbiturates (textbook) and PADIS drug-choice statements only; no doses, no ICU infusion protocol, no propofol or dexmedetomidine pharmacology.

## 1. Overview
Sedatives reduce anxiety and level of consciousness. ISMP lists intravenous moderate-sedation agents, general anaesthetic agents and opioids among its high-alert categories, and the FDA pump page names propofol (Diprivan) and morphine among high-risk infusions. In critically ill ventilated adults the SCCM PADIS pages state which agents are preferred and that sedation should be kept light where possible; see D03-T09 for monitoring depth and D07-T03 for pain. The nurse checks the order, delivers infusions safely, monitors sedation and breathing, and escalates; the prescriber and pharmacist choose the drug, dose and targets.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe the classes the textbook covers (benzodiazepines, barbiturates) and the propofol example, in general terms.
2. State the most important safety risk of intravenous benzodiazepines and the interaction with opioids.
3. State the PADIS 2018 and 2025 drug-choice statements with strength and certainty.
4. Describe general infusion safety (high-alert status, pump checks, double check).
5. Recognise respiratory depression and over-sedation and respond.
6. Separate nursing, prescriber and pharmacist roles; state what is not covered.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Sedatives can cause respiratory depression, hypotension and prolonged sedation, and the interaction with opioids can be dangerous. The nurse often adjusts the infusion within a protocol and is responsible for noticing over-sedation.

## 6. Clinical assessment
**Textbook (Open RN) content:**
- **Benzodiazepines** bind GABA receptors to potentiate GABA; effects are sedation, reduced anxiety and anticonvulsant action; lorazepam is given by oral, intramuscular or intravenous route. A black box warning states that using benzodiazepines with opioids may cause profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma and death. The most important risk of intravenous lorazepam is respiratory depression: airway patency must be assured and respiration monitored closely, with ventilatory support as required. Sedation, drowsiness, hypotension and unsteadiness occur. Older or debilitated patients are more susceptible to sedative and respiratory depressant effects and should be monitored frequently with careful dose adjustment; children and the elderly may have paradoxical reactions (tremor, agitation, hallucinations). Overdose is manifested by CNS depression from drowsiness to coma; treatment is mainly supportive with airway maintenance and assisted ventilation; flumazenil may be used in hospitalised patients but carries a risk of seizures, especially in long-term benzodiazepine users (a prescriber decision).
- **Barbiturates** (phenobarbital is the textbook example) are used for sedation and seizures; adverse effects include CNS depression, respiratory depression; they should not be combined with other CNS depressants; therapy should be tapered, not stopped abruptly.
- **Propofol:** the textbook names propofol as an intravenous general anaesthetic (onset within about 40 seconds of injection); general anaesthesia requires airway control. Midazolam (with fentanyl) is named as an example of procedural sedation. No further ICU pharmacology is given.

**US prescribing information (FDA labels; one manufacturer label each; paraphrased; US only):**
- **Midazolam** (boxed warning): intravenous use has been associated with respiratory depression and respiratory arrest; where this was not recognised promptly and treated effectively, death or hypoxic encephalopathy has resulted; it should be used only where respiratory and cardiac function are continuously monitored (for example pulse oximetry), with resuscitation drugs and equipment for bag-valve-mask ventilation and intubation immediately available and personnel skilled in airway management; concomitant use with opioids may cause profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma and death. The label recommends immediate availability of the specific reversal agent (a prescriber matter) and says vital signs should continue to be monitored during recovery. (Note: the label's indications are procedural sedation; it does not describe ICU infusion practice.)
- **Lorazepam** (boxed warning): risks from concomitant use with opioids (profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma and death), abuse, misuse and addiction, and dependence and withdrawal reactions; patients should be followed for signs of respiratory depression and sedation.
- **Propofol:** an intravenous general anaesthetic and sedation drug whose indications include ICU sedation of intubated, ventilated adults. Warnings in the ICU-sedation section: start as a continuous infusion and make rate changes slowly to minimise hypotension and avoid acute overdose; the label describes **propofol infusion syndrome** in ICU sedation (a constellation of metabolic derangements and organ failure, including severe metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, lipaemia and rhabdomyolysis, that has resulted in death); serum triglycerides may rise with extended use (caution in lipid disorders and pancreatitis); strict aseptic technique is required, vials are never to be used for more than one person, and administration should be completed within a limited time after the vial is opened (12 hours in the label read); the label also describes hypersensitivity reactions, bradycardia, asystole and cardiac arrest, and says to avoid significant falls in mean arterial pressure in patients with raised intracranial pressure because of the effect on cerebral perfusion pressure.
- **Dexmedetomidine:** an alpha-2 agonist indicated for sedation of initially intubated, ventilated adults in intensive care (the label limits continuous infusion to a stated duration); patients should be continuously monitored; bradycardia, sinus arrest and hypotension may need intervention and may be more pronounced with hypovolaemia, diabetes, chronic hypertension and in older patients, and with other vasodilators or negative chronotropic drugs; patients can be roused by stimulation, which on its own should not be read as lack of effect; tolerance and tachyphylaxis may occur with prolonged exposure.

**PADIS recommendation pages (SCCM; recommendation-level):**
- Suggest using either propofol or dexmedetomidine over benzodiazepines for sedation in critically ill, mechanically ventilated adults (conditional; low certainty; PADIS 2018).
- Suggest using dexmedetomidine over propofol where light sedation and/or reduction of delirium are the highest priorities (conditional; moderate certainty; PADIS 2025 focused update).
- There is insufficient evidence to recommend benzodiazepines to treat anxiety in adults in the ICU (2025).

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Check the order (drug, concentration, rate and sedation target), patient, allergies and the other sedating drugs, especially opioids; verify pump programming at the moments the FDA page names; double check where policy requires; label lines.
- Monitor sedation level with the unit's validated tool at the frequency set by the protocol, together with respiratory rate and pattern, oxygen saturation, airway, blood pressure and heart rate; see D03-T09.
- Be especially vigilant with intravenous benzodiazepines (respiratory depression), combinations with opioids, and in older or debilitated patients.
- Titrate only within a written protocol; report over-sedation or agitation that the protocol cannot manage.
- Keep resuscitation and airway equipment available; call for help if the patient is not breathing adequately.
- **Know the label cautions:** watch for hypotension and bradycardia with dexmedetomidine, and for hypotension after propofol rate changes; for propofol also watch for new unexplained metabolic acidosis, high potassium, raised triglycerides or muscle injury and report them (propofol infusion syndrome); keep strict aseptic technique and follow the single-patient, time-limited use stated on the label; avoid abrupt large changes in pressure in patients with raised intracranial pressure unless ordered.
- Report adverse effects, paradoxical reactions and signs of withdrawal (the textbook says barbiturates should be tapered; the lorazepam label lists dependence and withdrawal reactions; ICU withdrawal syndromes are not described in the sources read).
- Document drug, rate, targets, sedation scores, respiratory status, interventions and escalations.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Sedation depth by unit scale; respiratory rate, pattern and effort; SpO₂ and end-tidal CO₂ if available; airway patency; blood pressure and heart rate; delirium screen; pain score; pump settings and alarms; daily sedation interruption or wake-up trial results as protocolised; blood glucose and triglycerides are not covered by the sources read.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Slow or shallow breathing, falling oxygen saturation or rising CO₂; unrousable patient or oversedation compared with the target; hypotension after a bolus or rate change; paradoxical agitation; combined sedatives and opioids; older or debilitated patient; pump programming error or unexplained alarm; a patient who cannot be woken for the daily interruption.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Monitor sedation, breathing and haemodynamics; check pump and order; support airway and call for help; report; document | Independent nursing |
| Titrate sedation infusions within a written protocol; give ordered boluses; perform double checks; do the daily interruption per protocol | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Choose the sedative, dose and target; decide to use a reversal agent; change the strategy | Prescriber decision (pharmacist for drug and concentration review) |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate for inadequate breathing, oversedation, haemodynamic instability, agitation that the protocol does not control, pump or infusion problems, or any suspected error.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, ventilated patient oversedated compared with the target, with a falling blood pressure. **B:** on a sedative infusion and an opioid for 12 hours. **A:** oversedation with hypotension. **R:** please review now; I need an order to reduce or pause the sedation and assess the cause.

## 13. Potential complications
Respiratory depression; hypotension; oversedation; paradoxical reactions; prolonged sedation; dependence and withdrawal (not described in the sources read); medication errors from high-alert infusions.

## 14. Documentation
Order and pump settings; double-check; sedation scores and target; breathing and haemodynamic observations; interventions; adverse effects; escalations and responses.

## 15. Practical bedside example
Before starting a sedation infusion the nurse reads the order, notes that the patient is also on an opioid, checks the pump programming with a second nurse, records baseline sedation score, respiratory rate and blood pressure, and sets reassessment times.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 72-year-old ventilated patient on a benzodiazepine infusion and an opioid cannot be woken for the daily interruption and has a falling blood pressure. The nurse stops and reports as the protocol directs, supports the airway, calls the doctor, and documents the sedation scores and times.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (safety).** Which risk does the Open RN textbook describe as the most important with intravenous lorazepam?
A. Respiratory depression, so airway patency must be assured and respiration monitored closely.
B. Excessive urine output, so that the fluid balance must be recorded every hour after each dose is given.
C. A rise in blood sugar, so that blood glucose must be checked before each dose is given to the patient.
D. Permanent hearing loss, so that the patient's hearing must be assessed before each dose is given.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The textbook says respiratory depression is the most important risk of intravenous use and that airway and respiration must be monitored.
**Why not B:** The textbook does not describe urine output as the main risk of lorazepam.
**Why not C:** A rise in blood sugar is not described as a lorazepam risk in the section.
**Why not D:** Hearing loss is not described as a lorazepam risk in the section.

**Q2 (understanding).** What does the textbook's boxed warning say about benzodiazepines and opioids used together?
A. They can safely be combined because their effects cancel each other out in the central nervous system.
B. Combined use is recommended for all ventilated patients to keep them comfortable and settled on the ventilator.
C. Combined use is only a concern in young children and is not a concern in older adults or ventilated patients.
D. Combined use may result in profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma and death.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** This is the black box warning quoted in the section.
**Why not A:** The effects add rather than cancel.
**Why not B:** The warning is a caution, not a recommendation.
**Why not C:** The warning applies to patients generally and is not limited to children.

**Q3 (application).** Which drug-choice statement do the PADIS pages give for sedation in critically ill, mechanically ventilated adults?
A. Suggest using either propofol or dexmedetomidine over benzodiazepines (conditional; low certainty).
B. Recommend benzodiazepines as the preferred first-line drug in all ventilated patients because they act quickly.
C. Recommend deep sedation for all ventilated patients in order to prevent anxiety during the first days.
D. Recommend that sedation should never be reduced or interrupted during the first week of mechanical ventilation.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** This is the PADIS 2018 statement.
**Why not B:** The pages suggest avoiding benzodiazepines in favour of other agents.
**Why not C:** Light sedation is generally favoured.
**Why not D:** No such recommendation exists on the pages read.

**Q4 (judgment).** A ventilated patient on a sedative infusion and an opioid is hard to rouse and the blood pressure is falling. What is the best action?
A. Wait to see whether the blood pressure recovers by itself before telling anyone about the patient's condition.
B. Increase the infusion rate to stabilise the patient, because a lower pressure usually means too little sedation.
C. Assess airway, breathing and sedation level, follow the protocol for pausing sedation, and call the prescriber at once.
D. Give a reversal agent that is kept on the unit without waiting for an order, in order to save time.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Over-sedation with haemodynamic change needs immediate assessment, protocol action and escalation.
**Why not A:** Waiting could allow deterioration.
**Why not B:** Increasing sedation would worsen the problem.
**Why not D:** Reversal agents require an order and carry risks such as seizures.

**Q5 (understanding).** Which statement matches the PADIS 2025 focused-update recommendation on drug choice?
A. Suggest propofol over dexmedetomidine in all patients because it is thought to work faster at the start and to be easier to titrate.
B. Suggest dexmedetomidine over propofol where light sedation and/or reducing delirium are the highest priorities (conditional; moderate certainty).
C. Suggest using benzodiazepines to treat anxiety in all adults in the ICU, whatever the clinical picture, because they are thought to give the most reliable relief.
D. Make no statement at all about the choice of sedative agent for mechanically ventilated adults, leaving the choice to each unit.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** This is the 2025 statement as printed on the SCCM page.
**Why not A:** The 2025 statement favours dexmedetomidine in that situation.
**Why not C:** The page says there is insufficient evidence for benzodiazepines in anxiety.
**Why not D:** A drug-choice statement does appear on the SCCM 2025 page.

**Q6 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber with pharmacist input, rather than the bedside nurse?
A. Recording the sedation score and breathing pattern at the intervals set by the unit protocol.
B. Checking the pump programming against the order each time a new syringe is connected.
C. Calling for help when the patient's breathing becomes inadequate during the sedation infusion.
D. Choosing the sedative agent, dose and sedation target for the patient.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Selecting drug, dose and target is prescribing.
**Why not A:** Monitoring is a nursing task.
**Why not B:** Checking is a nursing safety task.
**Why not C:** Calling for help is a nursing responsibility.
