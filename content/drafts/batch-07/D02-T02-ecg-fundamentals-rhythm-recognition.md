# ECG fundamentals and recognising important rhythms
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. An introductory rhythm-recognition draft from a general nursing textbook: no strips, algorithms, doses or monitor-alarm settings are included.

## 1. Overview
An electrocardiogram (ECG) records the heart's electrical activity through electrodes on the skin. On a cardiac monitor or telemetry the rhythm is watched continuously; a paper rhythm strip is at least a 6-second tracing. The nurse's job is to apply electrodes well, follow a systematic method to look at every strip, recognise rhythms that need emergency action, assess the patient (not only the strip), and report. Diagnosis and treatment of dysrhythmias are prescriber decisions; the nurse responds to emergencies according to the unit's protocols.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe the heart's normal conduction pathway and the natural pacemaker rates the textbook gives.
2. Apply the systematic method to a strip: rate, regularity, P waves, PR interval, QRS duration, T waves, ST segment, QT interval, other features, then the patient.
3. Recognise normal sinus rhythm, sinus bradycardia and tachycardia, atrial fibrillation and flutter, premature ventricular contractions, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, asystole and pulseless electrical activity (PEA), and the three degrees of AV block.
4. Explain why artifact and the patient's clinical state matter more than the rhythm label displayed by the monitor.
5. State which rhythms need emergency assistance and what the nurse does first.
6. Separate nursing actions from prescriber decisions; state what this draft does not cover.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
ICU patients are on continuous monitoring because dysrhythmias can develop suddenly and cause low cardiac output or arrest. The textbook states that ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation and third-degree heart block can quickly lead to cardiac arrest and that the nurse must know which rhythms need emergency assistance. Electrolyte disturbance, drugs and acid-base disorders change the ECG (see hyperkalaemia in D04-T06).

## 6. Clinical assessment
**Conduction pathway and natural pacemaker rates (textbook):** the sinoatrial (SA) node is the natural pacemaker (60–100 beats/min); the atrioventricular (AV) node is the backup (40–60); the Purkinje fibres pace at 20–40 if all else fails; the bundle of His and the left and right bundle branches carry the signal to the ventricles.

**Systematic approach (Open RN 7.3):**
1. **Rate:** count R waves in a 6-second strip and multiply by 10 (and P waves for the atrial rate if present).
2. **Regularity:** regular, irregularly irregular (never equal; for example atrial fibrillation), regularly irregular (a repeating pattern), or occasionally irregular (an occasional out-of-place beat, such as sporadic PVCs).
3. **P waves:** present? all the same? regular? one for every QRS? Absent P waves mean the beat did not start in the SA node.
4. **PR interval:** normal 0.12–0.20 seconds (3–5 small boxes); prolonged suggests delay in conduction (heart block).
5. **QRS duration:** normal 0.04–0.12 seconds; a wide QRS means the beat did not originate in the atria; a prolonged QRS may reflect hyperkalaemia or bundle branch block.
6. **T waves:** upright and rounded; abnormalities (inverted, flat, biphasic, tall or peaked) have many causes, including electrolyte and acid-base disorders, drugs, ischaemia and others.
7. **ST segment:** should lie on the baseline; elevation or depression of 1 mm or more measured at the J point is abnormal; ST elevation in two or more contiguous leads in the right setting is highly specific for acute myocardial infarction (the textbook's criteria are given in millimetres for limb versus precordial leads); non-ischaemic causes of ST depression include digoxin toxicity, hypokalaemia, hypothermia and tachycardia.
8. **QT interval:** the corrected QT (QTc) is monitored; a prolonged QTc carries a risk of torsades de pointes, ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation; common causes include medicines and electrolyte abnormalities such as low calcium and low magnesium.
9. **Other components:** U wave (often linked to hypokalaemia) and J wave (typically hypothermia).
10. **Observe the patient.** The more abnormal the rhythm, the less blood the heart pumps; look for dizziness, confusion, low blood pressure, chest pain, shortness of breath, loss of consciousness, and check pulse, perfusion and blood pressure.

**Rhythms in the textbook, with key features:**
- **Normal sinus rhythm:** all components normal.
- **Sinus bradycardia** (below 60) may be normal in athletes but may indicate a cardiac condition or medicine effect; **sinus tachycardia** (above 100) has many causes including hypovolaemia, anaemia, fever or infection, low oxygenation, heart conditions and medicines; assess cardiac output and look for the cause.
- **Atrial fibrillation:** irregularly irregular, no distinct P waves, wavy baseline; risk of stroke; decreased cardiac output possible; atrial and ventricular rates differ and may affect automatic blood pressure readings. **Atrial flutter:** sawtooth pattern, atrial rates 250–300.
- **PVCs:** wide, bizarre QRS in an otherwise normal rhythm; frequent PVCs may signal a serious condition; an R on T beat can trigger ventricular tachycardia, and prolonged QT increases that risk.
- **Supraventricular tachycardia:** narrow QRS and rapid rates, P waves often hidden.
- **Ventricular tachycardia:** wide QRS without visible P and T waves, often above 120 per minute; tolerated only briefly; emergency response; the textbook says treatment depends on whether the patient has a pulse.
- **Torsades de pointes:** a type of ventricular tachycardia with twisting points in long QT; can become ventricular fibrillation.
- **Ventricular fibrillation:** chaotic quivering, nothing measurable, no cardiac output: immediate CPR and emergency response.
- **Asystole:** flat line, not shockable. **PEA:** electrical activity on the ECG without a pulse, also not shockable; treated with CPR and a search for reversible causes.
- **AV blocks:** first-degree (PR over 0.20 seconds, every P followed by a QRS; may be normal or an early sign of conduction disease, drug toxicity or hypokalaemia); second-degree and third-degree blocks are described in the chapter; third-degree can lead to arrest. Beta-blockers may worsen AV block.

**Artifact:** the monitor's rhythm label is not always correct; artifact occurs when electrodes do not make good contact or the patient moves. Always correlate with the patient.

## 6c. Monitoring accuracy and alarms – summary-level support only (Phase 3)
The public summary of the AACN dysrhythmia-monitoring alert states that the accuracy of cardiac monitoring – electrode placement, lead selection, interval assessment and rhythm interpretation – is an important component of patient safety in hospitalised patients who meet criteria for monitoring, that quality monitoring requires nurses to have high levels of knowledge and skill, that gaps in practice have been documented, and that inaccurate electrode placement can affect the tracing. The summary of the AACN alarm alert states that alarm fatigue is a patient-safety risk and that in cited studies 89 % to 99 % of ECG alarms were false or clinically insignificant. **The recommendations themselves (lead choice, alarm limits, skin preparation, daily electrode change, ST monitoring) are behind a login and were not read, so they are not taught.**

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- **Apply electrodes properly.** The Open RN telemetry checklist: verify the order, identify the patient with two identifiers, explain the procedure, prepare the skin (clean, dry, free of excess hair and sweat), apply electrodes with good contact, use the colour-coded five-lead placement the chapter shows (the two arm electrodes in the infraclavicular fossae near the shoulders, the two leg electrodes below the rib cage on the right and left upper abdomen, and V1 at the fourth intercostal space on the right sternal border), attach the wires, observe the rhythm, print a 6-second strip and interpret it. Follow agency policy and the manufacturer's instructions.
- **Interpret systematically**, check the patient, and verify an alarm or unusual rhythm at the bedside before acting.
- **Know the emergency rhythms** (ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, torsades, asystole, PEA, third-degree block) and call for help immediately, starting resuscitation per the unit's procedure for a patient without a pulse.
- **Report** significant changes with the strip and the patient's condition, and withhold or hold cardiac medicines only as the order or protocol allows and after notifying the prescriber (the textbook says to withhold cardiac medicines if indicated and notify the provider in symptomatic bradycardia).
- **Link rhythm to causes:** electrolytes (potassium, calcium, magnesium), drugs, hypoxaemia, acidosis, temperature, and ischaemia, and report suspected causes.
- **Document** rhythm, rate, intervals, time, patient status, actions and who was informed; attach strips as local policy requires.
- **Not covered:** alarm limits, lead choice for ST monitoring, 12-lead acquisition detail, paced rhythms, indications and duration of monitoring (no source read).

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Heart rate and rhythm; regularity; PR, QRS and QT intervals as available; ST segments; pulse, blood pressure, capillary refill, mental state, oxygen saturation, chest pain, dyspnoea; electrode contact and artifact; electrolytes and medicines relevant to the rhythm; alarms and their response.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Wide-complex rhythm; chaotic or absent rhythm; asystole; unresponsive patient with a rhythm on the monitor (check for a pulse); heart rate that is very slow or very fast with low blood pressure, chest pain, confusion or collapse; increasing ectopy, R on T beats or long QT; new heart block; sudden change after a medicine or electrolyte result; monitor alarms that are repeatedly silenced; lead-off signals mistaken for asystole (always check the patient).

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Apply electrodes, check the patient, interpret and report the rhythm, respond to emergency rhythms by calling for help and starting resuscitation per procedure, document | Independent nursing |
| Give ordered antidysrhythmic drugs or follow a standing protocol for defined rhythms; assist with cardioversion, defibrillation or pacing as trained | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Diagnose the rhythm formally, choose drug, cardioversion, pacing or other treatment, set targets | Prescriber decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Call for help immediately for pulseless rhythms or collapse, and urgently for any symptomatic dysrhythmia, a life-threatening rhythm with a pulse, new heart block, or deterioration.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, monitor shows a wide-complex tachycardia at about 150, patient confused and pale. **B:** day 3 after admission with sepsis; potassium and magnesium results pending. **A:** possible ventricular tachycardia with a pulse and low cardiac output. **R:** please come now; I have the arrest trolley and defibrillator pads ready and am checking the pulse and blood pressure.

## 13. Potential complications
Low cardiac output, collapse, cardiac arrest, stroke (atrial fibrillation), harm from misinterpreted artifact, delayed recognition of life-threatening rhythms, unnecessary treatment.

## 14. Documentation
Time; rhythm and rate; intervals if measured; strip filed as policy requires; patient assessment; actions taken; medicines given; who was called and when; response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
A patient's monitor alarms for asystole. The nurse immediately looks at the patient, finds a conscious patient, sees a loose electrode, reattaches it and the normal rhythm returns. If the patient had been unresponsive and pulseless, she would have called for help and started resuscitation according to the unit's procedure.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (application).** The monitor displays "ventricular fibrillation" alarm in a patient who is awake, talking and has a strong pulse. What should the nurse do first?
A. Check the patient and the electrodes and leads, because artifact can produce a wrong rhythm label.
B. Begin chest compressions at once, because the monitor label has identified a life-threatening rhythm that needs treatment.
C. Silence the alarm and wait to see whether it repeats before taking any action, since the patient looks well.
D. Call the family to the bedside, because a ventricular fibrillation alarm means that the patient is dying.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The textbook says the monitor's rhythm name is not always correct because of artifact; the patient's clinical state decides the response.
**Why not B:** A talking patient with a pulse does not need compressions.
**Why not C:** Waiting without looking at the patient is unsafe.
**Why not D:** It causes distress and is not a clinical response.

**Q2 (understanding).** A rhythm is described as "irregularly irregular", with no distinct P waves and a wavy baseline. Which rhythm does the textbook link to this pattern?
A. Normal sinus rhythm, because the rhythm in a healthy heart is described as varying from beat to beat.
B. Sinus bradycardia, because a slow sinus rhythm shows a wavy baseline and no distinct P waves.
C. Atrial fibrillation, because this is the pattern the textbook names as the hallmark of that rhythm.
D. Asystole, because the absence of P waves with an irregular baseline is the pattern of a flat line.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Irregularly irregular narrow QRS complexes with no distinct P waves or a wavy baseline are the hallmark of atrial fibrillation in the textbook.
**Why not A:** Normal sinus rhythm is regular with normal P waves.
**Why not B:** Sinus bradycardia is a regular slow rhythm.
**Why not D:** Asystole is a flat line.

**Q3 (judgment).** A patient on the monitor is unresponsive and the rhythm shows no organised complexes. What is the correct priority?
A. Print a strip and ask the doctor to confirm the rhythm first, before anyone starts any resuscitation measures.
B. Check for responsiveness and a pulse, call for help and start resuscitation according to the unit's procedure.
C. Check the electrode contact for two minutes before doing anything else, in case the trace is wrong.
D. Take a full set of observations and then decide what to do about the rhythm that the monitor displays.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Ventricular fibrillation is described as requiring immediate CPR and emergency response; unresponsiveness with an abnormal rhythm is treated as cardiac arrest.
**Why not A:** Confirmation must not delay CPR.
**Why not C:** Electrode checks are for a conscious patient with a doubtful trace.
**Why not D:** Observations delay emergency action.

**Q4 (understanding).** What is the textbook's rate-counting method on a 6-second strip?
A. Count the P waves and divide by six.
B. Count the large boxes and multiply by 20.
C. Measure the PR interval and divide by the QRS.
D. Count the R waves and multiply by 10.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The simplest method is to count R waves in a 6-second strip and multiply by 10.
**Why not A:** The atrial rate also uses ×10, not division by six.
**Why not B:** The textbook method is based on R waves in a 6-second strip.
**Why not C:** Intervals are not used to calculate the rate.

**Q5 (safety).** A patient with a prolonged QTc starts to have PVCs that fall on the T wave. Why is this important?
A. R on T beats can trigger ventricular tachycardia, and prolonged QT increases the risk, so this should be reported.
B. PVCs on the T wave are harmless and need no action.
C. The pattern shows that the electrodes have been placed incorrectly.
D. The pattern shows that the patient has first-degree AV block.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The textbook states that R on T can trigger ventricular tachycardia and that conditions causing a prolonged QT make it more likely.
**Why not B:** The textbook says it can trigger a dangerous rhythm.
**Why not C:** Ectopic beats come from the heart, not from electrode placement.
**Why not D:** First-degree block is a long PR interval.

**Q6 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber rather than the nurse?
A. Applying the electrodes to clean, dry skin with good contact and attaching the lead wires as per policy.
B. Printing a 6-second strip and recording the rhythm and the patient's status.
C. Deciding on the treatment for a newly identified dysrhythmia, such as the drug, cardioversion or pacing.
D. Calling for help when the patient is pulseless.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Treatment choice is a prescribing decision.
**Why not A:** Electrode application is a nursing skill.
**Why not B:** Documentation is a nursing task.
**Why not D:** Calling for help is a nursing responsibility.
