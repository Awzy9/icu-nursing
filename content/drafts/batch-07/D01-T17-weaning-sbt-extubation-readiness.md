# Ventilator liberation – spontaneous breathing trials (SBT) and what happens before extubation
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. AARC 2024 supports SBT teaching; ATS/CHEST 2017 and ATS 2026 recommendation/abstract summaries inform selective airway-risk and post-extubation support discussion. Local airway protocols and clinician decisions govern.

## 1. Overview
Liberation from mechanical ventilation means stopping invasive ventilation; weaning is the gradual reduction of support; extubation is removing the tube. The AARC guideline stresses that liberation and extubation must not be conflated: a patient can pass an SBT and still not be extubated, for example when secretions are abundant and the cough is weak, and some patients are liberated from the ventilator through a tracheostomy rather than extubated. An SBT is a period of spontaneous breathing with minimal or no positive-pressure assistance, usually 30–120 minutes. In many units respiratory therapists run the screen and the SBT; nurses adjust sedation, observe the patient and coordinate with the team. The decision to liberate or extubate belongs to the prescriber and the team.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Distinguish weaning, liberation, SBT and extubation.
2. State the four AARC 2024 SBT recommendations with their strength and certainty.
3. Describe a simple readiness screen and what the guideline says to watch for during an SBT.
4. Explain why nurses and respiratory therapists coordinate sedation adjustment with SBT timing.
5. Recognise features that suggest an SBT is not being tolerated and report them.
6. Separate nursing actions from respiratory therapist and prescriber decisions, and state what the guideline does not cover.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
The guideline's aim is to liberate adult critically ill patients from mechanical ventilation more rapidly. It notes that in two large trials about 75 % of patients passed the first SBT, suggesting clinicians were slow to recognise readiness, and that adherence to liberation guidelines is low, which delays liberation. The nurse's sedation management and observations are central to timely trials.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Readiness screen.** The guideline suggests that a simple screen is likely sufficient: some reversal of the underlying cause of respiratory failure, adequate oxygenation, haemodynamic stability, and the capability to initiate an inspiratory effort. It suggests the rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI) is not needed to decide readiness (conditional; moderate certainty). The 2022 meta-analysis it cites found the RSBI had moderate sensitivity and poor specificity for predicting extubation success; one older trial found requiring an RSBI delayed liberation by about a day.
- **Common oxygenation criteria in trials.** The guideline describes SpO₂ above 90 % at an FiO₂ of 0.40, or PaO₂/FiO₂ above 200 mm Hg, as common criteria before starting, and SpO₂ below 90 % as a common reason to end an SBT. These come from earlier trials and are described, not recommended as a standard; it suggests the criteria may be too conservative. Local protocol governs.
- **During the SBT.** The criteria for whether a patient is tolerating the trial should include assessment, especially changes, of respiratory pattern, haemodynamics, gas exchange and patient comfort.
- **After the SBT.** Passing an SBT is one factor in extubation readiness. The guideline names airway protection and the ability to clear secretions as additional factors predicting success. How these are assessed is not described in the guideline.

## 6b. Extubation is a separate decision from passing an SBT — risk stratification and post-extubation support (ATS/CHEST and ATS)
The AARC 2024 SBT guideline distinguishes the assessment of breathing without ventilator support from **whether a patient can safely have the artificial airway removed**. A successful SBT alone does not establish airway protection or clearance of secretions.
- **Before extubation:** communicate the patient's mental state, cough effectiveness, secretion burden, airway concerns (including known difficult airway or suspected swelling) and ability to protect the airway. If there is risk of post-extubation stridor, the ATS/CHEST 2017 guideline suggests selecting high-risk patients for a clinician-led cuff-leak assessment, rather than performing it universally. Its treatment recommendations are prescriber decisions and are not reproduced here.
- **Plan respiratory support before tube removal:** the ATS 2026 clinical-practice-guideline abstract describes a risk-stratified approach after critical illness: it suggests HFNC for lower-risk patients and preventive face-mask NIV for those at higher risk of failure. This is a summary-level citation, not a full-text extraction of evidence tables, not a guarantee of successful extubation and not a nurse-authorised device prescription.
- **Post-extubation nurse assessment:** monitor work of breathing, oxygenation, upper-airway noise/stridor, secretion clearance, consciousness and signs of fatigue, and promptly communicate deterioration. The prescribed oxygen / NIV / HFNC strategy, monitoring frequency and re-intubation readiness follow the treating team and the unit's airway rescue plan.
- **Do not substitute noninvasive support for emergency airway review:** a deteriorating patient requires urgent senior assessment. This paragraph does not prescribe whether to attempt NIV, change settings or reintubate.
Evidence limits: AARC 2024 article reviewed for SBT distinctions; ATS/CHEST 2017 guideline recommendation summary and ATS 2026 PubMed abstract inspected 2026-11-23. Specific contraindications, thresholds, technique and post-extubation rescue treatment are not taught without clinician review.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- **Know the four recommendations** (conditional, as printed): no RSBI needed for readiness (moderate); an SBT may be done with or without low-level pressure support up to 8 cm H₂O (moderate); a standardised approach to assessment and, if appropriate, completion of an SBT before noon each day (very low); do not increase FiO₂ during an SBT (very low).
- **Coordinate sedation with the SBT.** The guideline states that morning screening and SBT requires coordination between nurses adjusting sedation and respiratory therapists performing SBTs, and that protocols should address spontaneous awakening trials (SATs), SBTs and their coordination. The SCCM PADIS 2018 page describes a daily sedation interruption as a period each day when sedative medication is stopped so the patient can wake and show arousal. Follow your unit's sedation interruption and SBT protocol, and report if the patient cannot be woken or is distressed (see D03-T09).
- **Observe during the trial** the respiratory pattern, haemodynamics, oxygen saturation and gas exchange as available, and patient comfort. Be ready to report changes immediately to the respiratory therapist and the prescriber. The guideline notes that on-ventilator SBT approaches (for example pressure support and PEEP both set to zero) allow use of the ventilator's own monitoring and alarms and a quick return to support if the trial fails.
- **Do not increase FiO₂ during the SBT** unless ordered and the team has decided to end the trial: the guideline says raising FiO₂ can raise SpO₂ and mask the underlying hypoxaemic problem, producing a false pass.
- **If the patient will be extubated to non-invasive ventilation,** the guideline says it is reasonable to perform the SBT on the settings that will be used after extubation (for example in patients at risk of extubation failure); this is a team decision.
- **Document** the screen, the SBT start and end times, the mode or technique, the observations, the outcome and who was informed.
- **Extubation preparation and post-extubation care (Phase 3; secondary summary, flagged for clinician review):** the 2026 peri-extubation best-evidence summary lists, for difficult-to-wean patients, among its 48 items (JBI grades 1a to 5b; most extubation-procedure items are expert-consensus level): confirm that an SBT has been done, assess readiness, evaluate clinical factors that may impair ventilation after extubation and verify that all airway equipment and emergency devices are available; clear airway secretions and pre-oxygenate before tube removal; have suitably qualified staff present and help available if a difficult airway is anticipated; high-risk factors for extubation failure (for example first SBT failure, older age, high BMI, heart failure, COPD, previous extubation failure, weak cough, long ventilation); a cuff-leak (air leakage) test with systemic steroids before extubation considered for patients at high risk of post-extubation stridor who fail it (a prescriber decision); the rapid shallow breathing index not recommended for predicting extubation success (consistent with AARC); definitions of extubation failure and signs of failure (for example fast breathing, use of accessory muscles, low SpO₂, very high heart rate, abnormal blood pressure, cyanosis, mottled skin, reduced consciousness); post-extubation monitoring for airway complications such as hoarseness, swelling and difficulty swallowing; and preventive non-invasive ventilation immediately after extubation for patients at high risk who have been ventilated for more than 24 hours and passed an SBT, with NIV and high-flow nasal oxygen judged comparable overall. **Limits:** the summary is secondary and not a guideline, covers difficult-to-wean patients (not all patients), comes from a Chinese hospital setting, and its numeric thresholds are not reproduced here as nursing criteria; the underlying ATS/ACCP, Korean and difficult-airway guidelines were not read.

- **After the decision to extubate (summary-level sources):** document the planned post-extubation modality selected by the clinical team, agreed escalation pathway and concern for airway protection; watch for new stridor, increased effort and poor secretion clearance, and arrange urgent review if deterioration develops. No fixed surveillance interval or rescue algorithm is taught here.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Respiratory rate and pattern; tidal volume and minute ventilation if displayed; SpO₂ (and gas exchange if measured); heart rate, rhythm, blood pressure; sedation and agitation level; patient comfort and distress; secretions and cough; ventilator alarms; time on the trial.

## 9. Important findings – AARC 2024 statements
| Question | Recommendation (paraphrased) | Strength; certainty | Population and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| RSBI | Not needed to determine readiness for an SBT | Conditional; moderate | Adults; RSBI may be useful in intermediate pre-test probability but unconfirmed |

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
During an SBT: increasing respiratory rate or laboured breathing, falling SpO₂, rising heart rate or blood pressure change, new arrhythmia, agitation, sweating, distress, or a fall in consciousness; abundant secretions and a weak cough before or after the trial; a failed trial with no plan for the next step. (The guideline names respiratory pattern, haemodynamics, gas exchange and comfort as what to assess; the specific signs listed here are general clinical descriptions not itemised in the guideline and are flagged for clinician review.)

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Adjust sedation within the ordered protocol or interruption; observe and record during the SBT; report intolerance; document; keep the patient safe and comfortable | Independent nursing (observation, reporting, documentation, safety) |
| Carry out the unit's sedation interruption and SBT protocol; stop the sedative infusion or restart it as ordered | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Perform the screen and run the SBT (in many units); choose ventilator settings for the trial; operate the ventilator | Respiratory therapist (per local roles) |
| Decide to liberate or extubate; choose strategy, tube removal and post-extubation support; order sedation changes | Prescriber decision (physician or credentialed provider) |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for intolerance, distress, desaturation, haemodynamic change or arrhythmia during the SBT. End an intolerable trial and restore prescribed support under the authorised protocol; provide emergency oxygenation and ventilation as needed. The AARC 2024 conditional suggestion against raising inspired oxygen during the trial is intended to avoid masking intolerance, not to withhold rescue oxygen. Passing an SBT does not alone establish readiness for extubation: the team also evaluates airway protection, secretion clearance and the post-extubation support plan.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, ventilated patient 15 minutes into a spontaneous breathing trial with a rising respiratory rate and falling SpO₂. **B:** passed the morning screen; sedation interruption completed an hour ago. **A:** possible SBT intolerance. **R:** please come to the bedside now; I am staying with the patient and the respiratory therapist is ready to return to support; I need a decision about ending the trial.

## 13. Potential complications
SBT failure with respiratory distress; hypoxaemia; haemodynamic instability or arrhythmia; agitation or unplanned device removal during sedation reduction; delayed liberation; extubation failure (not addressed in the guideline's recommendations). General clinical complications are flagged for clinician review.

## 14. Documentation
Screen result and criteria; sedation interruption times; SBT mode and settings; start and end times; observations during the trial; outcome (pass, fail, reasons); people informed; plan after the trial; any ventilator return to support.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At 07:30 the respiratory therapist screens a patient and finds the patient suitable. The nurse reduces sedation as the protocol requires, checks that the patient responds, tells the respiratory therapist the patient is ready, observes respiratory rate, SpO₂, heart rate and comfort during the trial, and records the times and values. The patient completes the SBT; the doctor decides on extubation after reviewing secretions and cough.

The nurse stays with him, the respiratory therapist returns him to ventilator support, the nurse reports to the doctor, and the failed SBT with its time and findings is recorded.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (understanding).** During a multidisciplinary round, an intubated patient completes an SBT successfully but has a weak cough and substantial secretions. Which statement best explains why the team still needs to assess extubation readiness?
A. They are the same thing, so a patient who passes a spontaneous breathing trial is automatically extubated on the same day without further review.
B. Liberation is discontinuing invasive ventilation, while extubation is removing the tube; a patient may pass an SBT and still not be extubated.
C. Extubation is done before liberation, and a tracheostomy is not used as a route to liberation from the ventilator.
D. Liberation refers to weaning by gradually reducing the pressure support level over several days of ventilation.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Passing an SBT demonstrates tolerance of the trial, not all requirements for extubation. The team must additionally assess airway protection, cough, secretion burden and other risks. Liberation from invasive ventilatory support can also occur with a tracheostomy still in place.
**Why not A:** Tolerance of spontaneous breathing does not establish that the patient can protect the airway or clear secretions after tube removal.
**Why not C:** Patients may be liberated via tracheostomy.
**Why not D:** Gradual reduction of support is weaning, a different concept.

**Q2 (application).** A patient has met the unit's SBT readiness criteria on the prescribed FiO₂. A colleague suggests increasing FiO₂ solely for the trial to improve the chance of passing. What does AARC 2024 suggest?
A. Increase the FiO₂ by a fixed step for each trial, because this improves the chance of success.
B. Increase the FiO₂ if the nurse thinks the patient looks anxious before the trial begins.
C. Alternate between a higher and a lower FiO₂ every few minutes to see which one the patient seems to prefer.
D. Do not increase the FiO₂ during the SBT, because raising it can mask hypoxaemia and cause a false pass.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** AARC conditionally suggests not raising FiO₂ solely for the SBT (very low certainty), because this can conceal impaired oxygenation. Verify readiness under the unit protocol; the guideline does not establish a universal starting FiO₂ threshold. If hypoxemia occurs, assess and stop an intolerable trial and provide needed rescue oxygenation. The recommendation is not a reason to withhold emergency oxygen.
**Why not A:** No evidence shows that raising FiO₂ improves success.
**Why not B:** Anxiety is not a reason in the guideline.
**Why not C:** Alternating FiO₂ is not described.

**Q3 (understanding).** Which approach to timing do the AARC authors suggest for SBTs?
A. A standardised approach to assessing for, and if appropriate completing, an SBT before noon each day, with other care coordinated around it.
B. Starting SBTs in the evening, when the patient is more settled and the day's sedation has had hours to wear off.
C. Doing an SBT at a different time every day, so that staff can compare how the patient copes at each time of day.
D. Avoid performing an SBT on the same day as a sedation interruption, so that the two procedures are kept separate and judged one at a time.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The AARC authors suggest a standardised approach to assessing for, and if appropriate completing, an SBT before noon each day, coordinated with other care including sedation interruption (conditional recommendation, very low certainty).
**Why not B:** The guideline recommends before noon.
**Why not C:** The guideline suggests a standard approach, not variation.
**Why not D:** The guideline says SATs and SBTs should be coordinated.

**Q4 (safety).** During an SBT, a patient's respiratory rate rises steadily, SpO₂ falls and the patient looks distressed. What is the best action?
A. Wait until the full 120 minutes are over to see whether the patient adapts to breathing without ventilator support.
B. Increase the FiO₂ on the ventilator and carry on with the trial, so that the SpO₂ stays above the usual target.
C. Stop the poorly tolerated trial under protocol, restore ventilatory support and summon help promptly.
D. Record the observations carefully and review them with the team at the end of the shift or at the next handover.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Rising respiratory rate, falling oxygen saturation and distress indicate possible SBT intolerance. Remain with the patient and promptly stop the trial under the authorized pathway, restore appropriate ventilatory and oxygen support, assess airway, breathing and circulation, and call for urgent assistance. Do not wait for the planned duration or raise oxygen merely to make the trial appear successful.
**Why not A:** Waiting risks harm to an intolerant patient.
**Why not B:** Increasing oxygen to force a pass conceals intolerance; rescue oxygen for hypoxemia is appropriate as part of stopping the trial and treating deterioration.
**Why not D:** The report must be immediate.

**Q5 (understanding).** Why does the AARC guideline suggest the RSBI is not needed to decide readiness for an SBT?
A. Because the RSBI has been shown to be harmful to the patient when it is measured while the patient is at rest on the ventilator.
B. Because the RSBI has moderate sensitivity but poor specificity for extubation success, and requiring it delayed liberation in a trial.
C. Because the RSBI can only be measured by physicians and cannot be measured by nurses or respiratory therapists in any unit.
D. Because modern ventilators can no longer display the breathing rate and tidal volume that are needed to calculate it reliably at the bedside.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The AARC recommendation is conditional, with moderate certainty: RSBI calculation need not be a prerequisite to an SBT. Requiring it can delay liberation and it has limited specificity. Readiness still requires clinical improvement, adequate oxygenation, hemodynamic stability and ability to initiate an inspiratory effort. The index alone does not determine extubation readiness.
**Why not A:** No harm from measurement is claimed.
**Why not C:** Who measures it is not the reason.
**Why not D:** The reason is not technical.

**Q6 (scope).** Which of the following is the respiratory therapist's or prescriber's decision rather than a nursing action?
A. Choosing to extubate the patient after a successful SBT.
B. Observing and recording respiratory pattern, oxygen saturation and comfort during the SBT.
C. Reporting signs of intolerance immediately.
D. Documenting the start and end times of the trial and the outcome.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Deciding to extubate is a prescriber decision after considering airway protection and secretions.
**Why not B:** Observation is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not C:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not D:** Documentation is a nursing responsibility.

## 19. Local protocol note
Local protocols define who screens and runs SBTs, the screening criteria, the technique used (T-piece, low-level pressure support or on-ventilator), the sedation interruption process, monitoring and termination criteria, extubation criteria and the post-extubation plan.

## 20. Evidence, applicability and open questions
- Source quality: an AARC clinical practice guideline using GRADE with a medical librarian and four PICO questions; all four recommendations are conditional and two are very low certainty. The authors note the relative absence of high-level evidence. Authors disclosed relationships with device companies (listed in the paper).
- Applicability: adult mechanically ventilated patients; written for a North American setting where respiratory therapists drive protocols.
- Newly added at recommendation/abstract level: selective cuff-leak assessment in patients at high risk of post-extubation stridor (ATS/CHEST 2017) and risk-stratified post-extubation HFNC/NIV support (ATS 2026). Full recommendation evidence and implementation tables were not reviewed.
- Not covered or only secondary-level: a validated nurse-administered extubation readiness checklist, cuff-leak testing technique, exact post-extubation monitoring frequency, rescue thresholds, tracheostomy liberation, paediatric care, sedation protocols and delirium (see D03-T09, D03-T10).
- Open questions for reviewers (CCP, RT, NE, BN): nursing role in SBT observation; extubation readiness checklist; local oxygenation criteria; coordination with sedation interruption.
