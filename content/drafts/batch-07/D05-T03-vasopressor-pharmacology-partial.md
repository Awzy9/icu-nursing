# Vasopressors and inotropes – classes, mechanisms and nursing monitoring (partial evidence)
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. PARTIAL: only epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine and dobutamine have a pharmacology source here; no doses, concentrations or titration are included.

## 1. Overview
Vasopressors raise blood pressure mainly by narrowing blood vessels; inotropes strengthen the heart's contraction. The Open RN textbook groups epinephrine, norepinephrine and dopamine as catecholamines that stimulate adrenergic (alpha and beta) receptors, and dobutamine as a beta-1 agonist. ISMP classes intravenous adrenergic agonists and inotropic medications as high-alert; the FDA pump page lists vasopressors among infusions that may need an independent double check. The prescriber and pharmacist select the drug, concentration and target; the nurse checks the order, delivers the infusion safely, monitors the response and adverse effects, and escalates. For monitoring targets in shock see D02-T12; for septic shock care see D02-T09.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
These drugs act within minutes, are given by continuous infusion to the sickest patients and have narrow margins. A wrong rate, interruption or extravasation can cause harm quickly, and the nurse is often the first to notice.

## 6. Clinical assessment
**Textbook descriptions (Open RN Nursing Pharmacology):**
- **Epinephrine** (alpha and beta agonist): used by several routes; uses listed include severe allergic reactions, bronchospasm, cardiac resuscitation, hypotension in severe shock and local bleeding control. Nursing considerations: use caution in the elderly and in cardiovascular disease; when given intravenously, monitor blood pressure, heart rate and respiratory rate and the cardiovascular and respiratory systems closely, and monitor the IV site for extravasation; discard the solution if discoloured. The textbook states that if blood pressure rises sharply, rapid-acting vasodilators are given (a prescriber action).
- **Norepinephrine:** a catecholamine used as a peripheral vasoconstrictor (alpha action) and as an inotropic stimulator and coronary dilator (beta action) in patients with critically low blood pressure.
- **Dopamine:** dose-dependent effects (renal vasodilation at low doses, beta effects at moderate doses, vasoconstriction at higher doses, per the textbook); used in shock and to improve perfusion. During infusion monitor ECG, blood pressure, cardiac output, pulse, urine output, and colour and temperature of the limbs. If urine output falls without hypotension, notify the prescriber because the dose may need to be reduced. Adverse effects: hypotension, tachycardia, palpitations, dyspnoea, decreased blood flow to extremities. Concurrent alpha or beta blockers can antagonise dopamine.
- **Dobutamine** (beta-1 agonist): increases heart rate, force of contraction and conduction speed; used in cardiogenic shock and severe heart failure. The textbook says to dilute before intravenous administration, to monitor ECG, blood pressure, cardiac output and urine output continuously, and warns of marked rises in heart rate and blood pressure; report labored breathing, angina, palpitations and dizziness promptly.
- **Phenylephrine:** the textbook sections read describe alpha-1 stimulation only for oral decongestants (and caution in hypertension and with MAOIs); they do not describe its intravenous use as a vasopressor.

**What SSC 2026 says for adults with septic shock (recommendation-level):** norepinephrine as first-line over dopamine, epinephrine or selepressin (strong; certainty high for dopamine, low for the others) and over vasopressin or angiotensin II (conditional; low to very low); add vasopressin if norepinephrine doses escalate (conditional; moderate); add epinephrine if MAP remains inadequate (conditional; very low); suggest against terlipressin (conditional; low); with cardiac dysfunction and persistent hypoperfusion despite adequate fluid and pressure, add dobutamine to norepinephrine or use epinephrine alone (conditional; very low), with inotropes used in addition to, not instead of, vasopressors; suggest against levosimendan (conditional; low); start vasopressors peripherally rather than delay (conditional; very low). These are prescriber decisions and apply to septic shock only.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Check the order (drug, concentration, rate, target), the patient and allergies, and verify pump programming at the moments the FDA page names; obtain a double check where policy requires.
- Monitor continuously as the textbook grids state: ECG and rhythm, blood pressure, perfusion (including limb colour and temperature), urine output, respiratory status; trend the values and report sustained deviation from the ordered target.
- Check the infusion site and line regularly; epinephrine's textbook grid names extravasation monitoring. How to manage extravasation is NOT covered by the sources read (local policy; flagged).
- Do not titrate outside an order or protocol; do not interrupt the infusion without an order; follow your unit's changeover procedure.
- Report adverse effects (arrhythmia, chest pain, marked blood pressure rise, falling urine output, cold or discoloured limbs, breathlessness) promptly.
- Keep high-alert medication safeguards (labelling, double check, smart-pump library) as in D05-T16.
- Document drug, rate, targets, observations, titrations and who authorised them, site checks and escalations.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Blood pressure and MAP against the ordered target; heart rate and rhythm (continuous ECG); perfusion (capillary refill, skin temperature and colour, limb colour and temperature); urine output; respiratory rate and oxygenation; mental state; lactate if ordered; cardiac output if measured; infusion site and line; pump settings and alarms.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Sustained hypotension despite infusion; sudden severe hypertension; new arrhythmia, marked tachycardia or palpitations; chest pain; falling urine output; cold, mottled or blue limbs; breathlessness; pain, swelling, blanching or discolouration at the infusion site; infusion nearly empty or interrupted; pump alarm or malfunction; mismatch between order and pump settings.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |

| Choose the agent, concentration, dose range and target; add, change or stop drugs; manage extravasation or severe hypertension | Prescriber decision (pharmacist for drug and concentration review) |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate for failure to reach the target, rising requirements, new arrhythmia, chest pain, a marked rise in pressure, falling urine output, limb ischaemia signs, site concerns or infusion interruption.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, MAP 55 against an ordered 65 and rising infusion requirement; fingers cool. **B:** septic shock on a vasopressor for 5 hours. **A:** inadequate response with worsening perfusion. **R:** please review now; I am checking the line, pump and site and need a decision about further support.

## 13. Potential complications
Persistent hypotension; excessive hypertension; arrhythmia; reduced blood flow to extremities (named for dopamine); infusion-site injury (extravasation named for epinephrine); under- or over-infusion from pump problems (FDA). Other drug-specific complications are not described in the sources read.

## 14. Documentation
Drug and concentration as ordered; pump rate as programmed; double-check record; targets; observations and trends; titrations with authorisation; perfusion and urine output; site checks; adverse effects; escalations; patient response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At the start of the shift the nurse verifies the infusion against the order and the pump settings, checks the label and the site, records baseline blood pressure, rhythm, limb temperature and urine output, and plans the next syringe changeover so that the infusion is not interrupted.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (understanding).** How does the Open RN textbook describe norepinephrine?
A. A peripheral vasoconstrictor and inotropic stimulator of the heart used in patients with critically low blood pressure.
B. A drug that lowers blood pressure by relaxing blood vessels in patients who have hypertension at presentation.
C. A bronchodilator that acts only on the lungs and has no effect on the blood vessels or the heart.
D. A drug used mainly as a nasal decongestant for symptom relief in patients with upper respiratory infections.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The textbook gives alpha-mediated vasoconstriction and beta-mediated inotropic action.
**Why not B:** Norepinephrine raises blood pressure.
**Why not C:** It acts on vessels and the heart.
**Why not D:** That describes oral phenylephrine and pseudoephedrine.

**Q2 (application).** Which agent does SSC 2026 recommend as the first-line vasopressor in adults with septic shock?
A. Dopamine, because the textbook says that in low doses it dilates the renal vasculature and protects the kidneys.
B. Vasopressin, because it is the only agent on the page that has been given a strong recommendation as first line.
C. Norepinephrine, with a strong recommendation over dopamine, epinephrine and selepressin.
D. Terlipressin, because it acts for longer than other agents and so needs fewer adjustments of the infusion.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The page recommends norepinephrine over dopamine, epinephrine or selepressin (strong).
**Why not A:** The page recommends norepinephrine over dopamine.
**Why not B:** Vasopressin is suggested as an addition, not first line.
**Why not D:** The page suggests against terlipressin.

**Q3 (judgment).** A patient on dopamine has a falling urine output but a stable blood pressure. What does the textbook advise?
A. Increase the infusion rate to improve renal perfusion, without informing the prescriber of the falling urine output.
B. Stop the infusion immediately without telling the prescriber, because the drug seems to be harming the kidneys.
C. Record it and recheck at the end of the shift, since urine output is not important when the pressure is stable.
D. Notify the prescriber, because the dose may need to be reduced.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The dopamine grid says to notify the prescriber if urine output falls without hypotension.
**Why not A:** Dose changes need an order.
**Why not B:** Stopping vasoactive infusions without an order is unsafe.
**Why not C:** The change needs reporting.

**Q4 (safety).** Which monitoring does the textbook describe for a patient receiving dobutamine?
A. Weekly blood tests only, because the drug has effects that only appear after several days of therapy.
B. Continuous monitoring of the ECG, blood pressure, cardiac output and urine output during the whole therapy.
C. Monitoring of the patient's body temperature only, because temperature is the key sign of a drug reaction.
D. No special monitoring is needed, because dobutamine is considered a low-risk drug by the textbook.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** This is the dobutamine grid statement.
**Why not A:** Weekly tests are not adequate for an acute infusion.
**Why not C:** Temperature is not the key parameter.
**Why not D:** The drug can cause marked heart rate and pressure rises.

**Q5 (application).** Which nursing check does the epinephrine grid specifically name for intravenous administration?
A. Checking the patient's weight each hour, because weight changes show how much of the drug has been absorbed.
B. Weighing the syringe before each use, because the textbook says the drug concentration must be confirmed by weight.
C. Monitoring the IV site for extravasation while the drug is given by the intravenous route.
D. Measuring the room temperature, because the drug is destroyed if the room is warmer than the recommended level.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The grid says to monitor the IV site for extravasation.
**Why not A:** Hourly weights are not part of the epinephrine grid.
**Why not B:** The textbook does not describe weighing the syringe as a check on the drug.
**Why not D:** Room temperature is not a monitoring item in the grid.

**Q6 (scope).** Which decision belongs to the prescriber, with pharmacist input, rather than the bedside nurse?
A. Selecting the vasopressor and deciding to add a second agent.
B. Checking the pump settings against the order each time a new syringe is connected to the infusion line.
C. Recording limb colour, limb temperature and urine output at the intervals that the unit policy requires.
D. Reporting a sudden fall in blood pressure to the prescriber immediately, together with the pump and line status.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Choosing and adding agents is prescribing.
**Why not B:** Checking is a nursing safety task.
**Why not C:** Observation and recording are nursing tasks.
**Why not D:** Reporting is a nursing task.
