# Acid–base disturbances and arterial blood gas interpretation – a stepwise nursing approach
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. A basic four-disorder stepwise method from a general textbook plus a 2013 sampling guideline; includes one worked example of a mixed disturbance.

## 1. Overview
Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis reports the blood's pH, the partial pressures of carbon dioxide (PaCO₂) and oxygen (PaO₂), bicarbonate (HCO₃) and oxygen saturation. The AARC guideline says blood gas analysis and hemoximetry are recommended for evaluating a patient's ventilatory, acid–base and/or oxygenation status (1A) and suggested for judging the response to therapy (2B). The nurse obtains or helps obtain a good specimen, labels and transports it correctly, interprets the result in a stepwise way as a basis for reporting, links the result to the patient's condition, and escalates abnormal results. The prescriber and respiratory therapist decide on treatment and ventilator changes.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Name the ABG components and the textbook's adult reference ranges, and explain why the laboratory's own ranges apply.
2. Apply the four-step method: pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃, compensation.
3. Identify respiratory acidosis, respiratory alkalosis, metabolic acidosis and metabolic alkalosis, and the clues in a patient's presentation.
4. Describe the specimen requirements and common causes of an unreliable result.
5. State when a venous or capillary sample is or is not an acceptable substitute, according to the guideline.
6. Separate nursing actions from prescriber and respiratory therapist decisions, and state what the sources do not cover.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
ICU patients commonly have disturbed ventilation, perfusion, kidney function and metabolism. The textbook notes that failure to recognise ABG abnormalities can have serious consequences and gives critical values that mean serious risk if not corrected rapidly. Correct interpretation helps the nurse and team judge treatment effect. A wrong specimen can lead to inappropriate treatment, which the AARC guideline lists as a hazard.

## 6. Clinical assessment
**Components and textbook adult values:** pH 7.35–7.45; PaCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg (managed by the lungs); HCO₃ 22–26 mEq/L (managed by the kidneys); PaO₂ 80–100 mm Hg; SaO₂ 95–100 %. Critical values the textbook lists: pH below 7.25 or above 7.60; PaCO₂ below 25 or above 60 mm Hg; HCO₃ below 10 or above 40 mEq/L; PaO₂ below 60 mm Hg; SaO₂ below 88 %. Check your laboratory's ranges and critical-value policy.

**Stepwise method (Open RN and Merck Manual):**
1. **pH:** below 7.35 is acidemia; above 7.45 is alkalemia.
2. **PaCO₂:** if abnormal, ask whether the problem is respiratory: PaCO₂ moves in the opposite direction to pH ("respiratory opposite"). pH below 7.35 with PaCO₂ above 45 is respiratory acidosis; pH above 7.45 with PaCO₂ below 35 is respiratory alkalosis.
3. **HCO₃:** check for a metabolic process even when PaCO₂ is abnormal: HCO₃ moves in the same direction as pH ("metabolic equal"). pH below 7.35 with HCO₃ below 22 is metabolic acidosis; pH above 7.45 with HCO₃ above 26 is metabolic alkalosis.
4. **Compensation:** Compare the observed response with the expected response for the suspected primary disturbance. A pH within the reference interval can occur with a mixed disturbance. A PaCO₂ within its reference interval can still be too high for a metabolic acidosis. For example, with HCO₃ of 12 mEq/L, Winter’s formula (1.5 × HCO₃ + 8 ± 2) predicts PaCO₂ of 24–28 mm Hg. A PaCO₂ of 40 mm Hg in this setting suggests a concurrent respiratory acidosis. Interpret results with the clinical history and trend. The lungs compensate rapidly for metabolic disorders. Sustained renal adaptation to respiratory disorders takes longer.

**Clinical clues the textbook gives (not diagnostic):**
- **Respiratory acidosis** (retained CO₂): chronic lung disease may be compensated and better tolerated; acute respiratory acidosis can cause confusion and reduced consciousness. SaO₂ may be normal with hypercapnia and should not decide the assessment. Assess airway, breathing and circulation and seek urgent help. Textbook figures for loss of consciousness (PaCO₂ above 75–80 mm Hg in normal lungs, 90–100 in chronic hypercapnia) are given as a general guide.
- **Respiratory alkalosis** (too much CO₂ removed): anxiety, pain, head injury, mechanical ventilation, or early response to hypoxaemia and conditions such as pulmonary embolism or asthma; symptoms include breathlessness, dizziness, chest tightness, tingling; the underlying cause may be life-threatening. The textbook says breathing into a paper bag is no longer recommended because it can cause significant hypoxaemia.
- **Metabolic acidosis:** common causes include diabetic ketoacidosis and lactic acidosis; the nurse may first notice rapid breathing, confusion, reduced consciousness or hypotension; notify promptly so an ABG can be drawn.
- **Metabolic alkalosis:** prolonged vomiting, nasogastric suction, diuretics and bicarbonate administration are named; hypokalaemia can shift hydrogen ions into cells; the nurse may notice slow breathing or confusion.

**Specimen (AARC 2013):**
- Analyser and personnel must be trained and the analyser validated; the specimen must be properly anticoagulated, free of visible air bubbles, and properly labelled with the patient's full name and a unique identifier, with the requisition information the laboratory requires (patient location, ordering clinician, sample source and site, breathing frequency, FiO₂ or oxygen flow, ventilator settings).
- Storage: a sample kept at room temperature in a plastic container for more than 30 minutes, or at room temperature with high leukocyte or platelet counts, is a contraindication; samples that must be kept longer should be in glass and chilled; very high leukocyte counts need immediate cooling and analysis.
- Errors can arise from clotting, air contamination, contamination with saline or other fluids when taken from an indwelling catheter, accidental venous sampling when an artery was intended, and delays; high-speed transport tube systems can alter PO₂ results.
- **Venous and capillary samples:** the guideline does not recommend a peripheral venous PO₂ as a substitute for PaO₂ (1A), does not recommend using venous PCO₂ and pH as a substitute for arterial PaCO₂ and pH (2B), and does not recommend capillary samples to determine oxygenation (1A). When an arterial sample cannot be obtained, a central venous or capillary sample is preferable to a peripheral venous sample for acid–base status, in specific circumstances and with an accurate analyser. Central venous oxygen saturation is suggested in early goal-directed therapy (2B).

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Ensure the right patient, order and labelling, and record the patient's FiO₂ or oxygen flow, ventilator settings and sample source on the request.
- Obtain or assist with the sample following the unit's procedure (arterial line draw or arterial puncture is not described in the sources beyond the points above; arterial-line technique is unresolved in this curriculum – see D02-T04).
- Send promptly, avoid air bubbles and delays, and tell the laboratory if the sample was drawn from a line, where, and what fluids were infusing.
- Interpret stepwise, correlate with the patient's breathing, perfusion, mental state and treatment, and report abnormal or critical values immediately.
- Do not rely on SpO₂ alone to judge ventilation.
- Report patterns suggesting a cause the team can treat (hypoventilation, hypoperfusion, vomiting, diuretics, DKA, kidney failure) and monitor the response to the ordered intervention.
- Document the time, the values, the patient's condition, actions and who was informed.
- Knowledge limits: this lesson introduces expected compensation and mixed disorders through one example. It does not cover anion-gap analysis, lactate interpretation or oxygenation indices.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃, PaO₂, SaO₂ and the trend; respiratory rate and pattern; work of breathing; SpO₂ and end-tidal CO₂ if available; ventilator settings; perfusion, lactate if measured; mental state; urine output; electrolytes (especially potassium); glucose and ketones when relevant.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Critical values per the laboratory; a falling pH with rising PaCO₂ and drowsiness; a very low or very high pH; severe tachypnoea or deep rapid breathing; slow or shallow breathing with confusion; falling consciousness; hypotension; persistent acidosis in a patient with poor perfusion or kidney failure; an unreliable sample (air bubble, delay, clot, wrong source) that could cause wrong treatment; a normal SpO₂ with a rising PaCO₂.

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for critical ABG values, a worsening trend, a change in consciousness, respiratory distress, haemodynamic instability, or a result that does not match the patient.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, ABG shows pH 7.23, PaCO₂ 62, HCO₃ 25; patient drowsy, respiratory rate 8. **B:** on opioid infusion, post-operative day 1. **A:** acute respiratory acidosis (hypoventilation). **R:** please attend now; I am maintaining airway and breathing support and need an order to review the sedation or opioid and the ventilation plan.

## 13. Potential complications
Respiratory failure, arrhythmia, depressed consciousness, hypotension and circulatory collapse (as described for the disorders in the textbook); inappropriate treatment from an unacceptable specimen or incorrect reporting (AARC); infection risk to the sample handler (AARC).

## 14. Documentation
Date, time and source of the sample; FiO₂ or oxygen flow and ventilator settings; results and the nurse's interpretation; patient assessment; actions; people informed; repeat results and response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
A nurse takes a sample from an arterial line, notes the FiO₂ and ventilator settings on the request, removes air bubbles, labels the sample with two identifiers, sends it at once to the analyser, and reviews the result with the patient's breathing and consciousness before reporting.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 54-year-old man with vomiting has a pH of 7.48, PaCO₂ 46 and HCO₃ 33. The nurse recognises metabolic alkalosis (pH and HCO₃ both high, with PaCO₂ near the expected range for respiratory compensation), reports it with the history of vomiting and nasogastric suction, checks the patient's mental state and breathing, and records the values; the prescriber decides on treatment of the cause.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (application).** A nurse reviews a patient's arterial blood gas: pH 7.27, PaCO₂ 58 mm Hg and HCO₃ 26 mEq/L. What does the textbook's method identify?
A. Metabolic acidosis, because the pH is below 7.35 and the bicarbonate level is within its normal range.
B. Respiratory alkalosis, because a raised PaCO₂ always makes the blood more alkaline and raises the pH.
C. Metabolic alkalosis, because the PaCO₂ is higher than the normal range and the bicarbonate is unchanged.
D. Respiratory acidosis, because the pH is low and the PaCO₂ is high, moving in opposite directions.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The acidemia and raised PaCO₂ support respiratory acidosis. HCO₃ of 26 mEq/L is compatible with the small rise seen in acute respiratory acidosis.
**Why not A:** Low bicarbonate is the primary change in metabolic acidosis. The raised PaCO₂ explains the acidemia here.
**Why not B:** A high PaCO₂ makes the blood more acidic.
**Why not C:** The pH is low, not high, so metabolic alkalosis does not fit this blood gas.

**Q2 (judgment).** A patient has a rising PaCO₂ and is increasingly drowsy, but SpO₂ is 96 % on oxygen. What is the best interpretation?
A. The patient is fine, because the oxygen saturation is within the target range, so the rest can wait.
B. Oxygen saturation can be normal in hypercapnia, so the nurse should assess airway, breathing and circulation and seek urgent help.
C. The patient is only sleepy because of the time of day, so the nurse should leave the patient to rest quietly for now and look again later.
D. The ABG result must be wrong, so the nurse should repeat it after a few hours before telling anyone.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The textbook states SaO₂ may be normal with hypercapnia and should not be the determining factor.
**Why not A:** A normal SpO₂ does not exclude hypoventilation.
**Why not C:** Drowsiness with rising CO₂ is a warning sign.
**Why not D:** Delaying the response risks deterioration.

**Q3 (safety).** A nurse draws an arterial sample and notices a large air bubble in the syringe. According to the AARC guideline, what is the correct action?
A. Send the sample as it is and mention the bubble on the request form so that the laboratory can allow for it in its report.
B. Warm the syringe in the hand for several minutes and then send it, so that the bubble dissolves into the blood.
C. Treat the sample as unacceptable, because a specimen with visible air bubbles is a contraindication, and obtain a new one.
D. Add some saline to the syringe to replace the lost volume and then send the sample to the analyser.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The guideline lists a specimen containing visible air bubbles as a contraindication to analysis, and air as a source of erroneous results.
**Why not A:** The result could lead to inappropriate treatment.
**Why not B:** Warming does not correct air contamination.
**Why not D:** Saline contamination is itself a cause of error.

**Q4 (application).** The ABG shows pH 7.52, PaCO₂ 28 and HCO₃ 22. The patient is anxious, in pain and breathing fast. Which disorder does this fit?
A. Respiratory alkalosis, with a high pH and a low PaCO₂ and a normal bicarbonate.
B. Metabolic acidosis, because the patient is breathing fast, which is the usual sign of an acid excess.
C. Respiratory acidosis, because an anxious patient is likely to be retaining carbon dioxide in the blood.
D. Metabolic alkalosis, because the bicarbonate is within its normal range and the pH is high.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** pH above 7.45 with PaCO₂ below 35 is respiratory alkalosis; anxiety and pain are listed causes of hyperventilation.
**Why not B:** The pH is high, which does not fit an acidosis, and the HCO₃ is normal.
**Why not C:** The PaCO₂ is low, which does not fit respiratory acidosis, where it would be raised.
**Why not D:** Metabolic alkalosis needs a high HCO₃.

**Q5 (understanding).** What does the AARC 2013 guideline say about using a peripheral venous sample's PO₂ to assess oxygenation?
A. It is a good substitute for PaO₂ in these patients, as long as the sample is taken promptly.
B. It is not recommended as a substitute for an arterial PaO₂ (grade 1A).
C. It is acceptable in children, in whom venous samples are considered equivalent to arterial ones.
D. It is the preferred sample for ventilated patients because it avoids the risks of arterial puncture.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The AARC 2013 guideline recommends against using a peripheral venous PO2 as a substitute for arterial PaO2 (grade 1A, recommendation 14.7). The recommendation is not limited to children, and the arterial sample remains the reference.
**Why not A:** The guideline recommends against it.
**Why not C:** The recommendation is not limited to children.
**Why not D:** An arterial sample is the reference.

**Q6 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber (and respiratory therapist for the ventilator) rather than the bedside nurse?
A. Labelling the sample with two patient identifiers and recording the sample source on the request form.
B. Reporting a critical pH to the responsible clinician immediately, with the patient's clinical state.
C. Documenting the FiO₂ and the ventilator settings on the request as the guideline lists them.
D. Deciding to change ventilator settings or give a buffering drug in response to the result.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Treatment and ventilator changes are prescriber decisions, with the respiratory therapist operating the ventilator.
**Why not A:** Labelling is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not B:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not C:** Recording is a nursing responsibility.
