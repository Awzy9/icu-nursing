# Endotracheal tube care – securement, position and cuff pressure
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Evidence is mostly low certainty; many practices are local protocol.

## 1. Overview
The endotracheal tube (ETT) is the patient's airway. Nursing care aims to keep the tube in the right place, the cuff sealed without damaging the trachea, the skin and mouth healthy, and the patient safe from accidental extubation. Tube insertion, repositioning, exchange and extubation are performed by trained clinicians per policy.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe what to check about tube position and securement and when.
2. State the AARC position on measuring cuff pressure and on continuous cuff monitoring, and the SHEA position.
3. Explain how device-related pressure injury is prevented around the tube and securement.
4. Recognise and respond to cuff leak, tube displacement and obstruction.
5. Separate nursing actions from physician and respiratory-therapy decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
An unrecognised displaced or obstructed tube is an immediate threat to life; an under-inflated cuff allows secretions to pass the cuff, and an over-inflated cuff can reduce mucosal blood flow and cause tracheal injury. Skin, lip and mouth injuries are common and preventable.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Position and securement:** the documented tube depth at the teeth or lips, securement intact, no kinks or biting, tube and circuit not under tension, chest movement bilateral, breath sounds, ventilator waveforms and volumes; waveform capnography is advised to confirm tracheal placement (ERC ALS). AARC suggests assessing proper placement and securement (conditional recommendation, very low certainty).
- **Cuff:** measure cuff pressure with a manometer (AARC strong recommendation, high certainty); signs of leak (audible air, falling expired volume, alarm, need for rising inflation).
- **Skin and mouth:** AARC strongly recommends assessing the skin surrounding artificial airways; look at lips, corners of the mouth, face under tapes, tongue, oral mucosa; the pressure-injury guide advises regular checks under devices and securements.
- **Secretions and humidification:** see the suctioning and humidification content; AARC suggests assessing humidification adequacy and device.
- **Patient:** agitation, sedation level, cough, comfort, ability to communicate by other means.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Check and document tube position (depth marking) and securement at least as often as policy requires and after every move, turn, transfer or procedure.
- Use a manometer to measure cuff pressure at the interval set by unit policy; do not rely on feeling the pilot balloon (AARC: palpation is inaccurate compared with manometers). AARC's narrative cites an appropriate cuff pressure range of 20–30 cmH₂O (not a graded recommendation) and notes that no consensus exists on how often to measure.
- Do not use continuous cuff-pressure monitoring as a VAP-prevention measure (AARC strong recommendation against it for that purpose; SHEA also classes automated or frequent monitoring as not recommended for VAP prevention). Neither document says intermittent checks should stop.
- Re-secure the tube per policy using two staff when needed; take care not to pull or displace; move the tube from side to side of the mouth only according to policy; reduce pressure under securements (pressure-injury guide: check under and around devices, support or reposition them, consider preventive dressings).
- Keep the head of the bed raised unless contraindicated (SHEA essential practice) and provide mouth care with toothbrushing and no chlorhexidine (SHEA).
- Prevent unplanned extubation: assess agitation, optimise analgesia and sedation per protocol, avoid unnecessary circuit traction, and use restraints only per policy.
- Call for help immediately for suspected displacement, obstruction or cuff failure.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Tube depth and securement each shift and with moves; cuff pressure at policy intervals; SpO₂, ETCO₂ and waveform; expired tidal volume and airway pressures; secretions; skin and mouth condition; sedation and agitation; head-of-bed angle; mouth-care times.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Loss of capnography trace; sudden desaturation; loss of chest movement or breath sounds on one side; audible leak or falling expired volume; rising airway pressures or inability to pass a suction catheter; patient biting or pulling at the tube; tube moved from the recorded depth; visible tube in the mouth; swelling, bleeding or blistering of lip, mouth or skin; secretions around the cuff; cuff pressure repeatedly low or very high.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Immediate escalation for: suspected displacement or obstruction, loss of capnography trace, severe desaturation, cuff failure that cannot be corrected, accidental extubation, or new bleeding from the airway.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, tube has moved out by 3 cm from the recorded depth and there is an audible leak. **B:** day 5 ventilation, agitated overnight, last cuff check [time]. **A:** possible partial displacement. **R:** please come now; I am holding the tube secure, giving oxygen and monitoring; I have not re-inflated beyond policy.

## 13. Potential complications
Accidental extubation, endobronchial intubation, obstruction, aspiration around the cuff, tracheal injury from over-inflation, pressure injury of lips, mouth, nose or face, ventilator-associated pneumonia, laryngeal injury.

## 14. Documentation
Tube size and depth at the lips or teeth; securement method and changes; cuff pressure and time; skin and mouth findings; capnography confirmation; events (leak, displacement, agitation) and responses; escalation.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At the start of the shift you check the depth mark against the record, look at the securement, measure the cuff pressure with a manometer and record it, look at the lips and corners of the mouth under the tapes, check the capnography trace, raise the head of the bed, and brush the teeth per protocol.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (monitoring).** Which method of checking cuff pressure is supported by the AARC 2024 guideline?
A. Feeling the pilot balloon with the fingers and judging whether it is firm enough.
B. Measuring with a manometer at the interval set by the unit's policy.
C. Continuous automatic monitoring for every ventilated patient to prevent pneumonia.
D. Checking only when the alarm sounds for a leak or a low volume.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** AARC strongly recommends manometer assessment; no interval is specified, so policy decides.
**Why not A:** Palpation is inaccurate compared with a manometer.
**Why not C:** AARC strongly recommends against continuous monitoring to reduce VAP risk.
**Why not D:** Waiting for an alarm leaves over- or under-inflation unmeasured.

**Q2 (safety).** The depth mark of an ETT has moved from 22 cm to 25 cm at the lips and the expired tidal volume has fallen. What should the nurse do?
A. Push the tube back to 22 cm quickly yourself and chart that the position has been corrected and confirmed.
B. Continue the hourly observations because the SpO₂ is still above 94% and the patient looks comfortable.
C. Remove the securement to allow the tube to find its natural position, then re-tape it once it has settled.
D. Support the tube, call urgently for the doctor or respiratory therapist, monitor SpO₂ and capnography, and prepare to assist.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Possible displacement is an airway emergency; repositioning is a clinician's task.
**Why not A:** Advancing or withdrawing the tube is not a nursing decision, and the position may be wrong.
**Why not B:** Delay risks accidental extubation.
**Why not C:** Removing the securement increases the risk of extubation.

**Q3 (judgment).** A colleague says SHEA 2022 means cuff pressure no longer needs to be measured. What is the best reply?
A. Agree, because pressure checks do not prevent any complication and the measurements just take staff time.
B. Say cuff checks should be performed every ten minutes for every patient, whatever the unit policy says.
C. Say the SHEA statement concerns VAP prevention only; AARC still recommends manometer checks at the unit interval.
D. Say cuff pressure only matters for patients who are awake and able to cough around the tube.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The statements concern VAP prevention; intermittent manometer assessment remains recommended by AARC.
**Why not A:** Over-inflation can injure the trachea, so checks have other purposes.
**Why not B:** No such interval is stated.
**Why not D:** Cuff pressure matters in all intubated patients.

**Q4 (application).** Which approach best reduces pressure injury from the tube and its securement?
A. Check the skin and mucosa under and around the tube and securement regularly, support the device, and consider a preventive dressing.
B. Tighten the tapes so that no movement can occur at the lips, because friction is the main cause of skin damage here.
C. Leave the securement unchanged for the whole admission unless it comes loose, to avoid disturbing the tube.
D. Apply moisturiser under the securement before each tape change, so that the skin stays soft and does not crack.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The pressure-injury guide advises regular checks under devices, redistributing pressure, and preventive dressings.
**Why not B:** Tight securement increases pressure.
**Why not C:** Long periods without skin checks risk unnoticed injury.
**Why not D:** Added moisture under a device can worsen skin damage.

**Q5 (prioritization).** Which finding needs the nurse's attention first?
A. A mild redness at the corner of the mouth where the tape sits, with intact skin.
B. A cuff pressure measurement that is due at the next scheduled check.
C. The head of the bed at 30° with the oral care completed.
D. A flat capnography trace with falling SpO₂ and no chest movement.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Loss of capnography with desaturation suggests displacement or obstruction.
**Why not A:** Mild redness with intact skin needs care but is not an immediate threat.
**Why not B:** A scheduled routine check can wait for its time.
**Why not C:** This describes correct care, not a problem.

**Q6 (scope).** Which action is a physician or respiratory-therapy decision?
A. Measuring cuff pressure with a manometer at the policy interval.
B. Repositioning the tube or exchanging it for a new one.
C. Recording the tube depth and securement after a turn.
D. Reporting redness under the securement.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Tube repositioning or exchange belongs to trained clinicians per policy.
**Why not A:** A nursing assessment task.
**Why not C:** A nursing documentation task.
**Why not D:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
