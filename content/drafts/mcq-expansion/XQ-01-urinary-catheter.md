# Urinary catheter care (CAUTI prevention)
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Unpublished; never imported into the question bank.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A ventilated patient has had an indwelling urinary catheter for three days to measure hourly output. He is now off sedation, stable and mobilising with physiotherapy, with no retention. What is the best nursing contribution on the morning round?
A. Ask the prescriber whether the catheter is still needed.
B. Leave the catheter in until discharge from the unit, because removing it would increase the nursing workload on the ward.
C. Remove the catheter yourself without an order, because the patient is now mobilising and looks well.
D. Change the catheter and drainage bag for a new set to reduce the infection risk on day three.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The CDC guideline advises inserting catheters only for appropriate indications and leaving them only as long as needed; the nurse prompts the daily review and the prescriber decides.
**Why not B:** Convenience is not an indication, and prolonged catheter days raise infection risk.
**Why not C:** Removal needs an order or an approved nurse-led protocol; the nurse's role is to raise the question.
**Why not D:** Routine fixed-interval changes are not recommended; changes follow clinical indications.
**Trace:** N-CDC-CAUTI-2009 — Appropriate indications, leave only as long as needed (IB); no routine fixed-interval changes (II).

**Q2 (application).** You help a catheterised patient from bed to a chair. The drainage bag is hanging on the arm of the chair, level with his abdomen. What should you do?
A. Leave the bag on the chair arm so urine can drain by gravity into the bag while he is seated.
B. Rest the bag on the floor beside the chair so it hangs well below the bladder and stays upright.
C. Clamp the tubing for the time he is sitting so no urine reaches the bag and nothing can spill.
D. Hang the bag below bladder level, off the floor, with the tubing free of kinks, before he sits.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The guideline says to keep the collecting bag below the level of the bladder at all times, not resting on the floor, and to keep the tubing free from kinks.
**Why not A:** A bag at bladder level or higher allows urine to flow back toward the bladder.
**Why not B:** The guideline also says the bag must not rest on the floor, because that risks contamination.
**Why not C:** Clamping obstructs flow, and unobstructed urine flow is a recommended practice.
**Trace:** N-CDC-CAUTI-2009 — Keep bag below bladder, not on floor; keep catheter and tubing free from kinking (IB).

**Q3 (safety).** During a turn, the junction between a catheter and its drainage tube separates and the tube end touches the bed sheet. What is the best action?
A. Wipe the tube end with an alcohol swab, reconnect it and carry on, because the interruption was only brief.
B. Flush the tube end with antiseptic solution and reconnect, which should make the system safe again.
C. Replace the catheter and collecting system aseptically, using sterile equipment, and report the breach.
D. Leave the junction open to drain into a clean container until the doctor arrives to review it.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The guideline says that if aseptic technique breaks or the system disconnects or leaks, the catheter and collecting system should be replaced using aseptic technique and sterile equipment.
**Why not A:** Reconnecting a contaminated closed system breaks the closed-drainage principle.
**Why not B:** Antiseptic instillation is not a recommended way to restore a broken closed system.
**Why not D:** An open system increases infection risk and does not fix the breach.
**Trace:** N-CDC-CAUTI-2009 — If breaks in aseptic technique, disconnection or leakage occur, replace catheter and collecting system aseptically (IB).

**Q4 (scope).** A colleague suggests inserting a catheter in a patient whose skin is intact but who is incontinent overnight, 'to save time on the night shift'. What is the best response?
A. Agree, since a catheter is the safest way to keep a bed dry and protects the skin from moisture.
B. Explain that the guideline advises against catheters for managing incontinence, and discuss alternatives with the team.
C. Agree, provided the catheter is changed every few days so any infection risk stays low.
D. Insert it after asking the family for consent, since consent makes the use appropriate.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The guideline says to avoid urinary catheters for managing incontinence, and lists external catheters as an option in suitable patients.
**Why not A:** Convenience is not an appropriate indication, and the catheter itself adds infection risk.
**Why not C:** Routine fixed-interval changes are not recommended and do not make the indication appropriate.
**Why not D:** Consent does not create an indication; the use still has to meet the guideline's appropriate indications.
**Trace:** N-CDC-CAUTI-2009 — Avoid urinary catheters for management of incontinence (IB); consider external catheters in cooperative male patients (II).

**Q5 (judgment).** A nurse plans to clean around the urethral meatus of a catheterised patient with an antiseptic solution every shift to prevent infection. What should you advise?
A. Routine hygiene such as cleansing the meatal surface during daily bathing is appropriate.
B. Continue each shift, because antiseptic cleaning around the meatus is the main way to prevent infection.
C. Increase to every few hours, because more frequent antiseptic cleaning should lower the infection risk further.
D. Stop all cleaning of the area so the catheter site is never disturbed during the admission.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The guideline says not to clean the periurethral area with antiseptics to prevent CAUTI while the catheter is in place, and that routine hygiene is appropriate.
**Why not B:** This is the practice the guideline advises against.
**Why not C:** More frequent antiseptic cleaning is not recommended either.
**Why not D:** Routine hygiene during daily bathing is still appropriate.
**Trace:** N-CDC-CAUTI-2009 — Do not clean periurethral area with antiseptics to prevent CAUTI while catheter in place; routine hygiene appropriate (IB).

**Q6 (application).** On a busy shift a nurse empties four patients' catheter bags into one measuring jug, rinsing it only at the end. What is the issue?
A. There is no issue, because the jug is rinsed afterwards and all four patients are on the same unit.
B. The issue is the time spent, so empty the bags together and record one combined volume for all four.
C. The jug is acceptable if the spigots touch the rim, since the rim is cleaned between patients.
D. Each bag should be emptied into a separate, clean container, avoiding splashing and contact between the spigot and the container.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The guideline says to empty the bag regularly using a separate, clean collecting container for each patient and to prevent contact of the drainage spigot with the container.
**Why not A:** A shared container can carry organisms between patients; rinsing at the end is not enough.
**Why not B:** Combined volumes also lose each patient's accurate output record.
**Why not C:** Spigot contact with the container is itself the thing to avoid.
**Trace:** N-CDC-CAUTI-2009 — Empty bag using a separate clean container for each patient; avoid splashing and spigot contact (IB).
