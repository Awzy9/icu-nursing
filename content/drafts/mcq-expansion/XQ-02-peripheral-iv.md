# Peripheral IV care (CDC 2011)

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (application).** A patient has a peripheral catheter covered by an opaque gauze dressing and has no complaints or signs of infection. How should you assess the site on your daily check?
A. Remove the dressing every shift to inspect the skin, then apply a fresh one to keep the site clean.
B. Skip the check until the patient complains of pain, because tenderness is the sign to rely on.
C. Inspect the edge of the gauze and the tubing, since the skin under it is hidden from view.
D. Palpate through the dressing for tenderness and leave the gauze in place unless there are signs of infection.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Assess the site through the dressing, using inspection when visible and palpation for tenderness. WHO 2024 recommends at least daily site and vein assessment irrespective of the replacement schedule. An intact opaque dressing need not be removed just to inspect an asymptomatic site; new tenderness or other concerning findings require reassessment.
**Why not A:** Needless removal is not advised when there are no signs of infection.
**Why not B:** The guideline asks for a daily evaluation, not waiting for symptoms.
**Why not C:** Palpation through the dressing is the recommended way to assess tenderness.
**Trace:** S-CDC-BSI-2011, S-WHO-PIVC-2024 — Daily assessment, opaque dressing precautions and site inspection regardless of replacement schedule.

**Q2 (judgment).** During a peripheral infusion assessment, the nurse finds warmth, erythema and a tender venous cord. The assessment suggests phlebitis rather than suspected vesicant extravasation. Which response addresses the complication while preserving access for necessary therapy?
A. Flush the catheter gently and continue the infusion, then recheck the site at the next routine round.
B. Apply a warm compress over the dressing and keep the catheter, because the symptoms may settle.
C. Stop the infusion, remove or arrange removal of the catheter per policy, and report the findings.
D. Cover the area with a second dressing to protect the vein and record the finding in the notes.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** CDC advises removing a peripheral catheter if the patient develops phlebitis (warmth, tenderness, erythema or a palpable venous cord), infection or a malfunctioning catheter. If extravasation is suspected instead, stop delivery and follow the product-specific injury pathway before removing access, since it may be needed for trained assessment or treatment. Coordinate suitable alternative access for essential therapy; do not continue delivery through the affected catheter.
**Why not A:** Continuing to use a catheter with signs of phlebitis risks harm and infection.
**Why not B:** Comfort measures do not replace removal when phlebitis is present.
**Why not D:** Covering the site does not address the problem and delays removal.
**Trace:** S-CDC-BSI-2011 — Remove peripheral venous catheters if phlebitis, infection or malfunction (IB).

**Q3 (safety).** An adult's peripheral cannula was placed in a foot vein during an emergency. What should the nurse do once the emergency has settled?
A. Leave it until the routine replacement time, since the foot site works and the patient has no pain.
B. Ask for it to be replaced with an upper-extremity site as soon as possible.
C. Keep it because lower-limb veins are larger and carry a lower risk of infection in adults.
D. Cover it with a bandage and elevate the leg so the site is protected from movement.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** CDC advises using an upper-extremity site in adults and replacing a catheter placed in a lower extremity as soon as possible.
**Why not A:** The guideline says to replace it as soon as possible, not at the routine time.
**Why not C:** This reverses the guideline's advice for adults.
**Why not D:** Protection does not address the preferred site recommendation.
**Trace:** S-CDC-BSI-2011 — In adults use upper-extremity site; replace lower-extremity catheter as soon as possible (II).

**Q4 (application).** After applying skin antiseptic to a patient's forearm, you want to feel for the vein once more before inserting a peripheral cannula. What should you do?
A. Not palpate the site again unless aseptic technique is maintained, for example by using a sterile glove.
B. Palpate with a clean bare finger, because the antiseptic has already killed the organisms on the skin.
C. Palpate with a clean nonsterile glove once the antiseptic dries, then insert straight away.
D. Apply more antiseptic after palpating and insert before it dries to save time on the procedure.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** CDC says palpation of the insertion site should not be performed after the application of antiseptic unless aseptic technique is maintained.
**Why not B:** Touching the prepared site contaminates it, which is why the guideline restricts re-palpation.
**Why not C:** A nonsterile glove does not maintain asepsis when touching the prepared insertion site. Dryness alone does not make that contact safe.
**Why not D:** Re-applying antiseptic does not make unprotected palpation acceptable.
**Trace:** S-CDC-BSI-2011 — Palpation after antiseptic only if aseptic technique maintained (IB hand hygiene and aseptic technique).

**Q5 (judgment).** A patient needs about ten days of intravenous therapy and has poor peripheral veins. What can the nurse sensibly raise with the prescriber?
A. Whether to keep resiting short peripheral catheters every day, because that avoids any catheter-related infection.
B. Whether the same peripheral cannula can stay for the full ten days if the dressing is kept dry.
C. Whether a steel needle can be used, because it is quick to place and easy to remove.
D. Whether a midline catheter or a PICC would suit better than repeated short peripheral catheters.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** CDC's 2011 recommendation supports considering a midline or PICC for longer IV treatment. Select access with the vascular-access team according to the infusate, vessel health, patient risks and intended duration, rather than duration alone.
**Why not A:** Frequent resiting is not what the guideline recommends for longer therapy.
**Why not B:** A dry dressing is not proof of suitability. WHO 2024 conditionally permits scheduled or clinically indicated removal; neither strategy removes the need for daily assessment and timely removal for complications or lack of need.
**Why not C:** Steel needles are not recommended where tissue necrosis could follow extravasation.
**Trace:** S-CDC-BSI-2011, S-WHO-PIVC-2024 — CDC longer-therapy access recommendation; WHO removal strategy and reassessment principles, not a universal device selection rule.

**Q6 (scope).** A colleague proposes a steel winged needle for a drug that can cause tissue necrosis if it leaks out of the vein. What is the best response?
A. Accept it, because a steel needle is easier to remove if the drug starts to leak into the tissue.
B. Accept it if it is secured firmly with tape and checked every few minutes by two staff.
C. Avoid it and ask for a suitable catheter.
D. Accept it for one dose only, since a single short use should carry little risk of harm.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** CDC advises avoiding steel needles for fluids and medication that might cause tissue necrosis if extravasation occurs (Category IA).
**Why not A:** Rigid needles are more likely to damage the vein wall and extravasate.
**Why not B:** Close checking does not make a steel needle acceptable here.
**Why not D:** The advice is not limited by the number of doses.
**Trace:** S-CDC-BSI-2011 — Avoid steel needles for fluids/medication that might cause tissue necrosis if extravasation occurs (IA).
