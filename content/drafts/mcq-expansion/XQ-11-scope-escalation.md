# Scope of practice and escalation across the skills library

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (scope).** A patient's chest drain is bubbling and the nurse thinks suction might help. Who decides on suction and its pressure?
A. The nurse, because the nurse is the one who observes the drain most often.
B. The bedside nurse and the patient together, once the patient says it is comfortable.
C. The medical or specialist team, who prescribe or document the suction pressure before it starts.
D. The healthcare assistant who connects the equipment.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The BTS statement says suction pressures should be prescribed or documented by the medical team before suction is commenced.
**Why not A:** Observation does not make suction a nursing decision.
**Why not B:** Comfort does not set suction pressure.
**Why not D:** Support staff do not set clinical pressures.
**Trace:** N-BTS-PCS-2023 — Suction pressures prescribed or documented by the medical team.

**Q2 (scope).** The arterial line on a stable patient has not been used for sampling for a day. Who decides to remove it?
A. The nurse alone, once the line has been unused for a full day.
B. The authorized clinician or trained team under the line-removal protocol; the nurse raises whether the line is still needed.
C. The patient, once he asks for it to come out.
D. The pharmacist, who reviews the daily infusion chart.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** CDC recommends removing arterial catheters when no longer needed (infection prevention). Which trained clinician may authorize or perform removal is determined by local policy; the nurse raises daily necessity and follows the approved removal process.
**Why not A:** Removal needs an order or approved protocol.
**Why not C:** Patient preference is considered but does not replace the clinical decision.
**Why not D:** Line removal is not a pharmacy decision.
**Trace:** S-CDC-BSI-2011 — CDC: remove arterial catheters as soon as no longer required. Who removes the line is governed by local credentialling and protocol, not the CDC infection-prevention guideline.

**Q3 (scope).** A CRRT prescription says one fluid-balance goal, but the patient seems wetter than planned. Who changes the prescription?
A. The prescriber; the nurse reports the discrepancy and the delivered dose or balance.
B. The nurse, who adjusts the goal on the screen to match what the machine is actually delivering.
C. The machine, which adjusts itself automatically to correct any imbalance.
D. The next shift, who will notice any change when they take over.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** KDIGO recommends frequent assessment of delivered dose, and prescription decisions rest with the prescribing team; the nurse reports.
**Why not B:** Changing the prescription is not a nursing decision.
**Why not C:** The machine does not change the prescription.
**Why not D:** Delaying the report delays correction.
**Trace:** S-KDIGO-AKI-2012 — Frequent assessment of delivered dose; prescription by prescriber.

**Q4 (scope).** A nurse wonders whether she can increase a patient's vasoactive infusion to allow him to sit out for physiotherapy. What is the best answer?
A. Yes, because she knows the patient best and the change is small and temporary.
B. Yes, if the physiotherapist agrees, since the two professionals share the responsibility.
C. No, because vasoactive drugs always rule out sitting out, whatever the dose.
D. No; dose changes are for the prescriber or an approved protocol, case by case.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The mobilization consensus found no agreed vasoactive dose threshold and said units decide case by case; dose changes remain a prescriber or protocol decision.
**Why not A:** Size and duration do not remove the need for an order.
**Why not B:** Physiotherapist agreement is not a prescription.
**Why not C:** The consensus says they are not an absolute contraindication.
**Trace:** N-HODGSON-2014 — Vasoactive dose threshold decided by the unit case by case; no consensus.

**Q5 (scope).** Who makes the decision to transfer a critically ill patient to another department or hospital, according to the transfer guidance?
A. The escorting nurse, who knows the equipment and the route best.
B. The receiving ward manager, once a bed becomes available.
C. The appropriate consultants, not the escorting nurse.
D. The transport assistant, who is booked to take the patient at the time requested.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** ICS recommendation 42 says the decision to transfer and to accept a patient should be made by appropriate consultants.
**Why not A:** The decision rests with consultants.
**Why not B:** Bed availability does not make the clinical decision.
**Why not D:** The transport assistant does not make clinical decisions.
**Trace:** N-ICS-TRANSFER-2026 — Rec 42: decision to transfer and accept made by appropriate consultants.

**Q6 (escalation).** A patient on a vasoactive infusion has a cold, pale, painful arm with an arterial line. What is the nurse's role?
A. Remove the arterial line immediately without telling anyone, to restore circulation.
B. Report at once and follow instructions, keeping the arm visible.
C. Flush the line repeatedly until the colour returns to the hand.
D. Wait and document the finding, because circulation often improves within a few hours.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** A cold, pale, painful extremity with an arterial catheter raises concern for impaired limb perfusion and warrants immediate assessment and escalation under the unit's vascular-access protocol. CDC's cited infection-prevention guidance does not itself specify this ischemia response or professional scope.
**Why not A:** Removal without an order is outside the nurse's role and may cause bleeding.
**Why not C:** Repeated flushing can cause harm and does not address the cause.
**Why not D:** Signs of limb ischaemia need immediate reporting.
**Trace:** S-CDC-BSI-2011 — Clinical safety inference (D02-T04): suspected limb ischemia needs immediate escalation and an approved local vascular-access protocol; CDC's catheter guideline is for infection prevention, not an ischemia decision algorithm.
