# Preventing ventilator-associated events and pneumonia in adults
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Not for learners until qualified reviewers approve.

## 1. Overview
Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and the broader surveillance category of ventilator-associated events (VAE) are complications of invasive ventilation. Much prevention is daily nursing practice: positioning, mouth care, sedation and mobility routines, protecting the airway from unplanned loss, and noticing early signs of worsening oxygenation. Ventilator settings, sedation choices and antimicrobials are team or prescriber decisions.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Distinguish VAP (a clinical diagnosis) from VAE (a surveillance definition based on rising ventilator settings).
2. List the essential prevention practices for adults and their nursing components.
3. Recognise practices that are not recommended for VAP prevention.
4. Notice and escalate sustained increases in oxygen or PEEP requirements and signs of infection.
5. Separate nursing actions from prescriber and respiratory-therapy decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Ventilated patients have a tube bypassing upper-airway defences, secretions pooling above the cuff, sedation and immobility. VAE and VAP are associated with longer ventilation and ICU stay. Most of the evidence-based measures sit with the bedside nurse and the daily routine.

## 5. Relevant pathophysiology (brief)
Organisms from the mouth, throat and stomach reach the lower airway around the tube; biofilm forms on the tube; lung defences are impaired by sedation, immobility and critical illness. VAEs are triggered by worsening oxygenation (a sustained rise in PEEP or FiO₂ after a stable period) from many causes: pneumonia, fluid overload, atelectasis, ARDS, and others.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Oxygenation trend:** FiO₂ and PEEP requirement per shift; note upward trends and sustained increases (surveillance rule in the source: PEEP up by 3 cm H₂O or more, or FiO₂ up by 20 points or more, sustained for 2 days after 2 days of stability — used by infection-prevention teams, not a bedside trigger).
- **Respiratory:** secretion volume, colour and consistency; breath sounds; work of breathing; ventilator–patient synchrony; SpO₂.
- **Systemic:** temperature, white cell trend (where ordered), haemodynamics, new confusion.
- **Prevention checklist:** head of bed angle, mouth-care record, sedation depth and readiness to wake or breathe, mobility level, nutrition route, circuit condition, tube position and securement.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Keep the head of the bed raised to 30–45° unless contraindicated (essential practice; low-certainty evidence, retained because it is simple and low-risk).
- Provide mouth care that includes toothbrushing, **without chlorhexidine** (moderate evidence; older protocols used chlorhexidine).
- Use the unit's sedation-minimisation protocol, and take part in daily awakening and breathing readiness checks within the ventilator-liberation protocol (essential; moderate evidence). Avoid benzodiazepines where alternatives exist is a prescriber decision.
- Mobilise early according to protocol (moderate evidence).
- Use early enteral feeding where ordered (high-certainty evidence) and check feeding tolerance.
- Change the ventilator circuit only if visibly soiled or malfunctioning, or per the manufacturer (high-certainty evidence).
- Prevent unplanned extubation: secure the tube, check position, assess agitation, manage restraints or sedation per policy and avoid unnecessary manipulation.
- Hand hygiene: the AARC suctioning guideline states that, in addition to hand hygiene on entering and leaving the patient's area, the clinician should perform hand hygiene immediately before open suctioning (stated in the context of sterile open suctioning, evidence level C). Hand hygiene before other airway or circuit contact, and PPE choice, are general infection-control practice from D06-T04, not graded statements in the sources read here.
- Measure endotracheal or tracheostomy cuff pressure with a manometer at the interval set by unit policy; do not rely on feeling the pilot balloon (AARC 2024; see section 9). Cuff checks are for leak and tracheal-injury safety as well as aspiration; the exact interval and target range come from local policy.
- Report oxygenation changes and signs of infection promptly.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Each shift: FiO₂, PEEP, SpO₂ and trend; secretions; temperature; head-of-bed angle at each check; mouth-care times; sedation score; daily readiness findings; mobility achieved; feed tolerance.

## 9. Important findings – what the 2022 update says (summary table)
| Category | Interventions (adults) |
|---|---|
| Essential practices | High-flow nasal oxygen or non-invasive ventilation where appropriate to avoid intubation (high); minimise sedation – avoid benzodiazepines, use a protocol, ventilator liberation protocol (moderate); maintain physical conditioning (moderate); elevate the head of the bed 30–45° (low); oral care with toothbrushing but without chlorhexidine (moderate); early enteral rather than parenteral nutrition (high); change circuit only if soiled or malfunctioning (high) |
| Additional approaches | Selective oral/digestive decontamination in low-resistance settings (high); subglottic-drainage tubes for expected ventilation over 48–72 h (moderate); consider early tracheostomy; consider post-pyloric feeding in gastric intolerance or high aspiration risk (moderate) |
| Generally not recommended (for VAP prevention) | Oral care with chlorhexidine; probiotics; ultrathin or tapered cuffs; automated cuff-pressure control; frequent cuff-pressure monitoring; silver-coated tubes; kinetic beds; prone positioning; chlorhexidine bathing (the last three may be indicated for other reasons) |
| No impact on VAP rates | Stress-ulcer prophylaxis; monitoring residual gastric volumes; early parenteral nutrition |
| No recommendation | Closed suctioning systems |

**Cuff-pressure conflict – resolved by reading both documents.** SHEA 2022 classes *frequent* and *automated* cuff-pressure monitoring as generally not recommended **for VAP prevention** (moderate evidence). AARC 2024 recommends assessing cuff pressure with a manometer (strong recommendation, high certainty), recommends **against continuous** cuff-pressure assessment as a way to reduce VAP risk (strong, high), and notes that palpation of the pilot balloon is inaccurate compared with a manometer; it also says SHEA's recommendations agree with its own. So the documents are consistent: intermittent manometer measurement is still recommended; continuous or very frequent monitoring is not a VAP-prevention strategy. AARC's narrative notes that an appropriate cuff pressure for limiting micro-aspiration and tracheal injury has been identified as 20–30 cmH₂O (two cited studies; not a graded recommendation), that over-inflation can impede mucosal blood flow, and that consensus on how to measure and how often has not been established; SHEA gives no target. The trials compared every-8-hour checks with less frequent checks. The interval and exact range are therefore local policy (the 20–30 cmH₂O figure is background, not a graded recommendation).

General caution: "not recommended for VAP prevention" does **not** mean the practice is unnecessary for other reasons. Prone positioning and chlorhexidine bathing may be indicated for other purposes; unit policy and the respiratory-therapy team decide.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Rising FiO₂ or PEEP needs; new purulent or increased secretions; fever or hypothermia; new infiltrate reported; falling SpO₂; asynchrony or agitation; tube moved or loose; cuff leak; head of bed repeatedly flat; missed mouth care; sedation deeper than targeted; no daily readiness assessment recorded.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Head-of-bed elevation, toothbrushing and mouth care, tube securement, hand hygiene, circuit handling, mobility and feed-tolerance checks | Independent nursing |
| Sedation reduction, daily awakening and breathing checks, early mobility | Collaborative (per protocol; nurse-driven where the unit protocol allows) |
| Changing ventilator settings, cuff-pressure targets, circuit changes outside policy | Respiratory-therapy / prescriber decision (per unit) |
| Choice of sedative or antimicrobial, stress-ulcer prophylaxis, tracheostomy timing, feeding route changes | Prescriber decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate promptly for: sustained increase in oxygen or PEEP needs; new purulent secretions with fever or instability; accidental extubation or tube displacement; persistent barriers to the bundle (for example, head of bed cannot be elevated); suspected aspiration.
SBAR — **S:** ventilated patient, bed X, FiO₂ up from 0.40 to 0.60 over 6 hours. **B:** day 5 of ventilation, more secretions, temperature 38.4. **A:** worsening oxygenation, possible pneumonia or other cause. **R:** please review now; consider chest imaging and cultures; I will repeat observations every [interval].

## 13. Potential complications
Pneumonia, sepsis, ARDS, prolonged ventilation, tracheal injury from cuff pressure, pressure injury from tube securement, unplanned extubation with aspiration, mouth and skin injury.

## 14. Documentation
Head-of-bed angle checks, mouth-care times and findings, sedation and readiness assessments, mobility, feed tolerance, ventilator settings trend, secretion description, tube position and securement, escalation and response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At the start of the shift: check the head of the bed angle on the bedside indicator, confirm the tube depth and securement, review overnight FiO₂ and PEEP trend, brush the patient's teeth and gums per protocol and suction the mouth with the unit's method, review sedation targets with the team for a daily awakening and breathing readiness check, plan mobilisation, and document each item.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 58-year-old man on day 4 of ventilation has had FiO₂ rising from 0.40 to 0.55 and more secretions. The bed was found flat at 10° after a procedure, and mouth care was last documented 12 hours earlier. The nurse raises the bed, performs mouth care, reports the trend with SBAR, asks about sedation lightening and readiness testing, and documents. The team decides on imaging and further treatment.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (prioritization).** On the morning round a ventilated patient on day 4 has rising oxygen needs, increased secretions, a flat bed after a procedure, and no mouth care documented for 12 hours. What should the nurse do first?
A. Raise the head of the bed to 30–45° unless contraindicated, and mention the oxygen trend and secretions at the next routine handover.
B. Raise the bed, do mouth care, then report the rising FiO₂ and secretions to the team with SBAR.
C. Start a chlorhexidine mouth rinse as an extra measure, then hand over the oxygen trend at the next routine round.
D. Wait for the daily ward round to decide what to change, because ventilator settings and secretions are medical matters.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Both the bundle gaps and the oxygenation change need action now; positioning and mouth care are nursing tasks, and the trend must be escalated promptly.
**Why not A:** Raising the bed is right, but it omits mouth care and leaves the rising oxygen need unreported until the routine handover.
**Why not C:** Chlorhexidine oral care is generally not recommended for VAP prevention in the 2022 update, and reporting later delays assessment.
**Why not D:** Waiting for the round delays assessment of a possible deterioration; bundle tasks are nursing responsibilities.

**Q2 (understanding).** Which statement about VAE and VAP is correct?
A. VAE is a bedside diagnosis made by the nurse when a patient has a fever and increased secretions.
B. VAP and VAE are the same thing, so every VAE means the patient has pneumonia.
C. VAE is a surveillance category triggered only when pneumonia has been confirmed by a positive culture result.
D. VAE is a surveillance definition: it flags sustained worsening oxygenation, whatever the cause.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** VAE is defined by sustained rises in PEEP or FiO₂ after a period of stability and covers pneumonia and non-infectious causes.
**Why not A:** The VAE definition rests on ventilator settings, not on a nurse's assessment of fever and secretions.
**Why not B:** VAE includes non-infectious causes such as fluid overload and atelectasis.
**Why not C:** VAE is triggered by ventilator-setting changes, not by culture confirmation, and it covers non-infectious causes too.

**Q3 (application).** A colleague proposes adding chlorhexidine to routine mouth care "to lower VAP". What is the best response?
A. Say the 2022 update classes chlorhexidine oral care as generally not recommended and advises toothbrushing.
B. Agree, because older protocols used chlorhexidine and nothing has changed since those protocols were written.
C. Suggest stopping mouth care to avoid disturbing the patient and the endotracheal tube.
D. Suggest using chlorhexidine on the night shift when staffing is lower.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The 2022 update moved chlorhexidine oral care to "generally not recommended" and added toothbrushing as essential.
**Why not B:** Recommendations changed between editions.
**Why not C:** Mouth care is part of prevention.
**Why not D:** Dosing by shift is not evidence-based and does not address the recommendation.

**Q4 (scope).** Which task is a prescriber or respiratory-therapy decision rather than an independent nursing task?
A. Brushing the patient's teeth and gums as part of mouth care.
B. Checking and recording the head-of-bed angle at each assessment.
C. Deciding to change a ventilator setting or choose a different sedative.
D. Reporting a sustained rise in oxygen requirement to the team.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Setting changes and drug choice belong to the prescriber or respiratory therapist.
**Why not A:** Mouth care is an independent nursing task.
**Why not B:** Positioning and recording are independent nursing tasks.
**Why not D:** Reporting changes is a nursing responsibility.

**Q5 (judgment).** A ventilator circuit looks clean and is working properly on day 6. A colleague suggests changing it "to prevent infection". What is the best response?
A. Change it, because a fresh circuit always lowers the infection risk.
B. Change it every other day for consistency with the arterial line routine.
C. Ask the respiratory therapist to disconnect it for cleaning at each shift change.
D. Leave it: the 2022 update advises changing circuits only if visibly soiled, malfunctioning or per manufacturer.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Routine circuit changes do not reduce VAP and add disconnections.
**Why not A:** Evidence rates routine changes as unhelpful.
**Why not B:** Routine intervals are not recommended.
**Why not C:** More disconnections can increase risk and secretion exposure.

**Q6 (judgment).** A trainee says the SHEA 2022 update means cuff pressure no longer needs to be checked in ventilated patients. What is the most accurate reply?
A. It covers VAP prevention only; AARC 2024 still recommends manometer checks at the unit's interval, but not continuous monitoring.
B. That is correct: because frequent cuff monitoring does not prevent pneumonia, cuff pressure should never be checked again.
C. The update says cuff pressure must be checked every 15 minutes for every ventilated patient, whatever the indication.
D. The update has no view on cuff pressures, so each unit can simply judge the pressure by feeling the pilot balloon.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The statement concerns VAP prevention only; AARC 2024 recommends manometer assessment, recommends against continuous assessment for VAP prevention, and finds palpation inaccurate.
**Why not B:** This over-generalises a statement limited to VAP prevention and ignores leak and tracheal-injury safety.
**Why not C:** Neither document sets a 15-minute interval.
**Why not D:** Both documents take a position, and palpation of the pilot balloon is inaccurate compared with a manometer.

## 19. Local protocol note
Mouth-care products, positioning angle, sedation and weaning protocols, cuff-pressure policy and circuit-change rules follow the institution's policies and the respiratory team.

## 20. Evidence, applicability and open questions
- Source quality: graded, multi-society US guideline (2022), open access; several statements are moderate or low certainty; surveillance definitions come from the CDC.
- International applicability: practices are generally transferable; availability of subglottic tubes, selective decontamination (depends on local resistance) and staffing vary. Oral-care product choices differ by country.
- Open questions for reviewers (IPC, RT, CCP, NE, BN): local cuff-pressure interval and target range (AARC narrates 20–30 cmH₂O without grading it and states that no consensus exists on frequency); sedation-minimisation roles; whether to teach VAE surveillance definitions at all.
