# Enteral feeding in the ICU – tolerance monitoring and aspiration prevention
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. A dietitian reviewer is needed in addition to the standard roles.

## 1. Overview
Most critically ill adults who cannot eat are fed through a tube into the stomach (or small bowel). Starting feeding early is generally preferred to parenteral nutrition, but feeding must be started, advanced, slowed or stopped by the team according to the patient's condition. The nurse delivers the feed, protects the airway, watches for intolerance and complications, keeps the tube working, and reports promptly. Prescribing the feed, targets and changes is the team's decision.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Explain why early enteral nutrition is usually preferred, and when it may be delayed.
2. Monitor tolerance and recognise intolerance and serious abdominal warning signs.
3. Reduce aspiration risk through positioning and good practice.
4. Respond to a large gastric aspirate and to diarrhoea appropriately within scope.
5. Document and escalate; separate nursing actions from prescriber and dietitian decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Poor nutrition and feeding complications affect recovery, infection risk and ventilation duration. Feeding problems often appear at the bedside first as vomiting, distension, high aspirates or diarrhoea.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Before and during feeding:** tube position confirmed by the unit's method; head-of-bed angle; abdomen (distension, tenderness, bowel sounds are not required for feeding unless ischaemia or obstruction is suspected); vomiting; stool pattern; haemodynamic stability; sedation level; medicines affecting gut movement.
- **Gastric aspirate:** volume and character as per policy (ESICM threshold: more than 500 mL in 6 hours suggests delaying feeding; a single large aspirate should trigger reassessment and prokinetics, not prolonged withholding).
- **Metabolic and fluid status:** glucose and electrolytes as ordered; weight and balance.
- **Aspiration risk:** reduced consciousness, poor swallow, supine position, ventilation, high sedation.
- **Tube:** patency, securement, skin under the tube, flush routine, blockage.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Check tube position and securement before each feed or medicine and per policy.
- Keep the head of the bed raised unless contraindicated (the SHEA update lists 30–45° as an essential VAP prevention practice).
- Run and label feeds and giving sets as the unit requires, with clean technique, and record the volume given.
- Monitor tolerance at the frequency set; report intolerance early.
- Give mouth care; keep skin around the tube healthy.
- Work with the dietitian; report missed feeds and reasons.
- Follow local policy for flushing and medicine administration through feeding tubes.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Tolerance each shift or more often (vomiting, distension, aspirates, stools); volume delivered vs prescribed; head-of-bed angle; glucose; electrolytes; balance; tube position checks; skin at the tube site; signs of aspiration (cough, wheeze, rising oxygen need).

## 9. Important findings – what the sources say (selected, summarised)
| Topic | Statement | Nursing relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Early vs parenteral | ESICM suggests early enteral nutrition rather than early parenteral nutrition (conditional, low quality evidence); SHEA lists early enteral over parenteral as essential for VAP prevention (high) | Support timely starts |
| When to delay | ESICM suggests delaying if shock is uncontrolled, in uncontrolled life-threatening hypoxaemia, hypercapnia or acidosis, bowel ischaemia, obstruction, uncontrolled upper GI bleeding, or very high aspirate; start low-dose feeding once shock is controlled | Report instability; do not start or restart without an order |
| Specific situations | ESICM suggests not delaying solely because of neuromuscular blockers or prone positioning; early feeding is suggested in several groups (for example brain injury, stroke, after GI surgery, ECMO) | Do not stop feeds on your own for these reasons; ask the team |
| Large gastric aspirate | More than 500 mL in 6 hours: suggest delaying; a single large aspirate: prokinetics and reassessment, not prolonged withholding | Report and follow the plan |
| Gastric retention | Use prokinetics and/or post-pyloric feeding in a protocolised way | Prescriber/protocol decision |
| Bowel sounds, diarrhoea | Feeding is suggested regardless of bowel sounds (unless ischaemia or obstruction suspected) and with diarrhoea | Report change; look for causes |
| Aspiration risk | Post-pyloric feeding may be considered for diminished consciousness and inadequate swallowing; SHEA suggests considering post-pyloric feeding for gastric intolerance or high aspiration risk (moderate) | Prescriber decision |
| Gastric residual monitoring | **Different sources differ:** ESICM uses gastric aspirate volume in its recommendations; SHEA 2022 states that *monitoring residual gastric volumes* has no impact on VAP rates | Follow unit policy; reviewers to reconcile |

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Vomiting; new abdominal distension or pain; rigid abdomen; large or bile-stained aspirates; no stool with distension; severe or bloody diarrhoea; sudden cough, desaturation or new infiltrate after feeding (aspiration); hypotension or rising lactate with feeding (possible gut ischaemia); tube displaced or blocked; high glucose or low electrolytes.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Positioning, mouth care, tube checks and securement, running feeds as ordered, measuring aspirates and stools, reporting | Independent nursing |
| Pausing a feed temporarily for a safety concern (vomiting, suspected tube displacement) and informing the team | Independent nursing (per policy) |
| Reinserting or exchanging tubes; bowel regimens; prokinetics; glucose management | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Starting or changing the feed, rate, route, targets, post-pyloric access, parenteral nutrition | Prescriber / dietitian decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for: suspected aspiration; sudden abdominal distension, rigidity or pain; haemodynamic deterioration while feeding; persistent high aspirates or vomiting; tube displacement or blockage not resolved by policy; severe diarrhoea.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, vomited twice and abdomen is distended while on feeds. **B:** day 3, ventilated, vasopressor dose rising, aspirates 400 mL over 4 hours. **A:** feeding intolerance, possible gut hypoperfusion. **R:** I have paused the feed; please review now and advise on feeding, tests and treatment.

## 13. Potential complications
Aspiration pneumonia, vomiting, distension, diarrhoea, constipation, bowel ischaemia (rare), refeeding problems, tube displacement or blockage, nasal or mouth pressure injury, hyperglycaemia and electrolyte disturbance.

## 14. Documentation
Feed type and volume delivered; tube position check method and time; head-of-bed angle; tolerance findings; aspirate volumes; stools; interruptions with reason and times; dietitian communication; escalation and response.

## 15. Practical bedside example
Before starting a feed you check the tube position by the unit's method, confirm the bed head is raised, flush as per policy, run the feed at the ordered rate, record the volume given at the end of the shift, and check the abdomen and aspirates at the frequency set. If the patient vomits you stop the feed, make the airway safe, tell the team, and document.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 66-year-old ventilated man on vasopressors has a distended abdomen, a large aspirate and a rising lactate. The nurse pauses the feed per policy, informs the intensivist at once with SBAR, keeps the head of the bed raised, records the findings, and prepares for tests and orders. The team decides whether to resume, slow or stop feeding.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (prioritization).** A ventilated patient on rising vasopressor doses has a newly distended abdomen, vomited once and has a large gastric aspirate. What is the best nursing action?
A. Continue the feed at the same rate and re-check the abdomen at the next routine round.
B. Pause the feed per policy, protect the airway, keep the bed head raised and report urgently with SBAR.
C. Give a laxative from the ward stock and restart the feed at a lower rate.
D. Flush the tube and increase the feed rate to move the contents along.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** New distension with instability may indicate intolerance or gut hypoperfusion; pausing and escalating protects the patient.
**Why not A:** Continuing risks aspiration and worsening distension.
**Why not C:** Laxatives and restarting need an order and could harm if there is obstruction.
**Why not D:** Increasing the rate would worsen intolerance.

**Q2 (understanding).** What does the ESICM 2017 guideline suggest about early enteral nutrition compared with early parenteral nutrition in critically ill adults?
A. Early parenteral nutrition is preferred for all patients because it is delivered more reliably.
B. Early enteral nutrition and parenteral nutrition are equal and the choice is only for convenience.
C. Feeding should always be delayed until bowel sounds have returned and the first stool has been passed by the patient.
D. Early enteral nutrition is suggested rather than early parenteral nutrition in most patients, with defined delays.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** The guideline suggests early enteral nutrition (conditional, low-quality evidence) with defined delay situations.
**Why not A:** The guideline does not prefer early parenteral nutrition.
**Why not B:** The guideline states a preference rather than equivalence.
**Why not C:** It suggests feeding regardless of bowel sounds unless ischaemia or obstruction is suspected.

**Q3 (judgment).** A patient is turned prone and a colleague says the feed must always be stopped for proning. What is the best response?
A. Agree and stop all feeds for the whole prone period without telling the team, because proning always makes feeding unsafe.
B. Say ESICM suggests not delaying feeding solely because of proning, then ask the team for the plan and follow it.
C. Increase the feed rate during proning to make up for the time lost during the turn and the preparation for it.
D. Remove the feeding tube before every turn to avoid displacement, and re-insert it once the patient is supine again.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The guideline suggests not delaying enteral feeding solely because of prone positioning; the team sets the plan.
**Why not A:** Stopping without the team's plan is not guideline-based.
**Why not C:** Increasing rates is a prescriber decision and may worsen intolerance.
**Why not D:** Removing the tube is unnecessary and unsafe.

**Q4 (application).** Which measure best reduces aspiration risk in a ventilated patient who is being fed?
A. Keeping the head of the bed raised (SHEA: 30–45°, unless contraindicated) and giving regular mouth care.
B. Lying the patient flat during feeds to make the stomach empty into the bowel.
C. Giving large boluses quickly to finish the feed sooner, so that the stomach is empty for the next medicines.
D. Switching off the monitors during the feed so that the patient is not disturbed and can rest more easily.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Head elevation and oral care are essential practices in the VAP update.
**Why not B:** A flat position increases aspiration risk.
**Why not C:** Rapid large volumes increase vomiting risk.
**Why not D:** Monitoring is needed during feeding.

**Q5 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber or dietitian rather than the nurse?
A. Checking and recording tube position and securement.
B. Reporting vomiting and an increase in gastric aspirates.
C. Deciding to change from gastric to post-pyloric feeding or to parenteral nutrition.
D. Raising the head of the bed and giving mouth care.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Route and type of nutrition are team decisions.
**Why not A:** Independent nursing task.
**Why not B:** Reporting is a nursing responsibility.
**Why not D:** Independent nursing tasks.

**Q6 (monitoring).** The unit policy requires gastric aspirate checks, and the SHEA update says monitoring gastric residual volumes has no impact on VAP rates. How should the nurse proceed?
A. Stop checking aspirates at once because one document says they do not reduce VAP, and tell the next shift to do the same.
B. Follow unit policy, because the statements concern VAP rates, and raise the discrepancy with the dietitian or reviewers.
C. Change the policy yourself on your shift to match the SHEA update, since it is the most recent of the two documents.
D. Check aspirates only once a week to compromise between the two documents and reduce the workload for the team.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Policies change through governance; the discrepancy should be flagged, not resolved by individual nurses.
**Why not A:** Local policy and other guidelines still use aspirates.
**Why not C:** Individual nurses do not rewrite policies.
**Why not D:** An arbitrary compromise has no basis in either document.
