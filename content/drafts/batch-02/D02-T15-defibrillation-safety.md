# Defibrillation safety – the nurse's role in the arrest team
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Content is limited to safety and nursing responsibilities; it is not a resuscitation guideline. ERC licence: commercial use requires an agreement.

## 1. Overview
Defibrillation is a time-critical treatment for ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. Safe delivery protects the team from shock and the patient from fire and burns, and keeps interruptions to chest compressions as short as possible. Who may operate a manual defibrillator or an AED, and what is delegated, depends on the country, employer and the individual's competence.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Describe the safety checks before a shock, including oxygen precautions and the "stand clear" check.
2. Explain why the pause in chest compressions before and after a shock must be minimal.
3. Describe, at the level of principle, pad choice and placement and when an alternative position is considered.
4. State what changes for patients with implanted cardiac devices or on a ventilator.
5. Recognise the nurse's role in the team: assessing early, calling, starting compressions, preparing the defibrillator, documenting.
6. Separate nursing actions from physician or team-leader decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
ICU patients are on oxygen, ventilators and monitors, and may have implanted devices. Many arrests happen where the nurse is first on scene and prepares the defibrillator. Fire from sparking in oxygen-enriched air and electric shock to staff are preventable hazards.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Before the shock:** the rhythm is a shockable one as identified by the monitor or defibrillator and the team leader; the patient is unresponsive and without signs of life per the unit's arrest procedure.
- **Safety scan (from ERC):** oxygen delivery devices (masks, cannulae, high-flow, ventilator or bag circuit), implanted devices, people touching the patient or bed, whether compressions are manual or mechanical. *Local checklist items not stated in the ERC text read (for example medication patches, wet surfaces, metal contact, equipment checks) belong to the unit's resuscitation policy.*
- **Pad contact:** the pad is fully in contact with the chest (better contact lowers the risk of arcing); shave the anticipated pad area if necessary to reduce impedance (ERC); pad position; cables connected per the manufacturer and local checks.
- **After the shock:** compressions resumed at once; rhythm and signs of return of circulation reassessed at the next check.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Recognise the arrest, call the team, and start chest compressions without delay in line with unit procedure.
- Apply pads (preferred over paddles when available) as soon as the defibrillator arrives. Pads allow the operator to stand clear and reduce pauses in compressions; if paddles are used apply firm force and good contact to limit arcing.
- Keep the **pre-shock pause** as short as possible: compressions continue while the defibrillator charges, and the whole manual defibrillation process can be done with less than 5 seconds of interruption in a coordinated team. Even a 5–10 second delay reduces the chance of shock success.
- Perform and call the **safety check** so that nobody touches the patient or the bed at the moment of shock; do not defibrillate during manual chest compressions, even while wearing gloves; a shock can be delivered during mechanical compressions with a manual defibrillator.
- **Oxygen safety:** remove masks (including bag-valve masks) and nasal cannulae and place them at least 1 m from the chest; leave the ventilation bag or ventilator circuit connected to the tracheal tube or supraglottic airway, and direct any oxygen exhaust away from the chest. The risk is higher with non-invasive ventilation and high-flow nasal oxygen.
- Resume compressions immediately after the shock.
- Document times, rhythm, shocks and actions as scribe or team member.

**Distinguish preparation for synchronized cardioversion from defibrillation (2025 ALS).** A tachyarrhythmia with a pulse and signs of instability requires an urgent expert assessment. The team selects the appropriate electrical treatment for the rhythm and clinical situation; do not assume the synchronization setting or shock selection is the same for every rhythm. For indicated synchronized cardioversion, trained operators verify synchronization markers and coordinate airway support and any required sedation, recognizing that sedation can worsen circulation. Pulseless shockable arrest follows the arrest pathway. This is a conceptual distinction, not an operating sequence or device-specific instruction.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Rhythm on the monitor; pad and cable integrity; defibrillator status (charged, ready, battery or power supply); quality of compressions (rate, depth, recoil) as measured by the team; end-tidal CO₂ and arterial pressure where monitored, which may indicate return of circulation; pause times.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Oxygen mask or cannula still near the chest; hissing or sparking; poor pad contact; pad directly over an implanted device; staff touching the patient or bed; long pauses; equipment faults (loose cable, defibrillator not charging) – these equipment items follow local checks rather than ERC text.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Recognising arrest, calling help, starting compressions, applying pads, oxygen safety steps, safety check, documentation | Independent nursing (competence and local authority required) |
| Operating an AED or manual defibrillator, charging and delivering shocks | Per local authorisation, competence and team-leader direction; varies by country and employer |
| Deciding to shock, energy level, number and sequence of shocks, drugs, stopping resuscitation | Team leader / physician (prescriber) decision |
| Changing pad position to a vector-change position or double defibrillation | Team-leader decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Call the arrest team immediately for suspected arrest and begin resuscitation according to training. Continue manual compressions during charging, clear all contact with the patient during shock delivery, and resume manual compressions immediately afterward. ERC 2025 distinguishes mechanical compression devices: a manual shock can be delivered without interrupting mechanical chest compression when used within the device and team safety procedure. Synchronized cardioversion for an indicated rhythm with a pulse is a distinct procedure. Escalate equipment faults immediately and obtain a replacement device; report safety incidents after the event.
SBAR for the arrival of the team — **S:** bed X, cardiac arrest, compressions started at [time]. **B:** known [cause if known], on [oxygen/ventilator], pads applied, monitor shows [rhythm]. **A:** shockable rhythm confirmed by the leader. **R:** please take over leadership; I will keep compressions, oxygen safety and the log going.

## 13. Potential complications
Fire and burns from oxygen and sparking; electric shock to staff; skin burns under pads; pad-related device interference; prolonged compression pauses with lower chance of success; pressure injury; psychological distress for staff and relatives.

## 14. Documentation
Time of recognition and call; start of compressions; time of each rhythm check and shock; rhythm; pad position and any reasons for alternatives; oxygen precautions taken; drugs given as recorded by the team; return of circulation or stop time; equipment issues; debrief and incident reports.

## 15. Practical bedside example
An ICU patient on high-flow oxygen arrests. You call for help, start compressions, and a colleague applies the pads. Before the shock you remove the high-flow cannula and place it more than a metre from the chest, leave the ventilator circuit connected if intubated, make sure nobody is touching the patient or the bed, call "stand clear", and resume compressions straight after the shock.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 66-year-old man with an implanted defibrillator arrests with ventricular fibrillation. The nurse starts compressions, applies the pads away from the generator (more than 8 cm) per the team leader's instruction, removes the oxygen mask, and confirms no one is touching the bed before the leader delivers the shock. Compressions resume immediately and the scribe records times.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (safety).** A patient arrests on a non-rebreather mask, and the defibrillator is ready. Which action best reduces the fire risk before the shock?
A. Increase the oxygen flow so that the patient stays well oxygenated while the shock is being delivered to the chest.
B. Remove the mask, keep it at least 1 m from the chest, and leave any ventilator or bag circuit connected.
C. Turn off the monitor to avoid interference and leave the mask where it is, since the patient needs the oxygen.
D. Keep the mask on but loosen the strap so that the oxygen escapes more easily from around the face during the shock.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** For defibrillation during arrest, ERC 2025 advises moving free-flow oxygen masks or cannulae at least 1 m from the chest. A ventilation circuit attached to an advanced airway is different: leave it connected and direct exhaust away from the chest. Coordinate this with the resuscitation team without unnecessary interruption to CPR.
**Why not A:** More oxygen raises the fire risk.
**Why not C:** Leaving the mask in place is hazardous.
**Why not D:** A loosened mask still floods the area with oxygen.

**Q2 (judgment).** The monitor shows a shockable rhythm while compressions are being delivered by hand. The leader asks for a shock. What is the safest approach?
A. Deliver the shock while the compressor continues, because clinical gloves protect the compressor from any electrical effect.
B. Stop compressions for ten seconds to allow the defibrillator to charge fully before the rhythm is checked and the shock given.
C. Ask the compressor to hold the chest only lightly with the fingertips so that the shock risk to them is reduced.
D. Keep compressing while the defibrillator charges, then pause briefly, call a safety check, and shock with no one touching.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Charging during compressions shortens the pre-shock pause; the trained operator visually checks that everyone is clear before delivery. Resume chest compressions immediately after the shock unless there are clinical and physiological signs of return of circulation that justify a brief assessment. Do not rely on gloves for electrical protection.
**Why not A:** ERC states shocks during manual compressions are not safe even with gloves.
**Why not B:** Unnecessary pauses reduce shock success.
**Why not C:** Any contact at the moment of shock is unsafe.

**Q3 (application).** A patient has a visible implanted pacemaker/defibrillator under the left clavicle. What should guide pad placement?
A. Place the pad directly over the device so that the current takes the shortest route to the heart muscle.
B. Avoid using pads on this patient altogether, because defibrillation may damage the implanted device and should be withheld.
C. Place the pad more than 8 cm from the device, or use an alternative position, as the leader directs.
D. Use the usual position regardless of the device, since implanted devices cannot be affected by defibrillator pads.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** ERC advises keeping pads more than 8 cm from an implanted device or using an alternative position. Do not delay an indicated shock solely because the patient has an implanted device; use the trained operator and the local resuscitation algorithm.
**Why not A:** Pads should not be directly over the device.
**Why not B:** Defibrillation should not be withheld because of a device.
**Why not D:** The position should avoid the device.

**Q4 (application).** During preparation for adult manual defibrillation, a nurse finds both adhesive pads and hand-held paddles available. Which explanation best supports the team's equipment choice under its resuscitation protocol?
A. Pads are preferred when available: the operator can stand clear, pauses are shorter and contact is better.
B. Paddles are preferred because they allow the operator to continue compressions at the same time as the shock is delivered.
C. Pads should only be used in children, whereas adults should always be defibrillated with hand-held paddles.
D. The choice makes no difference to safety or efficiency, so the unit can use whichever device is nearest at the time.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Adhesive pads permit a hands-free shock and reduce interruptions and direct contact during shock delivery. The team still needs effective pad contact, the required safety check and the applicable device instructions.
**Why not B:** Defibrillating during manual compressions is not safe.
**Why not C:** The statement applies to adults.
**Why not D:** ERC lists safety and efficiency benefits for pads.

**Q5 (prioritization).** In an arrest, which task is the nurse's highest priority before the defibrillator arrives?
A. Search the previous notes for the likely diagnosis so that the team has the full history before it starts treatment.
B. Take a detailed history from the relatives at the bedside so that the leader has the full picture when the team arrives.
C. Start good-quality compressions, call for help and get ready to apply the pads as soon as the defibrillator arrives.
D. Call the family to the bedside straight away so that they can be present at the start of the resuscitation attempt.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Early compressions and help-calling are the first steps; information gathering follows.
**Why not A:** Not a priority during arrest.
**Why not B:** This delays treatment of the arrest and does not make the shock safer.
**Why not D:** Communication matters but follows life-saving action.

**Q6 (scope).** Which decision must follow the resuscitation algorithm and be made by an authorized, trained operator or designated team leader rather than independently by an untrained team member?
A. Choosing the energy level and whether to give a further shock.
B. Removing an oxygen mask before the shock.
C. Calling "stand clear" and checking that no one is touching the bed.
D. Documenting the time of each shock.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Choosing the shock energy and subsequent strategy requires adherence to the resuscitation algorithm, equipment instructions, and local authorization. Trained nurses may operate a manual defibrillator where permitted; this is not exclusively a physician's task.
**Why not B:** An oxygen safety step the nurse can take.
**Why not C:** A safety action for any team member.
**Why not D:** A nursing documentation task.
