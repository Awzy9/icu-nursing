# Cardiac arrest in the ICU – recognition, response and immediate post-ROSC nursing care
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Not a resuscitation guideline or algorithm. ERC commercial-use restriction applies (see licensing register).

## 1. Overview
Cardiac arrest in a critical-care unit is often preceded by deterioration, and the nurse is frequently the first to notice. The nursing contribution is early recognition, calling the team, starting high-quality compressions, preparing and supporting the defibrillator and airway equipment, protecting the team and patient from harm, documenting accurately, and delivering the first hours of post-return-of-spontaneous-circulation (ROSC) care within the team's plan. Decisions about drugs, airway procedures, defibrillation energy, investigations and prognosis belong to the resuscitation team leader and the treating physicians.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Recognise cardiac arrest and the premonitory signs that should trigger a call for help, including calls based on concern alone.
2. State the main elements of high-quality compressions and how they apply to a patient in a hospital bed.
3. Describe the nurse's tasks in the team and the points where handover and documentation matter.
4. Describe immediate post-ROSC priorities that nurses monitor and report: oxygenation, ventilation, circulation, temperature, seizures, glucose, sedation and neurological assessment.
5. Explain what the guidelines say about prognostication and why nurses must not offer predictions.
6. Separate nursing actions from team-leader and prescriber decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
ICU patients are monitored continuously, so arrest is detected early and the first minutes depend on the bedside team. Good post-ROSC nursing care (stable oxygenation and ventilation, avoiding fever, accurate neurological observation, communication with relatives) affects recovery and the quality of later decisions.

## 4. Basic physiology
During arrest, circulation stops and organs are deprived of oxygen. Chest compressions maintain some blood flow to the brain and heart; pauses interrupt it. After ROSC, the post-cardiac arrest syndrome (brain injury, myocardial dysfunction, systemic ischaemia–reperfusion response) makes the first hours unstable.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Before arrest:** early-warning trends, new arrhythmia, hypotension, hypoxia, falling end-tidal CO₂ in ventilated patients, new chest pain, deteriorating consciousness, and staff concern. ERC advises early-warning systems, empowering all staff to call for help including on clinical concern alone, training in recognising the acutely ill patient, and structured communication tools at handover.
- **Recognition of arrest:** unresponsive person; check breathing while help is called. ERC 2025 BLS: call for help for any unresponsive person without waiting to confirm abnormal breathing; slow, laboured, agonal or panting breathing counts as abnormal; a brief seizure-like episode can occur at onset. In a monitored patient, confirm the rhythm and absence of signs of circulation per unit procedure.
- **During CPR:** compression rate, depth and recoil, interruptions, airway and ventilation, rhythm checks, end-tidal CO₂ and arterial waveform where in place.
- **After ROSC:** airway position, SpO₂ and ventilation, rhythm, blood pressure, perfusion, urine output, temperature, level of consciousness and movements (including myoclonus or seizure activity), pupils, glucose, sedation depth.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
**Recognition and call**
- Call the arrest team and help as soon as arrest is suspected; do not delay for diagnostic certainty.
- Use SBAR on arrival of the team; state events, known cause, treatments and time of onset.
**Safe resuscitation support (BLS and ALS support)**
- Start chest compressions as soon as possible: heel of the hand on the lower half of the sternum, straight arms, shoulders above the chest; depth at least 5 cm but not more than 6 cm; rate 100–120 per minute; full recoil; as few interruptions as possible (ERC BLS).
- In a bed: start CPR on the bed; do not move the patient to the floor (delays compressions and risks injury); if the mattress has a CPR mode that stiffens it, activate it; compress deeper to compensate for a soft mattress. ERC does not advocate a backboard.
- Compressions first, then ventilation, in a 30:2 cycle for obese patients too, without modification (ERC BLS); ventilate with just enough air to make the chest start to rise; avoid excessive ventilation.
- Use defibrillation pads rather than paddles when available and follow the safety steps in D02-T15; do not delay for pad application when compressions can continue.
- Support the team leader: prepare equipment, assist the airway team, ensure waveform capnography is used to confirm tracheal tube placement (ERC ALS), record times.
- Do not give drugs, shocks or airway interventions unless authorised by the leader within your competence and policy.
**After ROSC (nursing monitoring within the team plan)**
- Stay with the patient; continue monitoring and a systematic ABC approach; report changes immediately.
- Oxygen and ventilation: ERC advises 100% (or maximum available) inspired oxygen immediately, then titration to a saturation of 94–98% or a PaO₂ of 10–13 kPa as soon as it can be measured reliably, avoiding both hypoxaemia and hyperoxaemia; the guideline notes pulse oximetry can overestimate saturation in darker skin tones and is unreliable in low-flow states. Normocapnia (PaCO₂ about 4.7–6.0 kPa / 35–45 mmHg) and lung-protective ventilation are targeted; frequent PaCO₂ checks are advised in hypothermia. These targets are set and changed by the team.
- Circulation: arterial-line monitoring is advised for all patients; avoid hypotension and aim for a mean arterial pressure above 60–65 mmHg; fluids, noradrenaline and/or dobutamine are given by prescription. Avoid hypo- and hyperkalaemia (arrhythmia risk). Echocardiography early. (RCUK 2025 expresses the same aim as a systolic pressure above 100 mmHg or a MAP above 60–65 mmHg – alternative measures of adequate perfusion, not a different recommendation; see section 20. The treatment goal is individualised and set by the team.)
- Temperature: actively prevent fever, targeting a temperature of 37.5 °C or below in patients who remain comatose, and keep this up for 36–72 hours; do not actively rewarm mildly hypothermic comatose patients (32–36 °C) to normothermia; use temperature-control devices with a feedback loop on continuous temperature monitoring when a device is used. Temperature management is prescribed; the nurse monitors, documents and reports.
- Seizures: report any convulsion or myoclonus; EEG is used by the team to confirm seizures; first-line anti-seizure drug choices (levetiracetam or valproate) and the advice **not** to give seizure prophylaxis are prescriber matters.
- Glucose: use the unit's standard glucose management protocol (ERC).
- Sedation and neurological assessment: ERC advises short-acting sedatives to allow neurological assessment and daily neurological examination; clinical examination is easily confounded by sedatives, opioids or muscle relaxants – record doses and timing.
- Prognosis: ERC advises multimodal prognostication (clinical examination, electrophysiology, biomarkers, imaging), with no single predictor 100% accurate. Nurses record observations and support families but must not offer predictions at the bedside.
- Families: provide honest, consistent information per the team's plan; ERC documents notable emotional burden for survivors and relatives (anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress) and recommends functional assessment before discharge and follow-up.
- Rehabilitation: ERC notes early mobilisation, delirium management and ICU diaries are recommended for post-intensive care syndrome in general; no cardiac-arrest-specific ICU rehabilitation trials were found.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
During CPR: compression quality, interruptions, rhythm checks, ETCO₂, arterial waveform if present, time stamps. After ROSC: continuous ECG, arterial pressure and MAP, SpO₂ and blood gases as ordered, ETCO₂, temperature, urine output, lactate and electrolytes as ordered, neurological examination, sedation level, seizure activity, glucose, ventilator values.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Before arrest: deteriorating early-warning score, new arrhythmia, hypotension, hypoxia, falling ETCO₂, chest pain, new confusion. During CPR: poor compression quality, long pauses, tube displacement, loss of capnography trace, equipment failure. After ROSC: re-arrest, hypotension, rhythm change, desaturation, fever, myoclonus or seizure, rising lactate, falling urine output, hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia, agitation or bucking on the ventilator.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Recognising arrest, calling help, starting compressions, bed management, equipment readiness, monitoring, documentation, family support | Independent nursing (competence required) |
| Applying pads and operating AED/manual defibrillator, supporting airway, giving drugs at the leader's request, titrating oxygen or sedation per protocol | Collaborative (per order, protocol and local authorisation) |
| Choosing drugs, doses, energy, intubation and sedation drugs, arrhythmia treatment, coronary angiography, temperature targets, anti-seizure drugs, stopping resuscitation, prognostication | Team leader / prescriber decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Call immediately for suspected arrest. After ROSC escalate any re-arrest, hypotension below the ordered MAP, new arrhythmia, desaturation, rising temperature, seizure or myoclonus, change in pupils or consciousness, unexpected sedation or equipment problems.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, cardiac arrest at [time], ROSC at [time]. **B:** [cause if known], rhythm, shocks and drugs as recorded, airway [type], current oxygen and ventilator settings. **A:** post-arrest patient, comatose, MAP [value], temperature [value]. **R:** please review the haemodynamic and temperature targets; I will continue observations every [interval] and report any seizure or change.

## 13. Potential complications
Re-arrest, rib or sternal injury, aspiration, hypoxic brain injury, myocardial dysfunction, arrhythmia, seizure, fever, pressure injury, ventilator-associated pneumonia, psychological distress in patients, relatives and staff.

## 14. Documentation
Time of recognition, call and first compression; team arrival; rhythm checks, shocks and drugs as recorded by the scribe; airway and capnography confirmation; time of ROSC; post-ROSC observations including MAP, temperature, neurological findings and sedation; seizures; family communication; equipment problems; incident report where indicated; staff debrief.

## 15. Practical bedside example
You find a patient unresponsive on the unit. You call for help and the arrest team, start compressions on the bed with the CPR mode activated, send for the defibrillator, ensure oxygen devices are removed from the chest before a shock, and hand over to the team leader with SBAR. After ROSC you stay, keep the airway secure, check SpO₂, ETCO₂ and blood pressure continuously, record the temperature and sedation, and report any change.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 63-year-old man on the unit after a myocardial infarction develops ventricular fibrillation. The nurse starts compressions, the team defibrillates, and circulation returns. The patient remains comatose. The nurse monitors the arterial line (MAP maintained above the ordered target by the team), records a temperature of 38.1 °C and informs the doctor, who starts temperature control. A relative asks "Will he wake up?" and the nurse explains that the team will assess him over the coming days and arranges for the doctor to speak to the family.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (prioritization).** A nurse finds a monitored ICU patient unresponsive, with abnormal breathing. What is the best first action?
A. Call for help and the arrest team at once, then start compressions on the bed without waiting for equipment.
B. Move the patient to the floor first so that the compressions will be effective, then call for the arrest team.
C. Check the chart for the diagnosis and for any limitation-of-treatment decision before taking any action.
D. Prepare and give the first drug before starting compressions, to save time while the team is assembling.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** Early call and compressions come first; ERC advises starting CPR on the bed.
**Why not B:** Moving the patient delays compressions and risks injury.
**Why not C:** Where a documented decision exists, it should be known in advance; delay in the arrest itself is harmful.
**Why not D:** Drug administration belongs to the team and follows compressions.

**Q2 (application).** Which description of compressions on an adult in a bed matches ERC 2025 BLS?
A. Compress 3–4 cm at a rate of about 80 per minute, leaning on the chest between compressions to keep the hands in place.
B. At least 5 cm but not more than 6 cm, 100–120 per minute, full recoil, deeper to compensate for a soft mattress.
C. Compress as hard as possible and as fast as possible, ignoring recoil, so that the blood flow is maximal.
D. Compress only once per second after the airway has been secured, since compressions before that are ineffective.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Matches the BLS text, including bed use and compensation.
**Why not A:** Too shallow, too slow, and leaning prevents recoil.
**Why not C:** Excessive speed and no recoil reduce effectiveness.
**Why not D:** Compressions should not wait for the airway.

**Q3 (judgment).** A nurse is worried about a patient whose early-warning score is only moderate, but who looks unwell and is becoming confused. What does ERC advise?
A. Wait until the early-warning score reaches the trigger value before calling anyone, so that the call is justified.
B. Reassess in four hours, since only the numerical score is a reliable trigger for calling the response team.
C. Ask a colleague whether they agree before deciding whether to call for help, to avoid an unnecessary call.
D. Call for help on the basis of clinical concern using structured communication, without waiting for a trigger.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** ERC advises empowering all staff to call based on clinical concern rather than solely on vital signs.
**Why not A:** Waiting for the score can delay help.
**Why not B:** A four-hour delay risks deterioration.
**Why not C:** Staff should be able to call without requiring agreement.

**Q4 (monitoring).** A comatose patient has just regained circulation after a cardiac arrest. Which finding should the nurse report to the team first?
A. An SpO₂ of 97% on the ordered oxygen, with a good pleth trace and no change in the last hour.
B. A MAP of 52 mmHg falling over ten minutes, when the team has ordered a target above 60–65 mmHg.
C. A temperature of 36.8 °C measured with the bladder probe, unchanged from the previous hour.
D. A capillary glucose that is within the range set by the unit protocol and has been stable since ROSC.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Hypotension after arrest should be avoided; ERC targets a MAP above 60–65 mmHg.
**Why not A:** 97% lies within the advised range of 94–98% with a reliable trace.
**Why not C:** Fever avoidance targets 37.5 °C or less; 36.8 °C is acceptable.
**Why not D:** A glucose within the protocol range, stable since ROSC, needs routine monitoring only.

**Q5 (scope).** Which task belongs to the team leader or prescriber rather than the nurse?
A. Recording the time of each rhythm check and shock on the resuscitation record during the attempt.
B. Deciding on neuroprognostication and discussing the likely outlook with the family at the bedside.
C. Activating the CPR mode on the mattress before starting compressions on a patient in the bed.
D. Reporting a rise in the temperature of a comatose post-arrest patient to the team promptly.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Prognostication is multimodal and a medical team decision; no single predictor is fully accurate.
**Why not A:** A documentation task for the nurse or scribe.
**Why not C:** A nursing action in CPR on a bed.
**Why not D:** A nursing monitoring task.

**Q6 (safety).** Why should sedative and opioid doses be recorded carefully in a comatose post-arrest patient?
A. Because the doses and times are only needed so that the pharmacy can reconcile its stock after the event.
B. Because these drugs have no influence on the clinical neurological examination in the first days after arrest.
C. Because recording them is optional once the patient has been transferred from the resuscitation area to the unit.
D. Sedatives, opioids and muscle relaxants can confound the neurological examination used in assessment.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** ERC notes clinical examination is prone to interference from these drugs, so timing and doses matter.
**Why not A:** Stock reconciliation is not the clinical reason for recording doses and times.
**Why not B:** ERC notes these drugs do interfere with the clinical examination.
**Why not C:** The doses matter for interpretation for as long as the patient is sedated, wherever they are cared for.

## 20. Evidence, applicability and open questions
- Source quality: three 2025 European guidelines (ERC; post-resuscitation care jointly with ESICM) built on ILCOR evidence reviews, read in full. Many statements are narrative; certainty is often low.
- Jurisdiction: European guidance. AHA 2025 could not be read; algorithms, drug and energy details, targets and nursing authority differ by country. Blood pressure expression: the ERC–ESICM 2025 full text recommends avoiding hypotension with a MAP above 60–65 mmHg; Resuscitation Council UK 2025 (key points page, which publishes the ERC–ESICM guideline for UK practice) states the aim as a systolic pressure above 100 mmHg **or** a MAP above 60–65 mmHg. These are alternative ways of expressing adequate perfusion pressure – systolic pressure is what is often seen on a cuff or monitor, MAP is the ERC-preferred measure on an arterial line – not conflicting recommendations. Individual targets (for example higher targets in chronic hypertension or lower in other situations) are set by the treating team; the nurse reports values against the ordered target.
- Population-specific: adults only; paediatric and neonatal arrest, pregnancy and traumatic arrest are not covered.
- Not covered: drug doses, energy levels, tracheal intubation technique, ECPR, termination of resuscitation, coronary angiography, targeted temperature details, detailed prognostication thresholds.
- Open questions for reviewers (CCP, NE, BN, resuscitation lead, NEURO): how the platform expresses the blood-pressure aim (MAP, SBP, or both) and how individualised targets are shown; fever-prevention duration; nurse authority for defibrillation and drugs; ICU-specific family communication; local DNACPR processes; how much prognostication content belongs in a nursing topic.
