# Artificial airway suctioning – safe practice for the ICU nurse
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Evidence is mostly level B/C expert-panel rating; adult frequency evidence is absent.

## 1. Overview
Suctioning removes secretions from an endotracheal or tracheostomy tube. It is a core nursing and respiratory-therapy skill, but it can cause low oxygen levels, changes in heart rate and blood pressure, arrhythmias, bleeding, airway injury, infection and (in some patients) a rise in intracranial pressure. The AARC 2022 guideline advises suctioning when indicated, preparing the patient, keeping the procedure brief, and avoiding routine saline.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Recognise the indications for suctioning rather than suctioning by the clock.
2. Prepare the patient and equipment to reduce complications.
3. Carry out and document the procedure within the unit policy.
4. Recognise complications and stop and escalate when needed.
5. Know what the guideline does not settle: adult frequency evidence, catheter size ratio, suction pressure details.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Retained secretions block gas exchange, but unnecessary or poorly performed suctioning causes harm. Good practice combines accurate assessment of need with a brief, safe technique.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Indications (AARC 2022, adults and children):** audible or palpable secretions (breath sounds), visible secretions in the artificial airway, and a "sawtooth" pattern on the ventilator flow waveform. In neonates an acute rise in airway resistance may be an indicator.
- **Other clues:** rising peak airway pressure with falling delivered volume, falling SpO₂, increased work of breathing, coughing, agitation. Always consider other causes (tube kink, bite, bronchospasm, pneumothorax) before suctioning.
- **Before suctioning:** haemodynamic stability, oxygenation, sedation or analgesia, intracranial pressure risk (neurological patients), bleeding tendency, secretion thickness, humidification adequacy.
- **After suctioning:** SpO₂, heart rate and rhythm, blood pressure, breath sounds, waveform, secretion colour and amount, patient comfort.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Suction when indicated, not on a fixed schedule unless unit policy requires a rule. The guideline's as-needed recommendation is graded for neonatal and paediatric patients; **no adult study of suctioning frequency was found**, so the adult as-needed approach rests on the earlier AARC guideline and committee judgment.
- Explain to the patient, provide analgesia or sedation per orders when needed, and position appropriately.
- **Preoxygenate** adult and paediatric patients before suctioning (evidence level B, committee unanimous). The text notes that an FiO₂ about 0.2 above baseline may be enough instead of 1.0; the amount is set by unit policy or the clinician.
- Perform hand hygiene on entering and leaving the patient's area and immediately before open suctioning (AARC text, accompanying the sterile-technique statement).
- Use either a **closed or open** system in adults (both can be used safely and effectively). For open suctioning use sterile technique (evidence level C); closed systems are described in SHEA 2022 as having no recommendation for VAP prevention.
- Do **not** instil normal saline routinely (evidence level B): it may worsen oxygenation and cause coughing, bronchospasm, rises in heart rate and ICP and may dislodge biofilm. Thick secretions are best raised with the team (the guideline sections read do not cover humidification or hydration).
- Keep each suctioning event as **brief as possible, no longer than 15 seconds** (evidence level C).
- Use the **shallow** technique routinely; use deep suctioning only when shallow suctioning is ineffective, aware of airway-trauma risk (evidence level B).
- Use the **lowest effective suction pressure**; the document gives an upper limit of −200 mm Hg for adults (evidence level C) and −120 mm Hg for neonates and children.
- Stop the procedure and escalate if desaturation, arrhythmia, haemodynamic change or distress occurs.
- Routine bronchoscopy for secretion removal is not recommended (a medical/respiratory therapy decision); devices to clear a blocked tube are supported when resistance rises due to secretions.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
During and after suctioning: SpO₂ and heart rate continuously; blood pressure as indicated; ECG rhythm; ICP and CPP where monitored; ventilator pressures and waveform; secretion characteristics; patient comfort and cough; number and duration of passes.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Desaturation not recovering within a short time; bradycardia, tachycardia or arrhythmia; hypotension; new bleeding in secretions; bronchospasm; rising ICP in a neurological patient; inability to pass the catheter (blocked or kinked tube); sudden rise in airway pressures; copious blood-stained or purulent secretions with fever; loss of the airway.

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for: persistent desaturation, instability or arrhythmia; bleeding; suspected tube obstruction or displacement; bronchospasm; ICP rises; secretions that cannot be cleared.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, SpO₂ fell to 82% during suctioning and is slow to recover. **B:** day 4 ventilation, thick secretions, preoxygenated, two passes. **A:** possible obstruction or plug; unstable. **R:** please come now; I have stopped suctioning, increased oxygen per protocol and am bagging/assisting per policy.

## 13. Potential complications
Hypoxaemia, arrhythmias, bradycardia, hypotension or hypertension, raised ICP, bleeding and mucosal injury, bronchospasm, atelectasis, infection, pain and distress, accidental extubation or disconnection.

## 14. Documentation
Indication; pre-assessment (SpO₂, rate, rhythm, pressures); preoxygenation; system used; number of passes and duration; secretions (amount, colour, consistency); patient response; complications and actions; escalation.

## 15. Practical bedside example
You hear gurgling and see a sawtooth waveform. You check the patient and tube, confirm analgesia and sedation, preoxygenate per policy, wash hands and put on gloves, use the closed system or a sterile open technique, suction with the shallow method for no more than 15 seconds, reoxygenate, reassess breath sounds, SpO₂ and haemodynamics, and document.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 59-year-old ventilated man with brain injury is suctioned for thick secretions; his ICP rises and SpO₂ falls. The nurse stops, restores oxygenation, reports the ICP change, avoids saline, optimises sedation and humidification as ordered, and documents. The team reviews the plan before the next suction.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (prioritization).** During suctioning of a ventilated patient the SpO₂ falls from 96% to 84%, the heart rate drops and the patient does not settle. What should the nurse do first?
A. Take a second pass quickly to clear the airway and fix the cause of the fall, then check the monitor again afterwards.
B. Instil saline to loosen secretions and continue suction on the same pass, because the secretions are probably the cause.
C. Wait a minute to see whether the readings improve by themselves before changing anything or calling anyone for help.
D. Stop suctioning, restore oxygenation per protocol, assess the patient and call for help if recovery is slow.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Stopping and restoring oxygenation is the priority; escalation follows if unstable.
**Why not A:** More suction would worsen hypoxaemia.
**Why not B:** Saline is not recommended and can worsen oxygenation.
**Why not C:** Waiting prolongs hypoxaemia.

**Q2 (application).** Which finding best indicates a need for suctioning in an intubated adult?
A. The clock shows it is two hours since the last suction.
B. Audible secretions in the airway and a sawtooth flow waveform on the ventilator.
C. The patient is awake and tolerating the tube without coughing.
D. The family asks that the tube be cleaned.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** AARC lists breath sounds, visible secretions and sawtooth waveform as indicators.
**Why not A:** The guideline does not recommend scheduled suctioning for adults on the basis of time alone.
**Why not C:** Absence of cough does not signal need.
**Why not D:** A request is not a clinical indication.

**Q3 (judgment).** A colleague wants to instil saline before every pass "to loosen secretions". What is the best response?
A. Agree, since saline thins secretions and improves clearance, so it should be used before every pass in every patient.
B. Say it is long-standing routine practice on the unit and not something that a nurse should question or discuss.
C. Say AARC advises generally avoiding routine saline because it may worsen oxygenation, and ask the team about alternatives.
D. Suggest using larger volumes so that the effect lasts longer.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** Seven of eight studies suggested a negative effect on oxygenation; the guideline advises generally avoiding routine saline, and alternatives are for the team to decide.
**Why not A:** The theoretical benefit is not supported.
**Why not B:** Practice should follow evidence and policy, and can be questioned.
**Why not D:** Larger volumes increase the risk of harm.

**Q4 (understanding).** Which statement about suction duration and depth is consistent with AARC 2022?
A. Suctioning should be deep every time, to make sure that all the secretions are removed from the lower airway.
B. Each event should be as brief as possible, at most 15 seconds, and shallow unless that is ineffective.
C. Suctioning for 30 seconds is better than a shorter event because it removes more secretions in one pass.
D. Depth makes no difference to airway trauma, so the catheter can be advanced as far as it will go.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The guideline recommends brevity (15 s maximum) and routine shallow suctioning.
**Why not A:** Deep suctioning increases trauma risk and is for ineffective shallow suction.
**Why not C:** Longer events increase hypoxaemia risk.
**Why not D:** The guideline links deep suctioning with trauma.

**Q5 (safety).** A nurse is asked what catheter-to-tube ratio to use. What is the most accurate response?
A. The guideline gives one clear number for the catheter-to-tube ratio that has been proven optimal in adults.
B. The nurse should choose the thickest catheter that will fit in the tube, to clear the largest possible volume of secretions.
C. The AARC text gives conflicting figures and says the optimum is not known, so follow unit policy and ask the respiratory team.
D. Catheter size does not affect oxygenation or airway pressure, so any size on the shelf is equally acceptable.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The abstract, Table 1 and text differ, and the text states the optimum is not known.
**Why not A:** The optimum is not established.
**Why not B:** A catheter that is too large can occlude the tube and cause negative pressure effects.
**Why not D:** Larger catheters can cause physiologic changes.

**Q6 (scope).** Which task belongs to the respiratory therapist or prescriber rather than the nurse?
A. Preoxygenating the patient before a pass in line with the unit protocol.
B. Deciding to perform bronchoscopy for secretion removal.
C. Stopping suctioning when the patient desaturates.
D. Recording the number and duration of passes.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** Bronchoscopy is a medical or specialist procedure and routine use is not recommended.
**Why not A:** Nursing task per protocol.
**Why not C:** The nurse should stop an unsafe procedure.
**Why not D:** Documentation is a nursing responsibility.

## 20. Evidence, applicability and open questions
- Source quality: the AARC 2022 guideline uses a modified RAND/UCLA appropriateness method; evidence is level B or C; the authors state the evidence is of low quality overall. Several statements come from neonatal and paediatric studies or from the earlier 2010 guideline.
- Internal inconsistencies: catheter-size ratio and the appropriateness score for the 15-second limit differ between sections.
- Applicability: US respiratory-therapy guidance; the nurse's role in suctioning varies by country and unit.
- Not covered: tracheostomy emergencies, cough assist, in-line saline use in special cases, paediatric and neonatal details.
- Open questions for reviewers (RT, CCP, NEURO, NE, BN): catheter ratio and pressure standards; preoxygenation level; use of closed systems; suctioning in raised ICP; competency and role boundaries.
