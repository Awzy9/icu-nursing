# Delirium – nursing screening, prevention and response in the ICU
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. ICU-specific guideline evidence (PADIS) was not accessible; this draft rests on NICE general-hospital guidance.

## 1. Overview
Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness, often with changes in thinking, perception, sleep and behaviour. It can be hyperactive (agitated), hypoactive (quiet, withdrawn) or mixed, and the hypoactive form is often missed. In ICU it is common and linked to worse outcomes. Nurses are best placed to notice change, screen with a validated tool, prevent delirium with simple measures, and escalate. Making the final diagnosis and prescribing belong to appropriately competent clinicians.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. List risk factors that identify patients at risk.
2. Recognise hyperactive and hypoactive presentations.
3. Name the NICE-recommended ICU screening tools and when to screen.
4. Apply the multicomponent prevention measures.
5. Respond to a positive screen: look for causes, de-escalate, escalate, document.
6. Separate nursing actions from diagnosis and drug decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Delirium prolongs stay, raises complications (falls, device removal, aspiration) and distress for patients and families, and is often caused by something treatable (pain, infection, hypoxia, drugs). Nurses spend the most time at the bedside and often see the first change from the patient's usual state.

## 5. Relevant pathophysiology (brief)
Delirium is multifactorial: acute illness, sepsis, hypoxia, metabolic disturbance, drugs, pain, sleep loss, immobility, dehydration and constipation interact with vulnerability such as age and prior cognitive impairment.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Risk (at admission and when conditions change; NICE):** age 65 or over; current or past cognitive impairment or dementia; current hip fracture; severe illness (deteriorating or at risk of deterioration).
- **Look for recent change (within hours or days) in:** cognition (poor concentration, slow responses, confusion), perception (hallucinations), physical function (restlessness, agitation, reduced movement, appetite or sleep change), and social behaviour (withdrawal, difficulty engaging). Family or carers can report these.
- **Be especially vigilant for hypoactive delirium:** withdrawal, slow responses, reduced movement, poor concentration, reduced appetite.
- **ICU screening tool:** NICE advises the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) or the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC) in critical care, instead of the 4AT; screen when indicators appear and at least daily observation for change (unit policy usually sets screening frequency).
- **Contributing factors:** pain (including non-verbal signs when the patient is ventilated), hypoxia, infection, dehydration, constipation, urinary retention or catheter, medicines, withdrawal (alcohol, nicotine, drugs), sensory impairment, sleep loss.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Screen with the unit's validated tool as policy requires; document the result and the reason for any missed screen.
- Do not attribute new confusion to "just the ICU": look for causes and report.
- Deliver the prevention measures (below) as a team.
- Reorient, reassure and involve family; keep communication simple and kind.
- Use verbal and non-verbal de-escalation first if the patient is distressed.
- Protect safety: lines, tubes, falls, restraints policy; use the least restrictive measures and follow policy.
- Escalate positive screens and any marked agitation promptly.
- Provide information to patients and families: delirium is common and usually temporary (NICE).

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Delirium screen results and trend; sedation and analgesia scores; pain; oxygen saturation; haemodynamics; temperature; hydration, bowel and bladder function; sleep; mobility; sensory aids in use; medicine changes; family presence.

## 9. Important findings – NICE CG103 prevention bundle (summarised)
| Domain | What the nurse does |
|---|---|
| Familiar team, few moves | Keep care continuity; avoid unnecessary room or bed moves |
| Orientation | Good lighting, clear signage; a clock (24-hour clock suggested in critical care) and calendar; explain where they are, who you are, what your role is; stimulating activities; regular visits |
| Hydration and bowels | Adequate fluids (consider IV/subcutaneous if needed, with care in heart or kidney disease); watch constipation |
| Oxygen | Assess hypoxia and optimise saturation; remember some pulse oximeters misread, especially in darker skin |
| Infection | Look for and treat infection (prescriber); avoid unnecessary catheterisation; infection control |
| Mobility | Mobilise early; range-of-motion exercises for those who cannot walk |
| Pain | Assess pain, including non-verbal signs in ventilated patients; start and review treatment (prescriber) |
| Medicines | Medication review in multiple medicines (prescriber and pharmacist) |
| Nutrition | Nutrition support; dentures fitting |
| Senses | Hearing and vision aids available and working; resolve reversible causes such as ear wax |
| Sleep | Avoid procedures and rounds during sleep periods; reduce noise |
Treatment notes: manage the underlying cause; reassure and reorient; de-escalate first; short-term haloperidol may be considered by the prescriber when distressed or risk to self or others and de-escalation has failed, taking account of risks (NICE; prescriber decision).

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Sudden confusion, drowsiness or agitation; fluctuating attention; hallucinations; pulling at lines or tubes; refusing care; new withdrawal or quietness; sleep reversal; new fever or unwell state; a drug change; hypoxia; pain behaviours; urinary retention or constipation; alcohol or drug withdrawal signs.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Screening with the unit tool; reorientation; sleep, light, noise, sensory aids, mobility, family involvement; de-escalation | Independent nursing |
| Pain assessment and non-drug comfort measures | Independent nursing |
| Mobilisation, oxygen titration and fluid measures per protocol | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Diagnosis of delirium, finding causes, medication review, sedative or antipsychotic use, antimicrobials | Prescriber decision (nurse reports and administers) |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate promptly for: a positive screen; agitation endangering lines, tubes or staff; sudden change with possible hypoxia, sepsis or stroke; suspected withdrawal; refusal of life-sustaining care.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, newly confused and pulling at his lines. **B:** day 4, post-laparotomy, positive delirium screen this morning, last analgesia [time], no sleep overnight. **A:** probable delirium; pain, infection and hypoxia not excluded. **R:** please review for causes and medicines; I am using reorientation and de-escalation and will repeat the screen.

## 13. Potential complications
Self-removal of tubes and lines, falls, aspiration, pressure injury, prolonged ventilation or stay, distress and later cognitive problems; harm from restraints or from sedating drugs.

## 14. Documentation
Screen tool, result, time; behaviours observed; contributing factors found; interventions and response; escalation and orders; family information given; restraint or safety measures with reasons.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At 06:00 a patient who was talking yesterday is quiet, not following requests and not eating. You screen with the unit tool (positive), check pain with a behavioural tool, oxygen saturation, temperature and bladder, move the clock and calendar into view, put hearing aids and glasses back on, introduce yourself and explain the situation calmly, then report with SBAR.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 71-year-old man, day 3 after bowel surgery, is restless, tries to pull out his central line and says he is "at home". His family says this is new. Screening is positive; pain is likely, urine retention is found, and sleep has been interrupted. The nurse reorients him with his daughter present, treats the cause under orders (analgesia, catheter), cluster-care for sleep, and documents. Medication review and drug decisions stay with the prescriber and pharmacist.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A usually talkative patient is quiet, slow to answer, not eating and doesn't follow requests this morning. The family say it began overnight. What is the best nursing action?
A. Record that the patient is "resting well" because he is not agitated and is not disturbing the other patients or staff.
B. Wait to see whether the patient is back to normal after lunch before mentioning it to anyone on the team.
C. Ask the family to stop visiting for the day so that he can sleep and recover without further stimulation.
D. Screen with the unit delirium tool, look for causes (pain, hypoxia, infection, retention) and report the findings.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Hypoactive delirium is often missed; recent change reported by family should prompt screening and a search for causes.
**Why not A:** Quietness can be a sign of hypoactive delirium, not rest.
**Why not B:** Waiting delays recognition of a possibly treatable cause.
**Why not C:** Family presence is a recommended prevention measure.

**Q2 (application).** Which tools does NICE CG103 recommend for assessing possible delirium in a critical care unit?
A. The 4AT tool alone, because it is quick, validated and used in all other hospital areas.
B. CAM-ICU or the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist.
C. A clinical impression, because validated tools are not helpful in ventilated patients.
D. A mini-mental state score done once at admission.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** NICE advises CAM-ICU or ICDSC instead of the 4AT in critical care.
**Why not A:** The 4AT is advised outside critical care or the recovery room.
**Why not C:** Validated ICU tools exist and are recommended.
**Why not D:** A single cognitive score at admission does not detect fluctuating change.

**Q3 (prioritization).** A patient with a positive delirium screen is pulling at a central line and an arterial line. Which action should come first?
A. Make the lines safe, use calm de-escalation, call for help and report so that causes are reviewed.
B. Apply wrist restraints at once to prevent line removal, then decide on the next steps with the team.
C. Give a sedative from the ward stock to settle the patient first, then inform the medical team afterwards.
D. Leave the patient alone so that he can calm down by himself, and check on him again in an hour.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** NICE advises de-escalation first; safety of devices is protected while the team seeks causes and decides on any drugs or restraint under policy.
**Why not B:** Restraints are a last resort under policy and can worsen distress.
**Why not C:** Sedation needs a prescriber's order, and causes should be sought first.
**Why not D:** Leaving a patient who is pulling lines unattended is unsafe.

**Q4 (scope).** Which task belongs to the prescriber or pharmacist rather than the nurse?
A. Turning the lights down and reducing noise during sleep periods.
B. Reorienting the patient and explaining who the nurse is.
C. Putting the patient's glasses and hearing aids back in place.
D. Reviewing and changing the patient's medicines to reduce delirium risk.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Medication review and changes are prescriber and pharmacist decisions.
**Why not A:** Independent nursing measure.
**Why not B:** Independent nursing measure.
**Why not C:** Independent nursing measure.

**Q5 (understanding).** Which statement about hypoactive delirium is correct?
A. It is harmless because the patient is calm and quiet, so no further assessment or action is needed from staff.
B. It is often missed, so look for withdrawal, slow responses, reduced movement, poor concentration and reduced appetite.
C. It only affects people who already have dementia, so it can be ruled out in anyone without that diagnosis.
D. It always needs sedation to prevent harm, because untreated delirium becomes dangerous within a few hours.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** NICE advises particular vigilance because hypoactive delirium is often missed.
**Why not A:** It carries the same risks of poor outcomes.
**Why not C:** Delirium can occur in anyone who is severely ill.
**Why not D:** Sedation is not an automatic response; causes are managed first.

**Q6 (monitoring).** A ventilated patient is agitated and the nurse suspects pain. What is the best step?
A. Assess non-verbal pain signs with the unit tool, use the comfort measures in policy, and report so analgesia is reviewed.
B. Assume the agitation is delirium and ask for an antipsychotic straight away, without any assessment of pain first, to save time.
C. Increase the sedation yourself to reduce the agitation quickly, since the patient cannot report pain in words.
D. Wait until the patient can tell you whether he has pain, because behaviour alone is not a reliable guide.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** NICE advises assessing non-verbal signs of pain, including in ventilated patients, and treating it.
**Why not B:** Untreated pain is a common trigger and must be assessed first.
**Why not C:** Sedation changes need an order or protocol.
**Why not D:** A ventilated patient may be unable to speak; waiting leaves pain untreated.
