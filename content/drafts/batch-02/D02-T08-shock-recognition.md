# Recognising circulatory shock and the nurse's response
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. ESICM 2025 shock guidance is partially checked (abstract and comparison table only); not for clinical use.

## 1. Overview
Circulatory shock is life-threatening acute circulatory failure in which cells do not get enough oxygen. Blood pressure may be low, but low pressure is not required: signs of poor perfusion in skin, kidneys and brain are the key clues, and compensation can hide the problem until late. The nurse recognises early signs, repeats observations, checks for a likely cause, and escalates quickly. Choosing fluids, vasopressors and diagnostic tests is a prescriber decision.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. Define shock in plain terms and list the four patterns (hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, obstructive, distributive).
2. Describe how to check perfusion through the skin, kidneys and brain ("three windows").
3. Explain why blood pressure alone is not enough.
4. Respond to early signs with assessment, monitoring, escalation and preparation.
5. Distinguish nursing actions from prescriber decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Shock can develop within minutes after surgery, bleeding, infection, arrhythmia or embolism. Frequent assessment at the bedside is the main way to detect it and to see whether treatment is working.

## 4. Basic physiology
Tissue oxygen delivery depends on blood flow (cardiac output), oxygen content (haemoglobin, oxygen saturation) and perfusion pressure. When delivery falls, the body compensates (faster heart rate, vasoconstriction) before blood pressure falls.

## 5. Relevant pathophysiology
The consensus describes four patterns: three low-flow (hypovolaemic from lost volume, cardiogenic from pump failure, obstructive from blockage such as tamponade or embolism) and one high-flow (distributive, for example sepsis or anaphylaxis). Patterns can combine. Cellular oxygen shortage leads to raised lactate.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Skin window:** cold, clammy, pale, blue or mottled skin; slow capillary refill; temperature difference between centre and toes.
- **Kidney window:** falling urine output (the consensus cites below 0.5 mL/kg/h as a marker of reduced perfusion).
- **Brain window:** new confusion, drowsiness or restlessness.
- **Circulation:** heart rate, blood pressure and trend, mean arterial pressure (MAP), rhythm, jugular and limb swelling, bleeding.
- **Respiratory:** rate, work of breathing, oxygen saturation.
- **Cause clues:** bleeding or drain output, fever and infection source, chest pain, recent procedure, allergen exposure, trauma, medicines, arrhythmia, chest or neck signs.
- **Tests (when ordered):** lactate, blood gases, haemoglobin, cultures.
- **Trend** matters more than a single value.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Take a full set of observations and examine the three windows at the frequency the risk requires; use continuous monitoring in ICU.
- Do not rely on a single variable, and do not wait for hypotension.
- Call for help early; use SBAR; repeat observations after any intervention.
- Prepare for likely orders: IV access, blood tests including lactate, monitoring, fluid or blood products, vasopressor setup, ECG, imaging, arterial or central line insertion (the consensus recommends arterial and central venous catheters when shock does not respond to initial therapy or needs vasopressor infusion).
- Support the team in treating the cause (for example, controlling bleeding with direct pressure when appropriate and per policy).
- Record observations, times, interventions and responses.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
Heart rate and rhythm, blood pressure and MAP (the consensus suggests initially targeting MAP of at least 65 mmHg and individualising the target; the target is set by the prescriber), skin findings, capillary refill, urine output, mental state, temperature, SpO₂, lactate trend where ordered, drain and wound losses, intake and output.

**Newer septic-shock statements (SSC 2021; SSC 2026 recommendation-level text from the official SCCM page, full publication not read; septic shock only):** capillary refill time may be used as an adjunct to guide resuscitation (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence); for adults with septic shock on vasopressors, an initial MAP target of 65 mmHg is recommended over higher targets (strong, moderate-quality evidence). SSC 2026 keeps the MAP 65 mmHg recommendation (strong, moderate) and adds a suggestion of a MAP range of 60–65 mmHg for adults aged 65 or older (conditional, low); it also suggests serial lactate and capillary refill time as adjuncts to guide resuscitation (conditional, low). These apply to **septic** shock and do not replace the individualised target set by the prescriber for other shock types.

**2025 update relevant to shock monitoring (verified against the 2025 guideline abstract and 2014/2025 comparison table; full 2025 text NOT read):** The 2025 ESICM guidance supersedes the older 2014 consensus for current shock diagnosis and monitoring. It emphasizes serial capillary-refill assessment with skin temperature and mottling as complements; assessment of fluid responsiveness before additional fluid in persistent shock, using dynamic rather than static markers where applicable; and echocardiography as first-line imaging to characterize shock type. It distinguishes arterial-pressure targets by shock mechanism and patient factors (including septic shock, traumatic hemorrhage with or without brain injury, and cardiogenic shock). **The 2014 generic initial MAP statement in the historical table is not a universal 2025 target.** The treating team selects the patient-specific target and intervention. Separately, SSC 2026 applies to **sepsis/septic shock only**; its official recommendation page has been read at recommendation level, not its full publication. Review the complete 2025 shock recommendations before clinical approval.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
Cold, mottled or blue skin; capillary refill prolonged; new confusion or drowsiness; urine output falling; heart rate rising or new arrhythmia; blood pressure falling or MAP below the ordered target; rising lactate; shortness of breath; chest pain; unexplained bleeding or swelling; no response to first treatments.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Frequent observations, perfusion checks, trend recording, early escalation, comfort and oxygen delivery per protocol | Independent nursing |
| Starting IV access, collecting blood samples, ECG, applying monitors | Collaborative (per protocol or order) |
| Giving fluids, blood products or vasopressors | Needs order or protocol (nursing administers and monitors) |
| Diagnosing the type of shock, choosing therapy, BP targets, inserting lines | Prescriber decision |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Escalate immediately for: any suspected shock; MAP below the ordered target; new mottling or confusion; urine output falling; lactate rising; no improvement after treatment; any arrhythmia with instability.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, systolic 82, heart rate 124, cold mottled legs, confused. **B:** day 1 after emergency laparotomy, drain output high, urine output 15 mL over the last hour. **A:** I think he is in shock, possibly bleeding or sepsis. **R:** please come now; I am preparing IV access, bloods including lactate and blood products if you order them.

## 13. Potential complications
Multi-organ failure (kidney, liver, brain, gut), arrhythmia, cardiac arrest, ischaemic limbs, fluid overload from over-treatment, pressure injury, delirium.

## 14. Documentation
Time of recognition; the three-window findings; observations and MAP trends; calls and response times; orders given; treatments started and responses; urine output; samples sent and results.

## 15. Practical bedside example
The patient's heart rate has risen over two hours although the blood pressure is still within range. You check capillary refill (slow), skin temperature (cool legs), urine output (falling) and mental state (restless). You repeat the observations, call for review with SBAR, and prepare bloods and lactate while waiting.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 47-year-old man, six hours after a bowel resection, has a heart rate of 120, systolic pressure 98, cold hands and falling urine output; his drain is filling with blood. The nurse sees a possible haemorrhage, calls the surgical and ICU teams, prepares IV access and blood tests, records the drain volume and urine output, and continues observations every 5 to 10 minutes. The team decides on fluids, blood and the next steps.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A patient 6 hours after surgery has a heart rate rising from 90 to 120, cool mottled legs, falling urine output and new restlessness, but a systolic pressure of 98 mmHg. What is the best interpretation and action?
A. The blood pressure is acceptable, so repeat the observations in one hour and continue the routine care already planned for the day.
B. The restlessness is probably pain, so give analgesia and review again once the patient has settled.
C. These are possible signs of shock despite a normal-range pressure; repeat observations, look for bleeding and escalate urgently.
D. Only the doctor can judge perfusion, so record the findings and wait for the next scheduled round.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** The consensus says low blood pressure is not required to define shock; skin, kidney and brain signs indicate poor perfusion.
**Why not A:** Waiting an hour ignores clear perfusion signs.
**Why not B:** Pain may contribute, but analgesia alone would delay recognition of shock.
**Why not D:** Perfusion assessment and escalation are nursing responsibilities.

**Q2 (understanding).** Which three body "windows" does the consensus identify as readily accessible for assessing tissue perfusion?
A. The skin, the kidneys (urine output) and the brain (mental state).
B. The lungs, the liver and the gut, because these organs show the earliest and most reliable changes at the bedside.
C. The heart, the eyes and the bones, because these structures can be examined without any equipment.
D. The blood pressure cuff, the pulse oximeter and the ECG, because monitors give the most objective information.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The consensus names the skin, kidneys and brain as windows because perfusion can be judged at the bedside from skin findings, urine output and mental state without special equipment; other organs are less accessible.
**Why not B:** These organs are less accessible at the bedside.
**Why not C:** Not part of the consensus statement.
**Why not D:** These are devices, not organ windows.

**Q3 (prioritization).** Which patient needs immediate escalation?
A. A patient with a MAP just above the ordered target, warm skin, normal urine output and a clear mind.
B. A patient with a stable low-normal pressure who has had these values for two days and is improving.
C. A patient who is awaiting discharge with normal observations and a stable lactate.
D. A patient with cool mottled skin, new confusion and urine output that has fallen over two hours.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Cool mottled skin, new confusion and falling urine output show poor perfusion in all three bedside windows at once, which suggests shock and needs urgent escalation. The other patients are stable.
**Why not A:** Stable with good perfusion.
**Why not B:** A stable and improving patient does not need the most urgent response of the group.
**Why not C:** Stable with normal findings.

**Q4 (scope).** The doctor orders a fluid bolus and then a vasopressor for suspected shock. What is the nurse's role?
A. Choose the vasopressor and rate according to the nurse's judgment of the patient's condition.
B. Prepare, give and monitor the ordered treatments, repeat observations and report the response.
C. Give the bolus but skip the reassessment afterwards, because the doctor will do it at the next review.
D. Delay giving the treatments until the lactate result is known, so that the team can see the trend first.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The nurse administers and monitors ordered therapy and reports response; the consensus recommends frequent reassessment.
**Why not A:** Selecting vasopressors and rates is a prescriber decision.
**Why not C:** Reassessment is shared and needed after each intervention.
**Why not D:** Delay may harm; early treatment is recommended.

**Q5 (understanding).** Which statement about lactate in suspected shock is most accurate based on the consensus?
A. A single normal lactate rules out shock, so no further assessment is needed after that one result is back.
B. Measure it when shock is suspected; it usually exceeds 2 mmol/L; repeated values help monitor response.
C. Lactate matters in patients with sepsis, so it is not needed when shock has another cause such as bleeding.
D. Lactate should be measured once at admission and not repeated, because later values do not change management.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The consensus advises measuring lactate when shock is suspected, notes that it is usually raised, and recommends repeated values to follow the response. It also stresses that no single variable proves or excludes shock.
**Why not A:** No single variable should be used to diagnose or exclude shock.
**Why not C:** The consensus applies to all shock types.
**Why not D:** Serial measurements are recommended to guide and monitor.

**Q6 (safety).** The patient's MAP drops below the ordered target but the monitor alarm was silenced earlier. What should the nurse do?
A. Check the patient, confirm the reading (transducer or cuff), call for review with SBAR, and keep monitoring.
B. Increase the vasopressor rate to bring the MAP up at once, because a prompt response matters most for the patient.
C. Re-silence the alarm until the next routine check, since the monitor had already alarmed once.
D. Document the reading carefully and review it after the shift, when the day has become less busy.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** The nurse verifies, escalates and monitors; titration follows orders.
**Why not B:** Changing rates without an order or titration protocol is outside scope.
**Why not C:** Silencing the alarm without action risks harm.
**Why not D:** Delay may allow deterioration.
