# Adult oxygen therapy in acute and critical care – the nurse's role
> DRAFT – NOT clinically reviewed. AI-generated. Evidence behind the AARC recommendations is mostly low-certainty (evidence level C).

## 1. Overview
Supplemental oxygen is used in most acutely ill patients, but both too little and too much can harm. The AARC 2022 guideline supports aiming for a defined SpO₂ range, early use of high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) in selected patients, and humidification at higher flows for comfort. The nurse monitors oxygenation and the whole patient, adjusts the delivery only within a prescription or protocol, and escalates deterioration.

## 2. Learning objectives
1. State the SpO₂ ranges supported by AARC 2022 for most patients, patients with COPD and critically ill adults, and the certainty of the evidence.
2. Assess adequacy of oxygenation using more than the SpO₂ number.
3. Describe what high-flow nasal oxygen is for, and the limits of the evidence.
4. Recognise oxygen-related hazards: hypoxaemia, hyperoxaemia, device problems, fire.
5. Escalate promptly and document; separate nursing actions from prescriber decisions.

## 3. Why this matters in ICU nursing
Oxygen delivery is adjusted many times a shift. Nurses are usually first to notice a falling saturation, rising work of breathing or an unsuitable device, and they help decide when to escalate to high-flow or ventilatory support.

## 6. Clinical assessment
- **Oxygenation:** SpO₂ and trend with a reliable trace; arterial blood gas results when available; FiO₂ or flow in use.
- **Work of breathing and ventilation:** respiratory rate, effort, accessory muscle use, ability to speak, mental state, fatigue, and (in patients at risk of retaining carbon dioxide) drowsiness or headache.
- **Circulation and perfusion:** heart rate, blood pressure, capillary refill, skin colour; low perfusion or cold peripheries can make SpO₂ unreliable.
- **Probe reliability:** NICE CG103 notes that pulse oximeters can under- or overestimate saturation, especially when the saturation is borderline, and that overestimation has been reported in people with dark skin; check the trace and correlate with the patient and, when ordered, a blood gas.
- **Equipment:** device and flow set correctly, tubing connected, humidifier and water level where used, no kinks, oxygen supply adequate.
- **Skin and comfort:** pressure from masks, cannulae and straps (see below), nasal and mouth dryness.

## 7. Nursing responsibilities
- Know the patient's **ordered target range**; if none is documented, ask the prescriber (the AARC guideline does not describe who sets targets; many organisations treat oxygen as a prescribed medicine – check local policy).
- Titrate within the order or protocol, and document each change with the reason.
- Observe and record SpO₂, respiratory rate and the patient's overall condition at the frequency set by the plan; increase the frequency when oxygen requirement rises.
- Escalate a rising requirement or falling saturation early rather than only turning the oxygen up.
- Check devices, connections and humidification each shift and after moves.
- Protect skin under masks, cannulae and straps; AARC 2024 strongly recommends assessing the skin around artificial airways and NIV interfaces and suggests assessing dressings.
- Keep oxygen equipment away from ignition sources; for defibrillation, follow the oxygen safety steps in D02-T15.

## 8. Monitoring parameters
SpO₂ with trend and probe check; respiratory rate and effort; mental state; heart rate and blood pressure; FiO₂ or flow and device; ABG results where taken; skin condition at contact points; patient comfort (dry mouth, nasal dryness); alarms set per policy.

## 9. Important findings – what AARC 2022 says (summarised)
| Question | Recommendation (strength as stated) | Nursing relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Target in most hospitalised adults needing oxygen | Committee **supports** an SpO₂ range of 94–98% (evidence level C) | Check the ordered range; do not treat 100% as safe or better |
| Adults with COPD needing oxygen | Committee supports 88–92% (evidence level C). RCP NEWS2 (2017, full text read) explains the rationale: patients with hypercapnic respiratory failure (PaCO₂ above 6 kPa, most often COPD, also obesity-related, chest-wall and neuromuscular causes) are at risk of rapidly worsening hypercapnia if too much oxygen is given, and recommends a prescribed target of 88–92% scored on a dedicated chart scale (Scale 2), chosen by a competent clinical decision-maker and recorded in the notes | Different range for patients with or at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure; ask if unsure; NEWS2 scoring is covered in D01-T19 |
| Critically ill adults | Recommends 94–98% (evidence level C) | Applies to critical care; individual exceptions are the prescriber's decision |
| Postoperative continuous pulse-oximetry monitoring | **No recommendation** (paucity of literature) | Monitoring intensity is local protocol |
| Early vs late HFNC | Limited evidence supports early initiation based on clinical condition (level C) | Escalate before exhaustion rather than after |
| HFNC vs conventional oxygen | HFNC may avoid escalation to NIV and invasive ventilation, via effects on oxygenation and breathlessness, but does not shorten length of stay (level B) | Know the patient's escalation plan |
| HFNC after extubation | Appears to reduce reintubation compared with conventional oxygen (level B) | Closer observation after extubation |
| Immunocompromised patients | No benefit in length of stay, escalation or morbidity for HFNC over conventional oxygen (level B); either may be used | Individual decision |
| Sepsis with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure (population-specific) | SSC 2026 (conditional, low certainty; official recommendation page, recommendation-level only) suggests titrating FiO₂ to either a more liberal or a more conservative oxygen target depending on patient factors and resources; most trials used a lower SpO₂ target of about 90–93% and a higher target of 96% or more, and the panel's own practice ranged from about 90% to 96%. This differs from the AARC 94–98% range for general acute-care patients; neither is universal | Targets are prescribed for the individual patient |
| Humidification | Consider for flows above 4 L/min to improve comfort (level C) | Humidification is for comfort; evidence for outcomes is lacking |

Evidence limitations stated in the guideline: low-certainty evidence, a modified RAND/UCLA appropriateness method (expert panel ratings, not GRADE), and heterogeneous studies.

**ATS 2026 update — distinguish oxygenation from ventilation.** For adults with acute hypoxaemic failure, ATS strongly supports HFNC; NIV or CPAP is a conditional option. In acute hypercapnic failure, NIV has stronger support; HFNC is a conditional alternative only for selected less severe cases with mild acidaemia and immediate NIV escalation available. A satisfactory SpO₂ does not exclude inadequate ventilation. Report increasing respiratory effort, fatigue, altered consciousness or worsening ordered blood gas results promptly; do not let a reassuring saturation delay urgent assessment. Modality selection and intubation decisions remain with the trained team. This is an abstract-level update, not an independent device prescription.

## 10. Warning signs and red flags
SpO₂ below the ordered range despite increased oxygen; rising respiratory rate or accessory-muscle use; new confusion, drowsiness or agitation; exhaustion; chest pain; low blood pressure or poor perfusion; falling SpO₂ after extubation or when turned; oxygen supply failure; device dislodged; very high SpO₂ in a patient with COPD or at risk of retaining carbon dioxide; smell of burning or sparking near oxygen.

## 11. Nursing interventions within scope
| Action | Scope label |
|---|---|
| Continuous assessment, probe checks, positioning to ease breathing, device checks, skin care, reporting and escalation | Independent nursing |
| Titrating oxygen within the written target range or protocol | Collaborative (per order or protocol) |
| Setting the target range, changing it, starting or stopping oxygen outside a protocol | Needs order or protocol; prescriber decision |
| Starting HFNC, NIV or invasive ventilation; deciding between devices | Prescriber / respiratory-therapy decision |
| Arterial blood gas sampling | Needs order or protocol (competence required) |

## 12. Escalation criteria
Activate emergency response for threatened airway, inadequate ventilation, severe hypoxaemia, exhaustion, new reduced consciousness or circulatory compromise. Provide emergency oxygenation and ventilatory support within the authorised pathway; a lower prescribed saturation target for hypercapnia risk must not delay rescue from life-threatening hypoxaemia. Escalate increasing oxygen requirements, deterioration on high-flow support or after extubation, or equipment failure. Interpret the oximeter reading alongside signal quality, perfusion, work of breathing and consciousness; a satisfactory saturation does not exclude hypoventilation or hypercapnia. Obtain blood gas assessment as indicated by the treating team.
SBAR — **S:** bed X, SpO₂ 89% on [device/flow], target 94–98%. **B:** day 2 pneumonia, respiratory rate rising to 28, increasing oxygen over 3 hours. **A:** worsening hypoxaemia and work of breathing; I am worried about fatigue. **R:** please review now; consider escalation of support; I am staying with the patient and will repeat observations every [interval].

## 13. Potential complications
Hypoxaemia and organ injury; hyperoxaemia; carbon dioxide retention in at-risk patients; dry or irritated mucosa; pressure injury from devices; fire risk; delayed recognition of deterioration when relying on SpO₂ alone.

## 14. Documentation
Ordered target; SpO₂ trend, device and flow or FiO₂ at each change; respiratory rate and work of breathing; reason for changes; escalation (time, person, response); humidification and skin checks; equipment problems.

## 15. Practical bedside example
At 02:00 a patient on a simple mask has an SpO₂ of 91% against a target of 94–98%. You check the trace and finger perfusion, look at the respiratory rate and effort, make sure the mask fits and the oxygen is connected, titrate within the written protocol, and call with SBAR because the requirement has risen over the shift. You document each step.


**Distinguish a poor signal from a rising oxygen requirement.** Compare the pulse shown by the oximeter with the measured pulse, then check the probe and oxygen delivery while assessing the patient. Report the current saturation with the device and flow, earlier settings, respiratory effort and alertness. A saturation back in target on more oxygen is still a change worth reporting.

## 16. Clinical case (fictional)
A 72-year-old man with COPD is admitted with breathlessness; his written target is 88–92%. His SpO₂ reads 97% after the oxygen was turned up by a night nurse who thought "higher is safer". He is becoming drowsy. The nurse reports it, and the team checks a blood gas and changes the plan. The nurse documents the reading, the oxygen settings, the change in alertness and the call.


**Why drowsiness changes the urgency.** Pulse oximetry estimates oxygen saturation. A blood gas can assess carbon dioxide and pH when ventilatory failure is suspected. Report the prescribed target, actual saturation, oxygen device and flow, and the timing of the change in alertness so the team can assess both oxygenation and ventilation.

## 17. MCQs
**Q1 (judgment).** A patient with COPD has a written SpO₂ target of 88–92%. After oxygen is turned up by a colleague, the reading is 98% and the patient is becoming drowsy. What is the best nursing action?
A. Leave the oxygen as it is, because a higher SpO₂ is safer for a patient who is short of breath and already unwell.
B. Turn the oxygen off and recheck the reading in thirty minutes, to see whether the patient wakes up.
C. Assess ABC and escalate urgently; titrate toward the prescribed target within protocol.
D. Wait for the next scheduled blood gas before taking any action, since drowsiness could simply reflect tiredness at night.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** New drowsiness is an emergency warning even with high SpO₂. Assess airway, ventilation and circulation and call urgently; request blood-gas assessment and titrate oxygen under the authorized target plan. Hypercapnia is possible, not established by oximetry; do not withdraw necessary oxygen abruptly.
**Why not A:** AARC supports a lower range for COPD; higher is not automatically safer.
**Why not B:** Stopping oxygen is not within the order and may cause hypoxaemia.
**Why not D:** Drowsiness with a high reading needs prompt assessment.

**Q2 (understanding).** Which SpO₂ range does AARC 2022 support for most hospitalised adults needing supplemental oxygen, and how strong is the evidence?
A. 94–98%, supported by the committee on low-certainty evidence (level C).
B. 100%, because maximum saturation avoids hypoxia and is recommended.
C. 88–92% in the general critically ill population, because lower saturations are considered safer.
D. 90–94% for critically ill patients, supported by strong high-certainty evidence.
**Answer: A.**
**Why correct:** AARC 2022 gives 94–98% for most patients (evidence level C); this question asks about that specific guideline, not a universal ICU prescription. UK-ROX 2025 randomized 16,500 ventilated adults and found no significant 90-day survival benefit from a conservative 88–92% target versus usual care. The trial does not establish one target for every patient; use the prescribed indication-specific range.
**Why not B:** The guideline raises concern about excessive FiO₂.
**Why not C:** 88–92% applies to patients with COPD.
**Why not D:** The critically ill range is also 94–98%, and certainty is low.

**Q3 (monitoring).** A patient's SpO₂ trace reads 99% but the patient looks pale, cold, sweaty and breathless. What is the best interpretation?
A. The reading is reassuring, so continue observations hourly as planned and let the patient rest without further checks.
B. The patient is anxious and probably only needs reassurance and a calmer room before any further assessment is done.
C. The oxygen should be reduced immediately because 99% is above the ordered range, whatever the patient looks like.
D. The patient may be deteriorating; check the probe and perfusion, assess breathing effort and escalate.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** Assess the patient first and escalate in parallel with checking the signal. FDA identifies poor perfusion, skin pigmentation and temperature among factors affecting accuracy. A saturation estimate does not establish adequate ventilation, oxygen delivery or tissue perfusion; request appropriate additional assessment when the reading conflicts with clinical signs.
**Why not A:** Routine monitoring would ignore danger signs.
**Why not B:** Attributing signs to anxiety risks missing deterioration.
**Why not C:** Reducing oxygen is not the priority when the patient is unwell.

**Q4 (understanding).** Which statement about high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) matches the AARC 2022 guideline?
A. HFNC shortens hospital stay compared with conventional oxygen therapy in adults with acute hypoxaemic failure.
B. The cited guideline supports HFNC to reduce escalation; a shorter stay was not established.
C. HFNC is recommended after intubation has failed, in preference to any other form of respiratory support.
D. HFNC has proven better outcomes than other support in all immunocompromised patients, whatever the cause of failure.
**Answer: B.**
**Why correct:** The guideline supports early HFNC in appropriate patients and reports potential avoidance of more invasive respiratory support, but did not establish reduced length of stay. Worsening work of breathing or gas exchange still requires urgent reassessment for escalation.
**Why not A:** The guideline states no length-of-stay reduction.
**Why not C:** The guideline supports early initiation in suitable patients.
**Why not D:** The cited evidence does not establish superior outcomes in all immunocompromised populations.

**Q5 (safety).** A patient is about to be defibrillated while on a mask with oxygen. Which action fits the ERC 2025 oxygen safety advice?
A. Leave the mask on to maintain oxygenation until after the shock, since the patient may become hypoxic if it is removed.
B. Direct the oxygen flow toward the chest so that the patient stays well oxygenated during the shock delivery.
C. Switch off the monitor to prevent interference with the shock, and then leave the oxygen running as before.
D. Remove the mask, keep it and the oxygen at least 1 m from the chest, and leave ventilator tubing connected.
**Answer: D.**
**Why correct:** ERC advises removing masks or cannulae and placing them at least 1 m away, with ventilation circuits left connected and exhausts directed away.
**Why not A:** An oxygen-enriched atmosphere raises fire risk.
**Why not B:** Oxygen should be directed away from the chest.
**Why not C:** Switching off monitoring is not advised and is not a fire precaution.

**Q6 (prioritization).** At the start of an ICU shift, four patients receiving or recently weaned from respiratory support need assessment. Which patient requires immediate evaluation for possible respiratory failure?
A. A patient on a stable HFNC with an SpO₂ of 96% who is comfortable and eating.
B. A patient with a mask-skin mark who is otherwise stable and asks for a softer strap or a different mask size.
C. A patient just extubated, now on HFNC, with a respiratory rate rising to 32 and SpO₂ falling.
D. A patient on room air with an SpO₂ of 97% awaiting a scan.
**Answer: C.**
**Why correct:** A rising respiratory rate with falling oxygen saturation after extubation suggests possible failure of respiratory support. Assess airway patency, breathing effort and circulation immediately, call for urgent help and follow the emergency support pathway. HFNC use does not exclude the need for escalation or further ventilatory support.
**Why not A:** This patient is stable and comfortable on HFNC.
**Why not B:** A device-related skin mark needs prompt skin assessment and safe pressure relief; the vignette does not describe the acute respiratory deterioration seen in option C.
**Why not D:** This patient is stable on room air.
